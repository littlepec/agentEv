# CRISPR-Cas9 Gene Editing: Mechanism of Action and Historical Development

## Introduction

CRISPR-Cas9 is a molecular system that can be directed to cut DNA at chosen locations, functioning as what its discoverers' institution described as "programmable DNA scissors" ([Berkeley Lab News Center, 2012](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2012/06/28/programmable-dna-scissors/)). The defining characteristic of the system, as recorded in the sources consulted for this report, is that it "could be programmed with RNA to edit genomic DNA" ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)). This RNA-guided programmability is the property that distinguishes CRISPR-Cas9 from earlier generations of sequence-specific nucleases and that underpins its rapid adoption as a laboratory and biomedical research tool.

The system was not invented from first principles but was adapted from a naturally occurring bacterial defense mechanism. The 2012 announcement of the key publication was explicitly framed in these terms, presenting the finding as "Programmable DNA Scissors Found for Bacterial Immune System" ([Berkeley Lab News Center, 2012](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2012/06/28/programmable-dna-scissors/)). The scientific community's recognition of the work culminated in October 2020, when Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna were awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry "for their work in this field" ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)).

This report provides an overview of CRISPR-Cas9 in two parts. The first traces the historical development of the system from its 2012 publication through patenting and Nobel recognition. The second describes the molecular mechanism, focusing on the Cas9 protein, the guide RNA, and the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) requirement. A final section assesses the significance of the findings and the limitations of the available source material.

## Historical Development

### The 2012 Seminal Publication

The foundational description of the programmable CRISPR-Cas9 system appeared in the paper by Jinek, Chylinski, Fonfara, Hauer, Doudna, and Charpentier, published in *Science*, volume 337, pages 816–821, on 28 June 2012 ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)). The same date carries the institutional announcement from Berkeley Lab, which described the discovery as yielding programmable DNA scissors derived from a bacterial immune system ([Berkeley Lab News Center, 2012](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2012/06/28/programmable-dna-scissors/)).

The central technical claim associated with this work, as summarized in the consulted sources, is that the CRISPR-Cas9 system "could be programmed with RNA to edit genomic DNA" ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)). This framing emphasizes two elements: the programmable nature of the system, and the role of RNA as the molecule that specifies the target. The research team was led by professor Jennifer Doudna and professor Emmanuelle Charpentier ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)).

### Patent Filing

In 2012, the research team led by Doudna and Charpentier "was the first group to file a patent application for the CRISPR-Cas9 system" ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)). The timing of the patent filing—in the same year as the seminal publication—indicates that the inventors and their institutions recognized the practical and commercial significance of the RNA-programmable editing capability at the point of disclosure rather than retrospectively.

### Nobel Prize Recognition

In October 2020, researchers Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna were awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for their work in this field ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)). The interval of approximately eight years between the 2012 publication and the 2020 prize is notable; Nobel recognition in the sciences frequently follows discovery by considerably longer periods, and the comparatively short interval in this case reflects the rapid and widespread impact of the technology on biological research.

## How CRISPR-Cas9 Works

### The Cas9 Nuclease

Cas9 is the protein component of the system responsible for cleaving DNA. The consulted sources describe Cas9 as being directed to its target by a guide RNA ([Wikipedia, 2026b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cas9)). It is the nuclease activity of Cas9, guided by RNA, that produces the double-strand break in the target DNA—the event that the Berkeley Lab framing captures in the metaphor of "programmable DNA scissors" ([Berkeley Lab News Center, 2012](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2012/06/28/programmable-dna-scissors/)).

### The Guide RNA and Its Two Components

A distinctive feature of the natural CRISPR-Cas9 system, as described in the consulted sources, is the composition of the guide RNA. Cas9 is directed by "a guide RNA composed of two distinct RNA molecules: CRISPR RNA (crRNA) and trans-activating crRNA (tracrRNA)" ([Wikipedia, 2026b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cas9)). The division of labor between these two RNA species is significant: the crRNA provides the sequence information that specifies the target, while the tracrRNA is required for the guide to function with Cas9.

This dual-RNA architecture is central to understanding both the natural biology of the system and the manner in which it was adapted. Because the targeting information resides in an RNA molecule, the specificity of the system is, in principle, reprogrammable by changing the RNA sequence rather than by engineering the protein—the essential insight captured in the statement that the system "could be programmed with RNA to edit genomic DNA" ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)).

### The Protospacer Adjacent Motif (PAM)

A further constraint on Cas9 activity is the requirement for a short recognition sequence adjacent to the target site. According to the consulted sources, "DNA cleavage requires the presence of a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) located at the non-target strand" ([Wikipedia, 2026b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cas9)). Two features of this statement merit emphasis. First, the PAM is a strict requirement: in its absence, cleavage does not occur. Second, the PAM is located on the non-target strand, meaning that the two strands of the DNA duplex are not treated symmetrically by the enzyme.

The PAM requirement has practical consequences that follow directly from its definition. Because cleavage depends on the presence of a PAM adjacent to the target sequence, not every sequence in a genome is equally accessible for targeting; the availability of a suitable PAM is a precondition for cleavage ([Wikipedia, 2026b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cas9)).

### Summary of the Mechanism

Taken together, the consulted sources describe a system with three interacting components: the Cas9 nuclease, the guide RNA (comprising crRNA and tracrRNA), and the PAM on the non-target strand. Cas9 is directed by the guide RNA, cleavage requires the PAM, and the targeting specificity is supplied by RNA ([Wikipedia, 2026b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cas9)). Because the targeting information is encoded in RNA, the system is programmable ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)).

## Significance and Limitations of the Available Evidence

The significance of CRISPR-Cas9, as represented in the consulted sources, rests on a single functional property: programmability through RNA. The statement that the system "could be programmed with RNA to edit genomic DNA" ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)) is the pivot on which the broader importance of the work turns. A nuclease whose target specificity is set by an RNA molecule can, in principle, be redirected to new targets by altering that RNA—a property the Berkeley Lab headline communicates through the image of programmable scissors ([Berkeley Lab News Center, 2012](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2012/06/28/programmable-dna-scissors/)).

The historical record available in these sources is, however, narrow. It establishes the date and authorship of the seminal publication, the identity of the team that first filed a patent application, and the award of the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)). It also establishes the basic mechanistic elements of the system: the guide RNA's two components, the role of Cas9, and the PAM requirement ([Wikipedia, 2026b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cas9)).

What the sources do not provide is equally worth noting for an impartial assessment. They do not describe the detailed three-dimensional structure of the Cas9-guide RNA-DNA complex, the precise chemistry of the cleavage reaction, the mechanisms by which the system avoids or fails to avoid off-target cleavage, the breadth of organisms to which the system has been applied, or the clinical and agricultural applications that followed. Nor do they document the pre-2012 body of research on CRISPR loci in bacteria that preceded the 2012 breakthrough, beyond the general characterization of the system as originating in a bacterial immune system ([Berkeley Lab News Center, 2012](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2012/06/28/programmable-dna-scissors/)).

One further caveat concerns source type. Two of the three sources consulted are encyclopedic summaries retrieved on 14 September 2026 ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing); [Wikipedia, 2026b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cas9)), and one is a contemporaneous institutional news release dated 28 June 2012 ([Berkeley Lab News Center, 2012](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2012/06/28/programmable-dna-scissors/)). The news release is primary in the sense that it was issued by the institution of one of the authors on the day of publication, but as a press communication it is intended to announce rather than to analyze. The encyclopedic sources are secondary and, by their nature, summarize claims originally made elsewhere. None of the three is the primary research article itself, although the citation details of that article—*Science* 337:816–821, 28 June 2012—are recorded ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)).

## Conclusion

CRISPR-Cas9 is an RNA-programmable DNA-cleaving system derived from a bacterial immune mechanism ([Berkeley Lab News Center, 2012](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2012/06/28/programmable-dna-scissors/)). Its mechanism, as described in the consulted sources, involves a Cas9 nuclease directed by a guide RNA composed of two distinct molecules, crRNA and tracrRNA, with DNA cleavage contingent on the presence of a PAM on the non-target strand ([Wikipedia, 2026b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cas9)). The property that made the system consequential is that it "could be programmed with RNA to edit genomic DNA" ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)).

Its development followed a compressed timeline: publication in *Science* on 28 June 2012 ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)); the announcement of the finding by Berkeley Lab on the same date ([Berkeley Lab News Center, 2012](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2012/06/28/programmable-dna-scissors/)); the filing of the first patent application for the system by the Doudna–Charpentier team in 2012 ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)); and the award of the Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Charpentier and Doudna in October 2020 ([Wikipedia, 2026a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRISPR_gene_editing)). The eight-year span from publication to Nobel recognition reflects the rapidity with which the RNA-programmable cleavage mechanism was taken up. At the same time, the sources available for this report are limited in scope, and a complete account of the system's mechanism and subsequent applications would require reference to the primary research literature and to more specialized reviews.
