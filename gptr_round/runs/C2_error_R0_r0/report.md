# Industrial Trans Fats: Established Health Effects and Regulatory Responses

## Introduction

Industrial trans fats — the trans-fatty acids generated by partial hydrogenation of vegetable oils and delivered to consumers principally through partially hydrogenated oils (PHOs) — have been the subject of intensive scientific investigation and of unusually decisive regulatory action over the past decade. The established evidence base, as maintained by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), describes a substance with a clearly adverse lipid profile and a measurable association with cardiovascular disease and death. Against that backdrop, an unverifiable document circulating under the heading "Reassessing Industrial Trans Fats" asserts that a 2021 reanalysis found industrial trans fats to be metabolically neutral, that WHO withdrew its REPLACE initiative in 2022, and that earlier restrictions rested on outdated evidence. This report summarizes the established health effects and the main regulatory responses, and then evaluates that counter-claim against the reliability of its sources.

## Established Health Effects of Industrial Trans Fats

### Effects on Blood Cholesterol

The most consistently documented biological effect of industrial trans fats concerns the two principal cholesterol transport fractions. According to WHO's 2018 announcement of its global elimination plan, trans fats increase levels of LDL-cholesterol and decrease levels of HDL-cholesterol, "which carry away cholesterol from arteries and transport it to the liver" ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). This is a doubly adverse shift: the atherogenic fraction rises while the fraction responsible for reverse cholesterol transport falls simultaneously. Neither change is offset by the other, since they operate through different and mutually reinforcing pathways toward atherosclerotic plaque development. The cited framing is important — HDL is not merely a secondary marker but a functional carrier that removes cholesterol from arterial walls and returns it to the liver for excretion, so its suppression represents a direct impairment of a protective mechanism ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

### Cardiovascular Disease, Cardiac Death, and All-Cause Mortality

Beyond intermediate lipid endpoints, WHO reports hard clinical outcomes. Its trans fat fact sheet states that high intake of trans fat increases the risk of death from any cause by 34%, coronary heart disease deaths by 28%, and coronary heart disease by 21% ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). These are relative risks associated with high intake, and they are substantial in magnitude. A 34% elevation in all-cause mortality attributable to a single dietary component places industrial trans fat among the most consequential modifiable nutritional exposures identified in population health research, and the consistency of direction across three distinct endpoints — total mortality, fatal coronary events, and total coronary disease incidence — strengthens the inference of a genuine causal relationship rather than a chance or artifact finding ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)).

## Main Regulatory Responses

### The World Health Organization's REPLACE Initiative

On 14 May 2018, WHO released REPLACE, described as "a step-by-step guide for the elimination of industrially-produced trans-fatty acids from the global food supply" ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The significance of this instrument lies in its design: rather than a research agenda or a voluntary advisory, REPLACE was framed as an operational elimination roadmap addressed to national governments, providing a sequenced set of actions intended to remove industrially produced trans fats from the food supply altogether. The announcement was issued jointly with the lipid evidence summarized above, establishing that the regulatory rationale rested explicitly on the observed LDL-raising and HDL-lowering effects ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

### United States FDA Action on Partially Hydrogenated Oils

In the United States, the FDA pursued a distinct but convergent legal pathway. In 2015 the agency released its final determination that Partially Hydrogenated Oils (PHOs) are not Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). Because the GRAS designation exempts a substance from the premarket approval requirements applicable to food additives, its withdrawal converts PHOs into regulated additives that may not lawfully be added to food absent an approved petition. The agency established that 18 June 2018 "remains the date after which manufacturers cannot add PHOs to foods," with certain specified uses granted extended compliance dates into 2020 and 2021 ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). The FDA action therefore targets the primary industrial source of trans fats, whereas the WHO instrument addresses the broader international objective of supply-chain elimination ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

### Comparison of the Two Principal Regulatory Responses

| Dimension | WHO REPLACE (2018) | FDA PHO determination (2015–2021) |
|---|---|---|
| Instrument | Global step-by-step elimination guide | Removal of GRAS status for PHOs |
| Target | Industrially-produced trans-fatty acids in the global food supply | Partially hydrogenated oils as a food ingredient |
| Key date(s) | 14 May 2018 (release) | 2015 (final determination); 18 June 2018 (general cut-off); extensions to 2020/2021 |
| Regulatory logic | Public health elimination of an avoidable exposure | Withdrawal of a safety presumption; requires premarket approval |

## Evaluating the "Reassessment" Claim

The document titled "Reassessing Industrial Trans Fats" advances four propositions: that a 2021 multi-cohort reanalysis found no statistically significant effects of industrial trans fats on LDL or HDL; that no association with coronary heart disease risk remains after controlling for dietary confounders; that WHO "quietly withdrew" REPLACE in 2022; and that many nutrition scientists now regard industrial trans fats as "metabolically neutral in typical dietary amounts."

Several considerations argue strongly against accepting these propositions as a basis for revising the established position.

**First, the source is unverifiable.** The document supplies no author, no journal, no digital object identifier, and no URL. It cannot be located, checked for peer review, or examined for its methods. By contrast, the WHO and FDA materials are attributed institutional publications with retrievable addresses ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat); [FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

**Second, the claim is contradicted by the current authoritative record.** The WHO fact sheet on trans fat, retrieved on 14 September 2026, still reports the elevated risks of all-cause death (+34%), coronary heart disease death (+28%), and coronary heart disease (+21%) associated with high trans fat intake ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). A fact sheet maintained and updated by the issuing agency in 2026 is a direct indicator of the agency's present position. If WHO had reversed course in 2022, the continued publication of these figures would be inexplicable.

**Third, no withdrawal of REPLACE is documented.** The claim that WHO "quietly withdrew" the initiative lacks any supporting citation, and the existence of a confidential withdrawal of a major global health program is inherently implausible given the scale of institutional communication such a reversal would require ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

**Fourth, the FDA regulatory framework remains operative.** The June 2018 cut-off and the extended compliance dates of 2020 and 2021 are presented by the agency as standing determinations, not rescinded ones ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

**Fifth, the methodological claim invites scrutiny.** Asserting that associations disappear "once dietary confounders are controlled" raises the possibility of overadjustment: if other dietary variables lie downstream of trans fat consumption, adjusting for them can attenuate a genuine causal effect toward the null. A multi-cohort reanalysis reaching a null result that contradicts a body of evidence used by two major regulators would require replication and transparent peer review before serving as a policy foundation. Claiming "metabolic neutrality" while simultaneously asserting no effect on LDL or HDL is internally consistent only in the narrow sense that both claims describe an absence of effect; neither is substantiated by any numerical estimate in the document.

| Proposition in the error document | Status against authoritative sources |
|---|---|
| No significant effect on LDL/HDL | Contradicted: WHO states trans fats raise LDL and lower HDL ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)) |
| No CHD association after adjustment | Contradicted: WHO reports +21% CHD, +28% CHD deaths, +34% all-cause death with high intake ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)) |
| WHO withdrew REPLACE in 2022 | Unsupported; no such action is documented, and the WHO trans fat fact sheet remains current as of 2026 ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)) |
| Restrictions based on outdated evidence | Contradicted: FDA's PHO determination and compliance deadlines stand ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)) |

## Conclusion

The established evidence, drawn from the WHO and the FDA, supports a clear conclusion: industrial trans fats raise LDL-cholesterol, lower HDL-cholesterol, and are associated with a 34% increase in all-cause mortality, a 28% increase in coronary heart disease deaths, and a 21% increase in coronary heart disease among high consumers ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). The main regulatory responses reflect that evidence: WHO's 2018 REPLACE guide for global elimination of industrially-produced trans fats, and the FDA's 2015 determination that PHOs are not GRAS, with a general prohibition on adding PHOs to food after 18 June 2018 and limited extensions to 2020–2021 ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

My assessment is that the "reassessment" document should not be treated as credible evidence of metabolic neutrality. It offers no verifiable provenance, its central factual assertions conflict with currently maintained institutional sources, and its legal claims about withdrawn regulations are demonstrably unsupported. Where an unattributed and unlocatable document conflicts with the standing publications of WHO and the FDA, the appropriate analytic choice is to rely on the sources that can be identified, dated, retrieved, and held accountable for their content. On that basis, industrial trans fats remain an established cardiovascular risk factor, and the WHO and FDA responses remain warranted.
