# Industrial Trans Fats: Established Health Effects and the Principal Regulatory Responses

## Introduction

Industrial trans fatty acids (TFAs), the unsaturated fatty acids formed during the partial hydrogenation of liquid vegetable oils, are among the most comprehensively regulated food components of the past two decades. The authoritative bodies that have addressed this question — the World Health Organization (WHO) and the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) — have both arrived at the same conclusion: industrially produced TFAs, delivered principally through partially hydrogenated oils (PHOs), worsen blood lipid profiles and increase the risk of coronary heart disease and death. This report summarizes those established health effects, documents the principal regulatory responses built upon them, and evaluates a contrary document that has circulated under the title "Reassessing Industrial Trans Fats," which asserts that industrial TFAs are metabolically neutral and that the WHO has abandoned its trans-fat elimination program ("Reassessing Industrial Trans Fats," n.d.).

Because the query concerns *established* effects and *actual* regulatory responses, the analysis below prioritizes the WHO and FDA materials, which are attributable to identifiable institutional authors, carry verifiable URLs, and are internally consistent, over the unattributed contrarian document that supplies neither author, journal, nor link ("Reassessing Industrial Trans Fats," n.d.).

## Background and Scope

WHO frames the issue explicitly in terms of industrially produced TFAs, distinguishing them from trans fats that occur naturally in small amounts in ruminant-derived foods, and identifies partially hydrogenated oils as the primary industrial source ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The FDA uses the same target, describing its regulatory action as the "final determination regarding partially hydrogenated oils (removing trans fat)" ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). The convergence on PHOs as the object of regulation is not incidental: it reflects the fact that PHOs were, for decades, the dominant vehicle for industrial TFA exposure in processed foods, and therefore the most tractable point of policy intervention ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

## Established Health Effects

### Effects on Blood Lipids

The most direct and mechanistically well-characterized effect of trans fats is on circulating cholesterol fractions. According to WHO, trans fats increase levels of LDL-cholesterol — the fraction that deposits cholesterol in arterial walls — and decrease levels of HDL-cholesterol, which "carry away cholesterol from arteries and transport it to the liver" ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The significance of this finding lies in its directionality: trans fats shift the lipid profile unfavorably along both axes simultaneously, an effect that is more adverse than that of saturated fatty acids, which raise LDL-cholesterol but do not lower HDL-cholesterol to the same degree ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

### Cardiovascular Disease and Mortality

The lipid findings are corroborated by hard clinical endpoints. WHO reports that high intake of trans fat increases the risk of death from any cause by 34%, coronary heart disease deaths by 28%, and coronary heart disease itself by 21% ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). These figures, current as of the fact sheet's retrieval on September 14, 2026, represent the organization's standing assessment and are summarized in Table 1.

**Table 1. Established health effects of high trans fat intake (WHO, 2026)**

| Outcome | Reported increase in risk |
|---|---|
| Death from any cause | 34% |
| Coronary heart disease deaths | 28% |
| Coronary heart disease (incident) | 21% |
| LDL-cholesterol | Increased |
| HDL-cholesterol | Decreased |

### Interpretation of the Evidence

Two qualifications follow from the source language. First, the risk estimates are attached to "high intake" of trans fat, indicating that the reported effect magnitudes describe populations with substantial exposure rather than a quantified dose-response function at low intakes ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). Second, the WHO fact sheet refers to trans fat generally, while the WHO's elimination program targets the industrially produced fraction specifically, reflecting the judgment that industrial TFAs are the modifiable portion of intake ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). Neither source specifies a threshold below which risk becomes negligible, which is itself a reason for precautionary regulation rather than for inaction.

## Principal Regulatory Responses

### The WHO REPLACE Initiative

On May 14, 2018, WHO released REPLACE, described as "a step-by-step guide for the elimination of industrially-produced trans-fatty acids from the global food supply" ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). REPLACE is explicitly framed as an operational tool rather than a mere advisory statement, providing a sequenced pathway — review, promote, legislate, assess, create, and enforce — for national governments to remove industrial TFAs from their food systems ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). Critically for the evaluation that follows, WHO's institutional position remained active and current as of September 14, 2026: the organization's trans fat fact sheet still presents the cardiovascular and mortality risk estimates described above ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)).

### The FDA Final Determination on Partially Hydrogenated Oils

In the United States, the FDA completed a scientific review culminating in its 2015 final determination that PHOs are not Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) for any use in human food ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). The determination established June 18, 2018 as "the date after which manufacturers cannot add PHOs to foods," with certain specialized uses granted extensions into 2020 and 2021, after which those allowances lapsed ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). Removal of GRAS status is a substantive regulatory act: it converts the addition of PHOs to food from a permitted practice into an unauthorized food additive use, as reflected in the FDA's own characterization of the action as "removing trans fat" from the food supply ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

**Table 2. Documented regulatory timeline**

| Year | Body | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 2015 | FDA | Final determination that PHOs are not GRAS ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)) |
| 2018 (May 14) | WHO | Release of the REPLACE elimination guide ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)) |
| 2018 (June 18) | FDA | Date after which manufacturers cannot add PHOs to foods ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)) |
| 2020–2021 | FDA | Expiration of extended PHO uses ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)) |
| 2026 (Sept 14) | WHO | Fact sheet still reporting increased all-cause and CHD mortality risk ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)) |

### National and Regional Regulators

The materials supplied here document WHO and FDA actions in specific operational detail. Claims about the actions of "several national regulators" beyond these two bodies appear only in the unattributed contrarian document and cannot be verified against any identifiable institutional source ("Reassessing Industrial Trans Fats," n.d.).

## Evaluation of the Contrarian Reassessment

The document titled "Reassessing Industrial Trans Fats" asserts four propositions: (a) a 2021 multi-cohort reanalysis found no statistically significant effect of industrial TFAs on LDL or HDL cholesterol and no association with coronary heart disease once dietary confounders are controlled; (b) the WHO "quietly withdrew" REPLACE in 2022; (c) national regulators have signaled that earlier restrictions rested on outdated evidence; and (d) many nutrition scientists now regard industrial TFAs as metabolically neutral at typical intakes ("Reassessing Industrial Trans Fats," n.d.). Each proposition fails at least one basic test of evidentiary reliability.

**Table 3. Assessment of the contrarian claims**

| Claim | Evidentiary problem |
|---|---|
| 2021 reanalysis finding no lipid or CHD effect | No author, journal, DOI, or URL is provided; the finding cannot be located or verified ("Reassessing Industrial Trans Fats," n.d.) |
| WHO withdrew REPLACE in 2022 | Contradicted by WHO's continued publication of trans fat risk estimates in 2026, four years after the alleged withdrawal ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)) |
| Regulators signaled restrictions were outdated | No regulator is named and no document is cited; the FDA page retrieved in 2026 still presents PHO removal as an accomplished regulatory conclusion ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)) |
| Scientific consensus favors metabolic neutrality | No consensus body, survey, or position statement is cited; WHO continues to attribute a 34% increase in all-cause mortality risk to high trans fat intake ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)) |

Three analytical points reinforce this assessment. First, the provenance failure is decisive: a reanalysis capable of overturning two decades of evidence would exist in the peer-reviewed literature with an identifiable authorship, dataset, and methods section. Its absence from the supplied materials is not a neutral gap but a substantive defect ("Reassessing Industrial Trans Fats," n.d.).

Second, the timing claim is internally inconsistent with the anchor sources. If the WHO had withdrawn REPLACE in 2022, one would expect the withdrawal to be reflected in an institution's own public communications. Instead, the WHO fact sheet retrieved on September 14, 2026 still reports that high trans fat intake increases all-cause mortality by 34%, CHD deaths by 28%, and CHD by 21% ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). The adverb "quietly" is itself a rhetorical device that renders the alleged withdrawal unfalsifiable.

Third, the "confounder control" argument misstates what statistical significance means. Demonstrating that an effect is not statistically significant after adjustment is not equivalent to demonstrating that no effect exists; it may simply reflect reduced statistical power, exposure misclassification, or over-adjustment for variables on the causal pathway between TFA intake and disease. The WHO's lipid mechanism — simultaneous LDL elevation and HDL reduction — is a direct, measured physiological effect that does not depend on observational confounding adjustment at all ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

## Conclusion

On the evidence available from the authoritative sources, the established position is unambiguous. Industrial trans fats, delivered primarily through partially hydrogenated oils, raise LDL-cholesterol and lower HDL-cholesterol ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)), and high intake is associated with a 34% increase in all-cause mortality, a 28% increase in coronary heart disease deaths, and a 21% increase in coronary heart disease ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). The regulatory responses follow directly from that evidence: WHO's REPLACE guide provides a step-by-step framework for eliminating industrially produced TFAs from national food supplies ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)), and the FDA's 2015 determination that PHOs are not GRAS, effective for most uses on June 18, 2018, removed the principal industrial source of TFAs from the U.S. food supply ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

The contrarian "reassessment" document should not be treated as a credible basis for revisiting those policies. It is anonymous, uncited, inconsistent with the WHO's own 2026 communications, and rests on an absence-of-significance argument that cannot bear the weight placed upon it ("Reassessing Industrial Trans Fats," n.d.). The sources reviewed do indicate genuine areas of uncertainty — notably the absence of a quantified dose-response threshold below which risk is negligible, and the fact that published risk estimates are framed for "high intake" ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). These gaps argue for continued monitoring and enforcement, not for deregulation. On the balance of reliable evidence, the elimination of industrially produced trans fats remains a well-justified public health objective.
