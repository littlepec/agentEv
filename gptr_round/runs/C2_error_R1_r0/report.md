# Industrial Trans Fatty Acids: Established Health Effects and Global Regulatory Responses

## Introduction

Industrial trans fatty acids (iTFAs) are unsaturated fatty acids containing at least one double bond in the *trans* configuration that are generated during the industrial partial hydrogenation of vegetable oils. They entered the food supply in quantity in the early twentieth century and became ubiquitous in margarines, shortenings, commercial frying oils, bakery products, and processed snacks because partial hydrogenation conferred desirable texture, stability, and shelf life ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra054035)). By the 1990s, epidemiological evidence began to indicate that these fats were uniquely harmful to cardiovascular health, and by the 2010s the World Health Organization (WHO) had identified their elimination as a global public health priority ([WHO, 2024](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). This report summarizes the established health effects of industrial trans fats, describes the principal regulatory instruments adopted in response, and assesses the evidence on their effectiveness.

## Chemical and Dietary Context

Partial hydrogenation converts liquid vegetable oils into semi-solid fats and, as a side reaction, isomerizes a proportion of naturally occurring *cis* double bonds into the *trans* configuration. The predominant isomer is elaidic acid (*trans*-9-C18:1), accompanied by numerous positional isomers ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra054035)). Naturally occurring *trans* fats from ruminant sources—dairy and meat from cattle, sheep, and goats—are structurally similar but are consumed at far lower levels, typically contributing less than 0.5% of total energy intake, compared with historically 2–4% of energy in some Western populations before reformulation ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra054035)).

WHO and the Food and Agriculture Organization have recommended that total *trans* fat intake remain below 1% of total energy intake, a threshold intended primarily to constrain iTFA consumption ([WHO, 2024](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). WHO estimates that *trans* fat intake is responsible for more than 500,000 deaths from cardiovascular disease annually worldwide ([WHO, 2024](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)).

## Established Health Effects

### Effects on Blood Lipids and Lipoproteins

The lipid effects of iTFAs are the best-characterized and most consistently replicated of their biological actions. When substituted isocalorically for *cis*-unsaturated fats, iTFAs raise low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), lower high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), increase triglycerides, and raise lipoprotein(a) ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra054035)). A quantitative meta-analysis of controlled feeding trials confirmed that industrial *trans* fats raise LDL-C and lower HDL-C relative to both saturated and *cis*-unsaturated fatty acids ([Brouwer et al., 2010](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0009434)).

The critical distinction from saturated fat is that iTFAs exert their adverse effect on the total-to-HDL cholesterol ratio at approximately double the potency of saturated fatty acids, because they simultaneously raise the atherogenic and lower the protective lipoprotein fraction ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra054035)). This has direct implications for policy: replacing iTFAs with saturated fat is not a neutral substitution, but replacing them with polyunsaturated or monounsaturated oils is clearly beneficial.

### Cardiovascular Disease

Prospective cohort evidence linking iTFA intake to coronary heart disease (CHD) is among the most consistent in nutritional epidemiology. Landmark analyses from the Nurses' Health Study and other cohorts found that higher *trans* fat intake was associated with substantially elevated CHD risk, independent of conventional risk factors ([Willett et al., 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/0140-6736(93)90350-P); [Hu et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199711203372102)). A pooled analysis of four large prospective cohorts concluded that each 2% increase in energy intake from *trans* fat was associated with a 23% increase in the incidence of CHD ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra054035)).

Evidence also links higher *trans* fat intake or higher tissue *trans* fat biomarkers to elevated risk of sudden cardiac death, an outcome less strongly related to LDL-C alone and suggestive of arrhythmic mechanisms ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra054035)). Accordingly, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) estimated that removing partially hydrogenated oils (PHOs) from the food supply could prevent approximately 20,000 myocardial infarctions and 7,000 deaths from heart disease each year in the United States ([FDA, 2015](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

### Inflammation, Endothelial Function, and Insulin Resistance

Beyond lipids, iTFAs promote systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. Higher *trans* fat intake has been associated with elevated plasma concentrations of C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, tumour necrosis factor-alpha receptors, and E-selectin, and with impaired flow-mediated brachial artery dilation ([Lopez-Garcia et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1093/jn/135.3.562)). These pathways plausibly mediate part of the excess cardiovascular risk not explained by lipid changes ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra054035)).

For type 2 diabetes, the evidence is less conclusive. A landmark cohort analysis found that *trans* fat intake was positively associated with incident type 2 diabetes in women ([Salmerón et al., 2001](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM200109133411101)), whereas a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies concluded that evidence for an association between *trans* fat intake and type 2 diabetes was insufficient to draw firm conclusions ([de Souza et al., 2015](https://www.bmj.com/content/351/bmj.h3978)). This remains one of the few areas of genuine scientific uncertainty in the field.

### Dose–Response, Substitution Effects, and the Rationale for Elimination

The dose–response relationship for iTFAs appears approximately linear with no identified safe threshold, meaning that even low population-level exposures carry measurable risk ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra054035)). Critically, modelling of substitution effects indicates that replacing iTFAs with polyunsaturated fats would reduce CHD risk substantially, providing a mechanistic justification for regulatory removal rather than mere reduction ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra054035)). Additional but less firmly established associations have been reported with ovulatory infertility, depressive symptoms, and cognitive decline; these are plausible but rest on weaker evidence than the cardiovascular findings.

## Regulatory Responses

### The International Framework

WHO launched the REPLACE action package in 2018, calling for the global elimination of industrially produced *trans* fat from the food supply. REPLACE comprises six strategic actions: **R**eview dietary sources, **P**romote replacement with healthier fats and oils, **L**egislate or enact regulatory actions, **A**ssess and monitor *trans* fat content in the food supply, **C**reate awareness among policymakers, producers, and the public, and **E**nforce compliance ([WHO, 2024](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)).

WHO defines a "best-practice" policy as one that is mandatory and either (a) limits industrially produced *trans* fat to 2 g per 100 g of total fat in all foods, or (b) bans the production or use of partially hydrogenated oils as an ingredient in all foods ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/teams/nutrition-and-food-safety/replace-trans-fat)). The original goal of global elimination by 2023 was not met. WHO's 2023 status report recorded 43 countries with best-practice policies covering approximately 2.8 billion people, leaving roughly 5 billion people unprotected, with 9 of the 16 countries carrying the highest estimated burden of *trans*-fat-related disease lacking any best-practice policy ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/teams/nutrition-and-food-safety/replace-trans-fat)). By the following reporting cycle, more than 50 countries had adopted best-practice measures ([WHO, 2024](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). International standard-setting discussions at the Codex Alimentarius Commission have also converged on the 2 g per 100 g total fat benchmark as a reference limit ([FAO & WHO, 2021](https://www.fao.org/fao-who-codexalimentarius/en/)).

### Mandatory Limits and Bans

Denmark was the first country to act decisively, introducing an ordinance in 2003 that limited *trans* fat from industrial sources to 2% of the fat or oil in a product, fully in force by 2004 ([Restrepo & Rieger, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2015.06.005)). The European Union followed with Commission Regulation (EU) 2019/649, which caps industrially produced *trans* fat at 2 g per 100 g of fat in foods intended for the final consumer, applicable from 2 April 2021 ([European Commission, 2019](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2019/649/oj)). In the United States, the FDA determined in 2015 that PHOs were no longer generally recognized as safe (GRAS) for any use in human food, with compliance required from 18 June 2018 and extended deadlines of 1 January 2020 and 1 January 2021 for certain uses ([FDA, 2015](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). Canada prohibited PHOs effective September 2018. Brazil's ANVISA mandated a 2 g per 100 g limit from January 2021 and a PHO ban from January 2023, and India's Food Safety and Standards Authority capped *trans* fat in fats and oils at 2% by weight, effective January 2022 ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/teams/nutrition-and-food-safety/replace-trans-fat)).

### Labeling-Based Approaches

The United States required declaration of *trans* fat on the Nutrition Facts panel from 1 January 2006, and similar labeling requirements exist in Canada, Brazil, and several other jurisdictions ([FDA, 2024](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/trans-fat)). Labeling has two well-documented limitations. First, in the United States a product may declare "0 g trans fat" if it contains less than 0.5 g per serving, allowing cumulative intake to rise with multiple servings ([FDA, 2024](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/trans-fat)). Second, labeling depends on consumer attention and literacy and does not constrain reformulation as directly as a mandatory limit; systematic reviews have accordingly found labeling less effective than mandatory compositional limits or bans ([Downs et al., 2013](https://www.who.int/bulletin/volumes/91/4/12-111112/en/)).

## Effectiveness of Regulatory Responses

Evidence on policy impact is strongest for mandatory national limits and bans. A quasi-experimental analysis of Denmark's regulation estimated a reduction in cardiovascular mortality of roughly 14 deaths per 100,000 persons per year attributable to the policy ([Restrepo & Rieger, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2015.06.005)). An evaluation of local *trans* fat restrictions in New York State found declines in hospital admissions for myocardial infarction and stroke in counties with restrictions compared with those without ([Brandt et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamacardio.2017.0091)). A systematic review of *trans* fat reduction interventions concluded that mandatory reformulation policies were the most effective and cost-effective approach, outperforming voluntary industry pledges, consumer education, and labeling alone ([Hyseni et al., 2017](https://www.who.int/bulletin/volumes/95/12/17-194738/en/)). An earlier systematic review reached broadly comparable conclusions ([Downs et al., 2013](https://www.who.int/bulletin/volumes/91/4/12-111112/en/)).

These findings are biologically coherent: because partial hydrogenation is an industrial process with readily available substitute oils and fats, and because iTFAs confer no nutritional benefit, removal is achievable without meaningful dietary loss. This distinguishes iTFA policy from interventions targeting nutrients that have physiological functions.

## Persistent Gaps and Challenges

Several obstacles limit global progress. First, the coverage gap is large: the majority of the world's population still lacks binding protection, and low- and middle-income countries often have the least capacity to monitor food composition and enforce limits ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/teams/nutrition-and-food-safety/replace-trans-fat)). Second, enforcement requires laboratory capacity and surveillance systems that are unevenly distributed. Third, labeling loopholes—particularly rounding rules permitting "0 g" declarations below defined thresholds—can undermine consumer-directed approaches ([FDA, 2024](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/trans-fat)). Fourth, the distinction between industrial and ruminant *trans* fats is not always captured in regulatory text, creating definitional and analytical complexities. Fifth, residual scientific uncertainty regarding type 2 diabetes, cancer, and cognitive outcomes means that the case for elimination rests primarily on the cardiovascular evidence, which is nonetheless strong and consistent ([de Souza et al., 2015](https://www.bmj.com/content/351/bmj.h3978)).

## Conclusion

The evidence that industrially produced *trans* fatty acids adversely affect cardiovascular health is robust, consistent across study designs, biologically plausible, and supported by dose–response and substitution analyses. Their principal established effects are elevation of LDL-C and lipoprotein(a), reduction of HDL-C, promotion of systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, and a well-quantified increase in coronary heart disease risk—approximately 23% per additional 2% of energy intake. Regulatory responses have evolved from disclosure-based labeling toward mandatory compositional limits and outright PHO bans, a shift justified by comparative effectiveness evidence showing mandatory reformulation to be the most successful and cost-effective strategy. As of the latest WHO reporting, more than 50 countries have adopted best-practice policies, but global elimination remains incomplete, and the principal remaining challenges are political commitment, enforcement capacity, and equitable protection for populations in low- and middle-income settings.
