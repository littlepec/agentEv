## Introduction and Definitions

Trans fatty acids (TFA) are unsaturated fatty acids containing at least one carbon–carbon double bond in the *trans* configuration, which straightens the acyl chain relative to the *cis* configuration found in most naturally occurring unsaturated fats (Mozaffarian et al., 2006). Two sources dominate human exposure. **Industrially produced TFA (iTFA)** arise principally from partial hydrogenation of vegetable oils, a process that converts liquid oils into semi-solid fats with improved oxidative stability and functional performance; smaller amounts arise from high-temperature refining (deodorisation) and repeated frying. **Ruminant TFA (rTFA)** are formed by bacterial biohydrogenation in the rumen of cattle, sheep, and goats and appear at low concentrations (typically a few percent of total fat) in milk, butter, cheese, beef, and lamb. The dominant iTFA isomer is elaidic acid (*trans*-18:1 n-9), whereas vaccenic acid (*trans*-11 18:1) predominates in ruminant fat.

This distinction matters because the health literature attributes substantially different risk profiles to the two categories, and because regulation has been directed almost exclusively at iTFA.

## Established Health Effects

### Cardiovascular disease and mortality

The causal relationship between iTFA intake and coronary heart disease (CHD) is the best-established and most quantitatively robust finding in the field. In a systematic review and meta-analysis of four prospective cohort studies, a 2% increase in energy intake from TFA was associated with a 23% increase in CHD incidence (pooled relative risk 1.23; 95% CI 1.11–1.37) (Mozaffarian et al., 2006). The Nurses' Health Study reported an approximately twofold elevation in CHD risk among women in the highest quintile of TFA intake compared with the lowest, after adjustment for established risk factors (Hu et al., 1997). Importantly, meta-analyses of cohort data have found that the association with CHD is stronger for iTFA than for rTFA, and that rTFA at habitual intakes is not consistently associated with elevated risk.

At the population level, the World Health Organization has estimated that TFA intake causes more than 278,000 deaths annually worldwide and has identified trans fat elimination as a priority intervention (WHO, 2018). The United States Food and Drug Administration estimated that removing partially hydrogenated oils (PHOs) from the food supply would prevent approximately 20,000 heart attacks and 7,000 deaths from heart disease each year in the United States (FDA, 2015).

### Blood lipid effects

The lipid mechanism is well characterised. Relative to an isoenergetic replacement with *cis*-monounsaturated or polyunsaturated fat, TFA raise low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), lower high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), increase lipoprotein(a), and raise triglycerides (Mensink & Katan, 1990; Mozaffarian et al., 2006). The Institute of Medicine concluded in its 2005 Dietary Reference Intakes report that the LDL-raising effect of TFA is at least as great as that of saturated fatty acids, that the effect on the LDL-C/HDL-C ratio is more adverse than that of saturated fat, and that there is no established threshold below which adverse effects disappear (Institute of Medicine, 2005). Consequently, the Institute recommended that TFA consumption be kept "as low as possible in the diet," a position subsequently echoed in the *Dietary Guidelines for Americans*.

### Inflammation, endothelial function, and metabolic outcomes

Beyond lipids, TFA intake has been associated with elevated circulating markers of systemic inflammation, including C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, and tumour necrosis factor-alpha, and with impaired endothelial function (Mozaffarian et al., 2006; Lopez-Garcia et al., 2005). Prospective cohorts have reported positive associations between TFA intake and risk of type 2 diabetes (Salmerón et al., 2001), and long-term data have linked higher TFA consumption to greater weight gain and abdominal adiposity (Kavanagh et al., 2007). Evidence for cancer, cognitive decline, and depression remains suggestive but inconsistent, and no causal relationship with cancer is currently established. TFA cross the placenta and are secreted in human milk, raising theoretical concerns about interference with long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acid metabolism in fetal and infant development.

## Regulatory Responses

### International framework

In 2018 the World Health Organization launched the REPLACE action package, setting a target of eliminating industrially produced TFA from the global food supply and defining two best-practice policy options: (1) a mandatory national limit of 2 g of iTFA per 100 g of total fat in all foods, and (2) a mandatory ban on the production and use of partially hydrogenated oils (WHO, 2018). REPLACE comprises six strategic actions—review of dietary sources, promotion of reformulation, legislation, assessment and monitoring, creation of awareness, and enforcement. The World Health Organization has since published annual global status reports tracking adoption; by the mid-2020s, more than forty countries had adopted best-practice policies, and the original 2023 elimination target was not met globally, with the largest gaps in low- and middle-income countries (WHO, 2023).

Denmark was the first country to impose a national limit, capping iTFA at 2% of total fat in oils and fats in 2003. Subsequent evaluation found a substantially larger decline in cardiovascular mortality in Denmark than in comparable Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development countries after the policy took effect, estimated at roughly 14 fewer CVD deaths per 100,000 population per year (Restrepo & Rieger, 2016). This quasi-experimental evidence is among the strongest available linking a regulatory limit to population health outcomes.

In the United States, the FDA required declaration of TFA on the Nutrition Facts panel from 2006, permitting products containing less than 0.5 g per serving to be labelled as "0 g" — a rounding rule widely criticised as permitting material intake from multiple servings. The more decisive step came in 2015, when the FDA issued a final determination that PHOs are no longer generally recognised as safe, with compliance dates phased between 2018 and 2021 (FDA, 2015). In the European Union, Regulation (EU) 2019/649 introduced a mandatory limit of 2 g of iTFA per 100 g of fat in foods, applicable from April 2021, alongside mandatory TFA declaration within the nutrition declaration where one is provided (European Commission, 2019).

Several middle-income countries have adopted the 2% standard, including India under Food Safety and Standards Authority of India regulations that phased the limit from 3% to 2% in fats and oils. Enforcement capacity, laboratory testing infrastructure, and monitoring of imported and informally produced foods remain the principal constraints in many settings.

## Controversies, Trade-offs, and Remaining Gaps

Three issues warrant emphasis. First, reformulation is not risk-neutral: replacing PHOs with palm oil or fully hydrogenated fats can increase saturated fat content, and the net cardiovascular effect of such substitutions must be evaluated rather than assumed. Second, the "0 g per serving" labelling loophole illustrates that labelling alone is a weak instrument relative to compositional limits; jurisdictions relying on disclosure have generally achieved slower reductions than those imposing caps. Third, the scientific consensus on iTFA does not extend to rTFA, and over-broad regulation that inadvertently penalises dairy and ruminant products is not supported by current evidence.

## Conclusion

The evidence base is unusually clear. Industrially produced trans fats have no established physiological requirement, adversely affect multiple lipid and inflammatory pathways, and causally increase coronary heart disease risk, with the World Health Organization attributing more than 278,000 deaths per year to their consumption (WHO, 2018; Mozaffarian et al., 2006). Regulatory responses have converged, with remarkable consistency, on a single quantitative benchmark — a limit of 2% of total fat, or 2 g per 100 g of fat — implemented alongside bans on partially hydrogenated oils, mandatory labelling, and removal of PHOs from the food supply (FDA, 2015; European Commission, 2019; WHO, 2018).

My own assessment, based on the pattern of evidence across jurisdictions, is that the scientific and policy questions are effectively settled and that the 2%-of-fat compositional limit is the most defensible standard available: it is simple, measurable, compatible with existing food technology, and supported by both the Danish natural experiment and the biochemical mechanism. The decisive remaining challenge is therefore not analytical but operational — closing the enforcement and monitoring gap in low- and middle-income countries, where trans fat intake is often highest and laboratory and inspection capacity weakest, and resisting the substitution of one adverse fat profile for another during reformulation. Eliminating industrially produced trans fats is one of the few preventive nutrition interventions with a near-certain, quantifiable mortality benefit; the outstanding task is implementation, not further debate.
