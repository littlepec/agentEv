# Industrial Trans Fats: Established Health Effects and Regulatory Responses

## Introduction

Industrial trans fats are a specific category of dietary fats that have been singled out by major public health authorities because of their adverse effects on cardiovascular health. Unlike general discussions of dietary fats, which often focus on flavor, texture, shelf stability, frying, baked goods, and spreads, the evidence on industrial trans fats centers on measurable lipid changes and serious clinical outcomes ([Dietary Fats in Everyday Cooking, n.d.](harmless_supplement.txt)). General consumer-facing materials may not state specific health outcomes or regulatory decisions, so this report relies on the World Health Organization (WHO) and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the established evidence and policy responses ([Dietary Fats in Everyday Cooking, n.d.](harmless_supplement.txt)).

The central findings are clear: industrial trans fats worsen blood lipid profiles, and high intake is associated with substantial increases in death from any cause, coronary heart disease deaths, and coronary heart disease itself. In response, WHO has launched a global elimination guide, and the FDA has removed partially hydrogenated oils (PHOs) from the category of substances generally recognized as safe (GRAS) and set compliance dates limiting their use in food. The following report details these health effects and regulatory actions, then offers an evidence-based assessment of what they imply for public health policy.

## Established Health Effects of Industrial Trans Fats

### Effects on Cholesterol and Lipid Transport

The WHO has summarized the lipid effects of trans fats in direct and unambiguous terms. According to the WHO news release from May 14, 2018, “Trans fats increases levels of LDL-cholesterol ... and decreases levels of HDL-cholesterol, which carry away cholesterol from arteries and transport it to the liver” ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). This statement identifies two simultaneous adverse changes: an increase in LDL-cholesterol and a decrease in HDL-cholesterol.

The decrease in HDL-cholesterol is particularly significant in the WHO framing because HDL is described as carrying cholesterol away from arteries and transporting it to the liver. A reduction in HDL therefore runs counter to a protective transport process. The combined effect—higher LDL and lower HDL—creates a more atherogenic lipid profile. Although the WHO news release does not quantify the lipid changes in milligrams per deciliter, it establishes the direction of effect and the biological plausibility of harm ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

### Clinical and Mortality Outcomes

The WHO fact sheet on trans fat provides quantitative estimates of the risk increases associated with high intake. It states that “high intake of trans fat increases the risk of death from any cause by 34%, coronary heart disease deaths by 28%, and coronary heart disease by 21%” ([World Health Organization, n.d.](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). These figures are relative increases in risk, not absolute risks, and they apply specifically to high intake rather than to any level of consumption. Even as relative measures, they are substantial and point to a serious public health burden.

The table below organizes these outcomes and their reported magnitudes.

| Health Outcome | Reported Increase with High Trans Fat Intake | Source |
|---|---:|---|
| Death from any cause | 34% | WHO fact sheet ([World Health Organization, n.d.](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)) |
| Coronary heart disease deaths | 28% | WHO fact sheet ([World Health Organization, n.d.](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)) |
| Coronary heart disease | 21% | WHO fact sheet ([World Health Organization, n.d.](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)) |

These estimates cover both fatal and nonfatal coronary outcomes. The 34% increase in death from any cause is the broadest and most severe reported outcome, while the 28% increase in coronary heart disease deaths and the 21% increase in coronary heart disease indicate a consistent cardiovascular signal. The fact sheet thus presents industrial trans fats as a risk factor for both mortality and morbidity ([World Health Organization, n.d.](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)).

### Interpretation and Significance

Taken together, the lipid and clinical findings support a strong conclusion: industrial trans fats are harmful to cardiovascular health. The WHO evidence links a biological mechanism—higher LDL and lower HDL—to hard outcomes such as coronary heart disease and death. This is not a matter of vague dietary advice or balanced fat consumption in general. It is a specific ingredient-level risk.

The fact that the WHO fact sheet was retrieved on September 14, 2026, indicates that these figures remain part of the current WHO public health position ([World Health Organization, n.d.](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). The 2018 news release similarly frames trans fats as a target for elimination, not merely reduction ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The consistency across sources strengthens the case for regulatory action.

## Main Regulatory Responses

### Global Response: The WHO REPLACE Guide

At the global level, the principal regulatory and policy response is the WHO’s REPLACE initiative. The WHO news release states that “WHO today released REPLACE, a step-by-step guide for the elimination of industrially-produced trans-fatty acids from the global food supply” ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The release is dated May 14, 2018, and it positions elimination—not just reduction—as the goal.

REPLACE is a guide rather than a binding international treaty, but it functions as a global policy roadmap. It provides a step-by-step approach for countries and food producers to remove industrially-produced trans-fatty acids from the food supply. The same WHO news release reiterates the lipid effects of trans fats, connecting the scientific evidence directly to the policy recommendation ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

### National Response: FDA and Partially Hydrogenated Oils

In the United States, the FDA has taken a regulatory approach that targets partially hydrogenated oils (PHOs). According to the FDA, it released a final determination in 2015 that PHOs are not Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). The removal of GRAS status is a significant legal and regulatory step because it means PHOs are no longer presumed safe for use in food.

The FDA also established a compliance date. The agency states that “June 18, 2018, remains the date after which manufacturers cannot add PHOs to foods,” with some uses extended to 2020 or 2021 ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). The extensions indicate that the transition was phased for certain uses, but the overall direction is clear: PHOs are being removed from the food supply.

The table below summarizes the key regulatory timeline.

| Year | Actor | Action | Target |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 2015 | FDA | Final determination that PHOs are not GRAS | Partially hydrogenated oils ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)) |
| 2018 | WHO | Released REPLACE, a step-by-step guide for elimination | Industrially-produced trans-fatty acids in the global food supply ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)) |
| 2018 | FDA | Date after which manufacturers cannot add PHOs to foods | PHOs in U.S. foods ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)) |
| 2020–2021 | FDA | Extensions for some uses | Certain PHO uses ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)) |

### Comparison of Regulatory Approaches

The WHO and FDA responses differ in scope and instrument but share the same objective. WHO provides a global, step-by-step guide aimed at eliminating industrially-produced trans-fatty acids from the worldwide food supply. The FDA provides a national regulatory determination that removes GRAS status from PHOs and sets enforceable compliance dates. The comparison is summarized below.

| Dimension | WHO | FDA |
|---|---|---|
| Level | Global | National |
| Instrument | Step-by-step guide (REPLACE) | Regulatory determination and compliance dates |
| Target | Industrially-produced trans-fatty acids | Partially hydrogenated oils (PHOs) |
| Key date | 2018 | 2015; June 18, 2018; extensions to 2020/2021 |
| Mechanism | Elimination roadmap | Removal of GRAS status and prohibition on adding PHOs to foods |
| Source | ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)) | ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)) |

The two approaches are complementary. The WHO guide establishes a global norm and implementation framework, while the FDA action demonstrates that a national regulator can remove a harmful food ingredient from the market. The FDA’s extensions for some uses also show that implementation may require technical adjustments, but they do not undermine the goal of elimination.

## Assessment and Opinion

Based on the provided information, the evidence is sufficient to support a decisive conclusion: industrial trans fats should be eliminated from the food supply, not merely reduced or labeled. The WHO reports that high intake increases death from any cause by 34%, coronary heart disease deaths by 28%, and coronary heart disease by 21% ([World Health Organization, n.d.](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). These are large relative risk increases for serious outcomes. The biological mechanism is also coherent: trans fats raise LDL-cholesterol and lower HDL-cholesterol, with HDL described as carrying cholesterol away from arteries to the liver ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

In my assessment, the appropriate policy response is mandatory elimination of industrially-produced trans fats, with phase-in periods only where technically unavoidable. Voluntary labeling or consumer education alone would likely be insufficient because the risk is tied to a food ingredient that manufacturers can reformulate. The FDA’s determination that PHOs are not GRAS provides a concrete regulatory model: remove the presumption of safety, set a compliance date, and allow limited extensions for specialized uses ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). The WHO’s REPLACE guide provides the global counterpart by offering a step-by-step elimination strategy ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

This opinion is grounded in the specific figures and regulatory actions provided. The background source on everyday cooking does not state specific health outcomes or regulatory decisions, so it cannot be used to evaluate the risks or policy responses ([Dietary Fats in Everyday Cooking, n.d.](harmless_supplement.txt)). The WHO fact sheet figures are relative increases associated with high intake, not absolute risks for every individual. The FDA timeline also includes extensions, meaning implementation is phased. Even with these limitations, the direction of evidence and regulation is consistent: industrial trans fats are a modifiable cardiovascular risk, and elimination is the most defensible goal.

## Conclusion

Industrial trans fats have well-documented adverse effects. They increase LDL-cholesterol and decrease HDL-cholesterol, and high intake is associated with a 34% increase in death from any cause, a 28% increase in coronary heart disease deaths, and a 21% increase in coronary heart disease ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [World Health Organization, n.d.](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). In response, WHO released the REPLACE guide in 2018 to support global elimination of industrially-produced trans-fatty acids ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The FDA determined in 2015 that PHOs are not GRAS and set June 18, 2018, as the date after which manufacturers cannot add PHOs to foods, with some uses extended to 2020/2021 ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). Together, these health findings and regulatory measures support continued and complete removal of industrial trans fats from the food supply.
