# Industrial Trans Fatty Acids: Established Health Effects and Regulatory Responses

## Introduction

Industrially-produced trans fatty acids (iTFAs) are unsaturated fatty acids generated when liquid vegetable oils are partially hydrogenated to make them more solid and shelf-stable. Because partially hydrogenated oils (PHOs) were, for decades, a low-cost functional ingredient in processed foods, they became a structural feature of the global food supply rather than a matter of individual dietary choice. That structural character explains why the two most prominent institutional responses reviewed here — the World Health Organization's *REPLACE* package and the United States Food and Drug Administration's PHO determination — both target the food supply rather than consumer behaviour. The health rationale for those responses rests on two pillars: a well-defined effect on blood cholesterol transport, and large relative increases in cardiovascular disease and mortality associated with high intake ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [World Health Organization, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)).

## Established Health Effects

### Effects on the Lipid Profile

The most direct and mechanistically specific effect of trans fats is their impact on circulating lipoproteins. According to the World Health Organization, trans fats "increase levels of LDL-cholesterol ... and decrease levels of HDL-cholesterol, which carry away cholesterol from arteries and transport it to the liver" ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The significance of this dual effect lies in its direction: the same dietary component simultaneously raises the lipoprotein fraction associated with cholesterol deposition in arteries and lowers the fraction that removes cholesterol from arterial tissue and returns it to the liver for clearance. The WHO frames this as the central biological rationale for treating industrially-produced trans fats as a food-safety and public-health concern rather than a nutrient of neutral or uncertain consequence ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

### Cardiovascular Morbidity and Mortality

The clinical consequences of that lipid profile are quantified in WHO's trans fat fact sheet, which reports that "high intake of trans fat increases the risk of death from any cause by 34%, coronary heart disease deaths by 28%, and coronary heart disease by 21%" ([World Health Organization, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). These three figures are worth disaggregating, because they describe different endpoints rather than a single summary statistic.

| Health endpoint | Reported increase associated with high trans fat intake |
|---|---|
| Death from any cause | 34% |
| Death from coronary heart disease | 28% |
| Coronary heart disease (event/occurrence) | 21% |

Two features of these estimates deserve emphasis. First, the coronary heart disease endpoint is the smallest of the three (21%), while all-cause mortality is the largest (34%) ([World Health Organization, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). Second, the estimates apply explicitly to *high* intake, which means the reported magnitudes describe the upper tail of exposure rather than the average effect across an entire population; they indicate direction and substantiality of risk but do not, by themselves, define a safe threshold or a dose–response slope across lower exposure levels ([World Health Organization, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)).

The combination of a coherent biological mechanism with elevated risks for both fatal and non-fatal coronary outcomes is what makes trans fats an unusually tractable policy target. The WHO's decision to publish *REPLACE* as a "step-by-step guide for the elimination of industrially-produced trans-fatty acids from the global food supply" implies a judgment that the exposure is both harmful and removable, and that the appropriate level of intervention is the composition of the food supply itself ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

## Regulatory Responses

### The WHO REPLACE Package (2018)

On 14 May 2018, the WHO released *REPLACE*, described as a step-by-step guide for eliminating industrially-produced trans fatty acids from the global food supply ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The release was accompanied by the organization's statement of the underlying lipid mechanism, linking the policy instrument directly to the established effect of trans fats on LDL and HDL cholesterol ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The provided source material does not enumerate the individual components of the *REPLACE* acronym, nor does it specify a numerical elimination target or a binding timetable; what it establishes is the character of the intervention — sequential, implementation-oriented guidance aimed at national food-supply policy rather than at clinical or behavioural recommendations to individuals ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

### United States FDA Action on Partially Hydrogenated Oils

The FDA's approach operates through the food-additive framework rather than through dietary guidance. In 2015, the agency "released its final determination that Partially Hydrogenated Oils (PHOs) are not Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS)" ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). Removing GRAS status is legally consequential: it converts PHOs from an ingredient presumed safe by default into a substance whose use requires an affirmative safety demonstration ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

The operative compliance date followed in 2018: "June 18, 2018, remains the date after which manufacturers cannot add PHOs to foods," although "some uses" were granted extensions into 2020 and 2021 ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). The extensions are an instructive detail. They indicate that the FDA judged the hazard sufficiently established to justify a general prohibition, while acknowledging that particular product categories required additional lead time — presumably for reformulation or for identification of functional substitutes — before removal could be achieved without disruption.

| Date | Institution | Action |
|---|---|---|

| 14 May 2018 | WHO | Release of *REPLACE*, a step-by-step guide to eliminating industrially-produced trans fats globally ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)) |
| 18 June 2018 | U.S. FDA | Date after which manufacturers cannot add PHOs to foods ([U.S. FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)) |
| 2020–2021 | U.S. FDA | Extended compliance dates for some specified uses ([U.S. FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)) |

Read together, the two instruments are complementary in design but convergent in direction. The WHO instrument is advisory, global in scope, and oriented toward national policy design; the FDA instrument is binding within one jurisdiction and operates by reclassifying the ingredient's regulatory status. Both, however, act on the availability of industrially-produced trans fats in the food supply rather than on consumers' choices ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

## Critical Appraisal of the Evidence Base

The sources examined here are institutional and authoritative: two WHO documents and one U.S. federal agency document, with the WHO fact sheet being the most recently retrieved (14 September 2026) and therefore the most current statement of quantified risk ([World Health Organization, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). The 2018 WHO news release is older and functions partly as an announcement document, but its value lies in the explicit statement of the LDL/HDL mechanism and in dating the launch of *REPLACE* ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The FDA document is public-domain U.S. government material and carries the authority of a formal regulatory determination ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

Three limitations should be acknowledged. First, the risk estimates are expressed as relative increases for high intake and are not accompanied, in the provided material, by absolute risk figures, confidence intervals, or the population groups and study designs from which they derive ([World Health Organization, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). Second, the provided material does not specify a quantitative exposure threshold below which risk is considered negligible, nor an intake-reduction target. Third, the WHO material describes *REPLACE* as step-by-step guidance without detailing its constituent steps, so the completeness of the policy architecture cannot be evaluated from these sources alone ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

## Assessment and Outlook

On the basis of the information provided, the regulatory trajectory is well justified. The evidence rests on a mechanism that is directionally unambiguous — LDL up, HDL down — combined with relative risk increases for coronary heart disease and coronary heart disease death in the range of roughly one-fifth to one-third among high-intake populations ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [World Health Organization, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). When a dietary exposure has no established benefit, raises the atherogenic lipoprotein fraction, lowers the protective one, and is associated with elevated coronary and all-cause mortality, reclassifying it as not generally recognized as safe becomes a proportionate response rather than a precautionary overreach ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

The more instructive question is not whether elimination is warranted but whether it is being achieved. The FDA's staggered compliance dates — a general prohibition from 18 June 2018, with particular uses extended into 2020 and 2021 — show that the binding constraint is technical substitutability in specific product categories, not scientific uncertainty about hazard ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). The same logic applies to the WHO's global ambition: a document that provides step-by-step guidance implies that the principal obstacle is implementation capacity across jurisdictions with differing regulatory systems, industry structures, and monitoring resources ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). My view, based strictly on these sources, is therefore that the scientific case is essentially settled while the policy case remains open — and that the marginal value of further evidence generation is now lower than the marginal value of enforcement, substitution, and surveillance, particularly for the product categories that required extended compliance timelines.

## Conclusion

Industrially-produced trans fatty acids are established to raise LDL cholesterol and lower HDL cholesterol, and high intake is associated with a 34% increase in death from any cause, a 28% increase in coronary heart disease deaths, and a 21% increase in coronary heart disease ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [World Health Organization, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). Two principal regulatory responses follow from that evidence. The WHO issued *REPLACE* in May 2018 as a step-by-step guide to eliminating industrially-produced trans fats from the global food supply ([World Health Organization, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The FDA determined in 2015 that PHOs are not generally recognized as safe, prohibiting their addition to foods after 18 June 2018, with extensions for some uses into 2020 and 2021 ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). The convergence of a global advisory framework and a binding national food-additive determination, both aimed at supply-side removal, represents the current state of the response.
