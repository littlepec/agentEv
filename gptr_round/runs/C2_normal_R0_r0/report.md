# Industrial Trans Fats: Established Health Effects and Regulatory Responses

## Introduction

Industrial trans fats, particularly industrially-produced trans-fatty acids, are a well-recognized public health concern because of their adverse effects on cardiovascular health. The World Health Organization (WHO) has characterized trans fats as substances that increase levels of LDL-cholesterol and decrease levels of HDL-cholesterol, the latter of which helps carry cholesterol away from arteries and transport it to the liver ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The WHO has also reported that high intake of trans fat is associated with substantially elevated risks of death from any cause, coronary heart disease deaths, and coronary heart disease itself ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). In parallel, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has taken regulatory action by determining that Partially Hydrogenated Oils (PHOs) are not Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS), with a compliance date after which manufacturers cannot add PHOs to foods ([FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). This report synthesizes the established health effects of industrial trans fats and the main regulatory responses, using the WHO and FDA sources as the primary evidence base.

The central conclusion of this report is that the evidence supports treating industrial trans fats as a high-priority, population-level cardiovascular risk rather than merely an individual dietary concern. The combination of mechanistic cholesterol effects and large relative risk increases for coronary heart disease and mortality provides a strong scientific rationale for regulatory elimination ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). The WHO’s REPLACE guide and the FDA’s PHO determination are therefore appropriate and complementary actions: one provides global guidance, while the other demonstrates enforceable national regulation ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

## Background and Definitions

### Industrial Trans Fats and Partially Hydrogenated Oils

The WHO’s 2018 news release specifically addresses “industrially-produced trans-fatty acids” and announces a plan to eliminate them from the global food supply ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The FDA’s regulatory action is framed around Partially Hydrogenated Oils (PHOs) and the removal of trans fat from the food supply ([FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). Together, these sources identify PHOs and industrially-produced trans fats as the primary targets of regulatory and public health intervention ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

### Biological Mechanism

The WHO states that trans fats increase levels of LDL-cholesterol and decrease levels of HDL-cholesterol, which carry away cholesterol from arteries and transport it to the liver ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). This lipid shift is directly relevant to cardiovascular risk because LDL-cholesterol is commonly associated with atherosclerotic plaque development, while HDL-cholesterol performs a reverse-transport function that removes cholesterol from arterial walls ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The WHO’s statement therefore provides a mechanistic explanation that aligns with the epidemiological findings discussed below ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)).

## Established Health Effects

### Cholesterol and Lipoprotein Effects

The primary established health effect of industrial trans fats is an unfavorable change in blood lipid profiles. According to the WHO, trans fats increase LDL-cholesterol and decrease HDL-cholesterol ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). This is a two-directional adverse effect: the rise in LDL-cholesterol and the fall in HDL-cholesterol both move the lipid profile in an atherogenic direction ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). Because HDL-cholesterol carries cholesterol away from arteries and to the liver, a reduction in HDL-cholesterol may impair a protective pathway ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The WHO’s description therefore supports the conclusion that trans fats are not neutral dietary components but active contributors to cardiovascular risk ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

### Cardiovascular Disease and Mortality

The WHO fact sheet provides quantitative risk estimates for high intake of trans fat. It reports that high intake of trans fat increases the risk of death from any cause by 34%, coronary heart disease deaths by 28%, and coronary heart disease by 21% ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). These figures are substantial and indicate that trans fat consumption is associated not only with nonfatal cardiovascular events but also with fatal outcomes ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). The fact sheet was retrieved on September 14, 2026, making it the most current quantitative source used in this report ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)).

### Population Health Significance

The magnitude of these risk estimates is important for public health policy. A 34% increase in the risk of death from any cause, a 28% increase in coronary heart disease deaths, and a 21% increase in coronary heart disease are large enough to justify population-level intervention rather than reliance on individual dietary choices alone ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). The mechanistic evidence from the WHO—specifically the increase in LDL-cholesterol and decrease in HDL-cholesterol—further strengthens the case for action because it provides a plausible biological pathway linking trans fat intake to cardiovascular harm ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). In my assessment, the combination of consistent mechanistic effects and large relative risks makes industrial trans fats a particularly suitable target for regulatory elimination, because the harm is serious, widespread, and avoidable through supply-side changes ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)).

## Regulatory Responses

### WHO REPLACE Initiative

In 2018, the WHO released REPLACE, described as a step-by-step guide for the elimination of industrially-produced trans-fatty acids from the global food supply ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The WHO’s stated plan was to eliminate industrially-produced trans-fatty acids from the global food supply, reflecting a global normative and policy commitment ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The REPLACE guide is significant because it provides a structured approach for countries and stakeholders rather than simply describing the problem ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The WHO’s action is especially relevant given the health effects reported in its later fact sheet, which documents elevated mortality and coronary heart disease risks associated with high trans fat intake ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)).

### US FDA Determination on Partially Hydrogenated Oils

The FDA’s regulatory response has been more directly enforceable within the United States. The FDA released its final determination that Partially Hydrogenated Oils (PHOs) are not Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) in 2015 ([FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). The agency further stated that June 18, 2018, remains the date after which manufacturers cannot add PHOs to foods, with some uses extended to 2020 or 2021 ([FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). This determination represents a supply-side restriction: rather than relying solely on consumer education or labeling, the FDA removed the GRAS status of PHOs and set a compliance date after which their addition to food is not permitted ([FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). The extension of certain uses to 2020 and 2021 indicates that implementation involved transitional complexity, but the overall direction was toward removal of PHOs from the food supply ([FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

### Comparative Analysis of the Two Responses

The WHO and FDA responses operate at different levels of governance. The WHO released a global step-by-step guide aimed at eliminating industrially-produced trans-fatty acids from the global food supply ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The FDA, by contrast, issued a national regulatory determination that PHOs are not GRAS and established a date after which manufacturers cannot add them to foods ([FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). The WHO action is therefore best understood as normative and strategic guidance, while the FDA action is a binding regulatory measure with compliance dates ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). The two responses are complementary: the WHO provides a global framework, and the FDA provides an example of national implementation ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

## Policy Implications and Assessment

The evidence presented in the WHO and FDA sources supports a clear policy direction: industrial trans fats should be eliminated from the food supply rather than managed only through dietary advice. The WHO’s mechanistic finding that trans fats increase LDL-cholesterol and decrease HDL-cholesterol provides a biological rationale for intervention ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The WHO’s risk estimates—34% higher all-cause mortality, 28% higher coronary heart disease deaths, and 21% higher coronary heart disease—provide quantitative evidence of serious harm ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). The FDA’s determination that PHOs are not GRAS and its compliance date after which manufacturers cannot add PHOs to foods demonstrate that supply-side regulation is feasible ([FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). In my assessment, the regulatory responses are scientifically justified and should be maintained and extended globally, because the identified health risks are substantial and the primary target—industrially-produced trans fats—is amenable to regulatory removal ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat); [FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

At the same time, the sources reveal some limitations that should be acknowledged. The WHO fact sheet reports relative risk increases but does not provide absolute risk figures or dose-response thresholds in the supplied information ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). The FDA source indicates that some uses of PHOs were extended to 2020 or 2021, suggesting that complete removal required transitional arrangements ([FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). The WHO’s REPLACE guide is described as a step-by-step guide, but the provided information does not detail its implementation components or enforcement mechanisms ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). These gaps do not undermine the case for action, but they indicate that continued monitoring, evaluation, and national implementation will be necessary ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

## Conclusion

Industrial trans fats are associated with unfavorable changes in cholesterol profiles and with significantly increased risks of death from any cause, coronary heart disease deaths, and coronary heart disease ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). The main regulatory responses identified in the sources are the WHO’s 2018 REPLACE guide for global elimination of industrially-produced trans-fatty acids and the FDA’s final determination that PHOs are not GRAS, with a compliance date after which manufacturers cannot add PHOs to foods ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). Based on the provided evidence, the most defensible position is that industrial trans fats should be treated as a serious, avoidable cardiovascular risk and that regulatory elimination—rather than reliance on individual behavior alone—is warranted. The WHO and FDA actions therefore represent appropriate and mutually reinforcing responses, though global implementation and evaluation remain essential ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat); [FDA, n.d.](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).
