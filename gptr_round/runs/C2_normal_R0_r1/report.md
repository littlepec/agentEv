# Health Effects of Industrial Trans Fats and Regulatory Responses

## Introduction

Industrial trans fatty acids (TFA) are a class of food fats associated with significant cardiovascular and mortality risks. The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified industrially-produced TFA as a target for global elimination and released REPLACE, a step-by-step guide for removing these fats from the global food supply, on May 14, 2018 ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has taken a regulatory route by determining that partially hydrogenated oils (PHOs), a key industrial source of trans fats, are not Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). Together, these sources document a clear scientific and policy consensus: industrial TFA are harmful, and their removal from the food supply is a public health priority. This report summarizes the established health effects and the main regulatory responses based on these authoritative sources.

## Established Health Effects of Industrial Trans Fats

### Effects on Cholesterol and Lipid Transport

The WHO states that trans fats increase levels of LDL-cholesterol and decrease levels of HDL-cholesterol, which carry away cholesterol from arteries and transport it to the liver ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). This dual effect is central to the biological concern: LDL-cholesterol is raised, while HDL-cholesterol, described by WHO as the fraction that removes cholesterol from arteries, is lowered. The result is a lipid profile that is unfavorable for cardiovascular health. The WHO’s description therefore links industrial trans fat consumption to a mechanism that can promote cholesterol accumulation in arteries and increase coronary risk ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

### Cardiovascular Disease and Mortality Outcomes

The health consequences of high trans fat intake are not limited to laboratory lipid changes. According to the WHO fact sheet on trans fat, high intake of trans fat increases the risk of death from any cause by 34%, coronary heart disease deaths by 28%, and coronary heart disease by 21% ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). These figures represent substantial percentage increases in risk for outcomes that are common and serious. The same source distinguishes between all-cause mortality, fatal coronary heart disease, and coronary heart disease more broadly, indicating that industrial trans fats are associated with both fatal and non-fatal cardiovascular events ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)).

The combination of mechanistic evidence and disease outcome evidence strengthens the case that industrial TFA are not merely a nutritional concern but a major preventable risk factor. The WHO’s decision to release REPLACE was explicitly tied to these health effects, including the adverse changes in LDL-cholesterol and HDL-cholesterol ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The FDA’s separate safety determination reinforces the same conclusion from a national regulatory perspective ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

## Main Regulatory Responses

### WHO REPLACE and Global Elimination

The WHO’s primary regulatory and policy response has been the REPLACE initiative. On May 14, 2018, WHO released REPLACE as a step-by-step guide for the elimination of industrially-produced trans-fatty acids from the global food supply ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The stated objective is elimination, not merely reduction or labeling. This reflects the WHO’s assessment that trans fats raise LDL-cholesterol and lower HDL-cholesterol, thereby increasing cardiovascular risk ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

The WHO fact sheet provides the epidemiological basis for this global policy approach by reporting the 34%, 28%, and 21% risk increases for all-cause death, coronary heart disease deaths, and coronary heart disease, respectively ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). REPLACE therefore functions as a practical policy template for governments seeking to remove industrial TFA from their food supplies. The WHO’s approach is global in scope and focuses specifically on industrially-produced trans fats, which are the category most amenable to regulatory action ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)).

### FDA Determination on Partially Hydrogenated Oils

In the United States, the FDA adopted a food-safety regulatory response. The FDA released its final determination that Partially Hydrogenated Oils (PHOs) are not Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) in 2015 ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). This determination removed the regulatory basis that had allowed PHOs to be added to foods without premarket approval. The FDA further established that June 18, 2018, remains the date after which manufacturers cannot add PHOs to foods, with some uses extended to 2020 or 2021 ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)).

This action is significant because PHOs are directly linked to the removal of trans fat from the food supply. The FDA’s final determination and compliance dates represent a transition from permitted use to prohibited use, subject to limited extensions for certain applications ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). The FDA action is therefore a concrete national regulatory response that complements the WHO’s global elimination agenda.

## Synthesis and Assessment

The evidence provided by WHO and the FDA supports a strong and concrete conclusion: industrial trans fats are harmful food components, and regulatory elimination is a justified public health objective. The WHO data show that high intake increases all-cause mortality by 34%, coronary heart disease deaths by 28%, and coronary heart disease by 21% ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). These are large percentage increases for serious outcomes, and they are accompanied by a plausible biological mechanism involving increased LDL-cholesterol and decreased HDL-cholesterol ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The combination of mechanism and outcome evidence makes industrial TFA a clear target for population-level intervention.

The regulatory responses align with this evidence. The WHO’s REPLACE guide calls for elimination of industrially-produced TFA from the global food supply ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). The FDA’s determination that PHOs are not GRAS, followed by a no-add date of June 18, 2018 and extensions for some uses to 2020/2021, demonstrates that national regulators can remove a major industrial source of trans fats from foods ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). In my assessment, the most valid conclusion from these sources is that elimination policies are preferable to approaches that merely inform consumers or permit continued use. The health risks are substantial, the mechanism is well described, and the regulatory precedents show that removal is feasible.

That said, the sources also indicate that implementation can be phased. The FDA allowed some PHO uses to continue until 2020 or 2021, suggesting that certain food applications required additional time to transition ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). This does not weaken the case for elimination; rather, it shows that regulatory action can include compliance timelines while maintaining the ultimate goal of removing PHOs from the food supply.

## Limitations of the Provided Evidence

The provided sources establish direction and magnitude of risk, but they do not provide absolute risk figures, dose-response thresholds, baseline intake distributions, or the number of deaths and coronary heart disease events that could be prevented by complete elimination. The WHO fact sheet reports percentage increases in risk, not absolute risks ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). The WHO news release and FDA source provide policy dates and regulatory determinations but do not quantify current global compliance, enforcement, or the extent to which industrial TFA remain in food supplies ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply); [FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). These gaps limit the ability to calculate the full population impact from these documents alone.

Even with these limitations, the direction of the evidence is consistent and unambiguous. Industrial trans fats adversely affect cholesterol transport, increase major cardiovascular and mortality risks, and have prompted both global and national elimination efforts. The appropriate conclusion is not that more evidence is needed before action, but that the existing evidence is sufficient to support elimination policies while monitoring implementation and evaluating outcomes.

## Conclusion

Industrial trans fats, particularly those from partially hydrogenated oils, increase LDL-cholesterol and decrease HDL-cholesterol ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)). High intake is associated with a 34% increase in death from any cause, a 28% increase in coronary heart disease deaths, and a 21% increase in coronary heart disease ([WHO, 2026](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). The main regulatory responses are the WHO’s REPLACE initiative, released in 2018 as a step-by-step guide for global elimination of industrially-produced TFA ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/news/item/14-05-2018-who-plan-to-eliminate-industrially-produced-trans-fatty-acids-from-global-food-supply)), and the FDA’s determination that PHOs are not GRAS, with June 18, 2018, as the date after which manufacturers cannot add PHOs to foods, subject to extensions to 2020/2021 for some uses ([FDA, 2026](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils-removing-trans-fat)). Based on these authoritative sources, industrial trans fat elimination is a scientifically justified and practical public health objective.
