## Introduction

Industrial trans fats, also called industrially produced trans-fatty acids (iTFAs) or partially hydrogenated oils (PHOs), are unsaturated fatty acids with at least one trans double bond. They are created when liquid vegetable oils are partially hydrogenated to make them more solid and shelf-stable, a process historically used in margarine, shortening, fried foods, baked goods, snack foods, and coffee creamers ([World Health Organization [WHO], 2023](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). Small amounts of trans fats also occur naturally in meat and dairy from ruminant animals, but the dominant public health concern is industrially produced trans fat.

The health evidence on industrial trans fats is unusually consistent: they raise harmful cholesterol, lower protective cholesterol, promote inflammation, and increase the risk of cardiovascular disease. The WHO estimates that trans fat intake causes more than 500,000 deaths from cardiovascular disease each year ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). Because no safe level of industrial trans fat consumption has been identified, the policy response has moved from voluntary labeling and dietary advice toward mandatory limits and bans ([Institute of Medicine [IOM], 2005](https://www.nap.edu/catalog/10490)). This report summarizes the established health effects and the main regulatory responses, including national bans, regional limits, U.S. federal action, and the WHO REPLACE initiative.

## Health Effects of Industrial Trans Fats

### Cardiovascular Disease

The strongest and most consistent evidence links industrial trans fat intake to coronary heart disease (CHD). A landmark review found that a 2% increase in energy intake from trans fats was associated with a 23% increase in the risk of CHD ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra054035)). This effect is larger than that observed for saturated fat at comparable energy levels, partly because trans fats adversely affect multiple lipid and non-lipid pathways simultaneously.

Mechanistically, industrial trans fats increase low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, decrease high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and increase triglycerides ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra054035)). The combination of higher LDL and lower HDL is particularly atherogenic. Trans fats also increase the ratio of total cholesterol to HDL cholesterol, a strong predictor of cardiovascular risk. In addition, they promote systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and insulin resistance, all of which contribute to atherosclerosis and cardiovascular events ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra054035)). The Institute of Medicine concluded that trans fatty acids have no known health benefit and that no safe level of consumption can be recommended ([IOM, 2005](https://www.nap.edu/catalog/10490)).

### Metabolic, Inflammatory, and Other Health Outcomes

Beyond cholesterol effects, industrial trans fats have been associated with increased markers of inflammation, including C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra054035)). These inflammatory changes may help explain why trans fat intake is linked not only to CHD but also to sudden cardiac death and possibly to type 2 diabetes. The WHO notes that trans fat intake is associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease and may also increase the risk of type 2 diabetes, although the evidence for diabetes is less definitive than for CHD ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)).

Evidence for other outcomes is more mixed. Some studies suggest associations with ovulatory infertility, cognitive decline, and adverse fetal development, but these findings are not as robust as the cardiovascular evidence ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). For cancer, the available evidence is insufficient to establish a causal link. The overall pattern, however, is that industrial trans fats provide no nutritional benefit and create substantial cardiovascular harm.

## Main Regulatory Responses

### Labeling and Dietary Guidance

One of the earliest regulatory responses was mandatory nutrition labeling. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) required trans fat to be listed on the Nutrition Facts label beginning in 2006 ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration [FDA], 2023](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/trans-fat)). This allowed consumers to compare products and created pressure on manufacturers to reformulate. However, labeling alone has limitations: products can be labeled as containing “0 g trans fat” if they contain less than 0.5 g per serving, which can lead to underestimated intake when multiple servings are consumed ([FDA, 2023](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/trans-fat)).

International dietary guidance has also converged. The WHO recommends that trans fat intake be limited to less than 1% of total energy intake, with the goal of eliminating industrially produced trans fats from the food supply ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). The Institute of Medicine went further, stating that trans fat has no known health benefit and that intake should be as low as possible ([IOM, 2005](https://www.nap.edu/catalog/10490)). These recommendations reflect the strong evidence that even small amounts increase cardiovascular risk.

### National and Regional Bans and Limits

The most effective regulatory approach has been mandatory limits or bans. Denmark became the first country to introduce a nationwide limit in 2003, capping industrial trans fats at 2 g per 100 g of fat in oils and fats ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241513992)). Other countries followed with similar best-practice policies. The European Union adopted Commission Regulation (EU) 2019/649, which set a maximum limit of 2 g of industrial trans fats per 100 g of fat in food, applicable from April 2021 ([European Commission, 2019](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2019/649/oj)). Canada prohibited partially hydrogenated oils in foods in 2018 ([Health Canada, 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/healthy-eating/trans-fats.html)).

In the United States, subnational action preceded federal action. New York City restricted trans fats in food service establishments between 2007 and 2008, and California enacted a statewide restaurant ban in 2008 ([Resolve to Save Lives, 2023](https://resolvetosavelives.org/trans-fat)). These measures demonstrated that replacement fats could be used without major disruption to taste or cost.

### FDA Action in the United States

The FDA’s 2015 final determination was a pivotal regulatory step. After reviewing the evidence, the agency concluded that partially hydrogenated oils—the primary source of industrial trans fats—were no longer generally recognized as safe (GRAS) for any use in human food ([FDA, 2015](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils)). Manufacturers were given until June 18, 2018, to remove PHOs, with extensions for certain uses until January 1, 2020, and January 1, 2021 ([FDA, 2015](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils)). After these dates, PHOs could no longer be used in U.S. food unless specifically authorized. This action effectively eliminated the main industrial source of trans fats from the U.S. food supply ([FDA, 2023](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/trans-fat)).

### WHO REPLACE and Global Elimination

In 2018, the WHO launched the REPLACE action package, calling for the global elimination of industrially produced trans fats by 2023 ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241513992)). REPLACE stands for Review, Promote, Legislate, Assess, Create, and Enforce. The package provides a roadmap for countries to implement best-practice policies, monitor progress, and replace industrial trans fats with healthier alternatives. Best-practice policies are defined as either a mandatory national limit of 2 g of industrial trans fats per 100 g of total fat in all foods or a mandatory national ban on the production and use of partially hydrogenated oils ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240073678)).

Progress has been substantial but uneven. By 2023, 43 countries had implemented best-practice trans fat policies, protecting approximately 2.8 billion people ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240073678)). However, the original 2023 elimination target was not met globally. Many low- and middle-income countries still lack mandatory limits, and enforcement remains a challenge in informal food sectors ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240073678)). The Pan American Health Organization has also prioritized trans fat elimination in the Americas, supporting national regulations and monitoring ([Pan American Health Organization [PAHO], 2022](https://www.paho.org/en/topics/trans-fat)).

### Industry Reformulation and Monitoring

Regulatory pressure has driven widespread reformulation. Food manufacturers have replaced partially hydrogenated oils with palm oil, interesterified fats, high-oleic oils, and other alternatives ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241513992)). This transition has generally reduced trans fat intake, but it has also raised concerns about increased saturated fat content and the need to monitor the overall nutritional quality of replacement fats. Monitoring systems, including food composition databases and biomarker surveys, are essential to verify compliance and measure population exposure ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240073678)).

## Discussion: Effectiveness and Remaining Challenges

The evidence base for eliminating industrial trans fats is strong. Cardiovascular disease risk increases with intake, and there is no apparent health benefit. Regulatory bans and limits have been effective where implemented. Denmark’s early limit and subsequent declines in cardiovascular mortality are frequently cited as proof that elimination is feasible and beneficial ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241513992)). The U.S. FDA’s GRAS revocation and the EU’s regional limit demonstrate that large economies can remove PHOs from the food supply ([FDA, 2015](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils); [European Commission, 2019](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2019/649/oj)).

Nevertheless, important gaps remain. First, global coverage is incomplete: as of 2023, many countries had no best-practice policy, leaving billions of people unprotected ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240073678)). Second, enforcement is difficult in settings with weak regulatory capacity and large informal food sectors. Third, the replacement of trans fats with saturated fats may create new cardiovascular risks if not carefully managed. Fourth, naturally occurring ruminant trans fats are not the primary target of these policies, and distinguishing them from industrial trans fats in labeling and regulation can be complex ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). Finally, industry opposition and competing public health priorities have slowed action in some regions ([Resolve to Save Lives, 2023](https://resolvetosavelives.org/trans-fat)).

In my assessment, the evidence justifies mandatory elimination policies rather than relying solely on labeling or voluntary reformulation. Labeling can inform consumers, but it does not reliably eliminate exposure, especially when small serving sizes allow “0 g” claims. Mandatory limits and bans have a stronger track record of reducing population-level intake and health harms. The most equitable approach is a national best-practice policy combined with monitoring and enforcement, as recommended by the WHO REPLACE package ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241513992)).

## Conclusion

Industrial trans fats are a well-established cause of cardiovascular disease and have no known health benefit. They raise LDL cholesterol, lower HDL cholesterol, increase triglycerides, promote inflammation, and increase CHD risk. The WHO estimates more than 500,000 cardiovascular deaths annually from trans fat intake ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/trans-fat)). Regulatory responses have evolved from labeling to mandatory limits and bans. Denmark led with a 2003 national limit, followed by subnational policies in New York City and California, FDA’s 2015 GRAS revocation, Canada’s 2018 prohibition, and the EU’s 2019 regional limit ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241513992); [FDA, 2015](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils); [Health Canada, 2018](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/healthy-eating/trans-fats.html); [European Commission, 2019](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2019/649/oj)). The WHO REPLACE initiative has accelerated global action, but the 2023 elimination target was missed, and many countries still lack enforceable policies ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240073678)). Continued global, regional, and national action—especially mandatory limits or bans—remains necessary to eliminate industrial trans fats and reduce preventable cardiovascular disease.
