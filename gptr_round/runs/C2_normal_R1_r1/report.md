## Introduction

Industrial trans fatty acids (iTFAs) are unsaturated fatty acids with at least one double bond in the *trans* configuration, generated when liquid vegetable oils are partially hydrogenated to produce semi-solid fats for baking, frying and food manufacturing ([Mozaffarian, Katan, Ascherio, Stampfer, & Willett, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMra054035)). They are chemically distinct from the *trans* fats that occur naturally at low levels in ruminant meat and dairy products, and it is the industrially produced fraction that has driven the most damaging metabolic effects and the strongest regulatory response ([Brouwer, Wanders, & Katan, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0009434)).

Two features of iTFAs made them a public health priority. First, partially hydrogenated oils (PHOs) could contribute 10% to more than 40% of their fatty acids as *trans* isomers, meaning that a single manufactured fat could deliver a very large dose ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMra054035)). Second, unlike saturated fat, iTFAs were already known by the early 1990s to worsen the lipid profile at intakes of only 2–3% of total energy, which was within the range of typical consumption in many high-income countries ([Mozaffarian, Aro, & Willett, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ejcn.1602973)). The World Health Organization (WHO) has estimated that industrially produced trans fat is responsible for up to 500,000 premature deaths from coronary heart disease each year globally ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/teams/nutrition-and-food-safety/replace-trans-fat)).

## Established Health Effects

### Effects on Blood Lipids and Lipoproteins

The most reproducible and mechanistically best-understood effect of iTFAs is their adverse impact on the lipid profile. Controlled feeding trials show that *trans* fat raises low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), lowers high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), increases the LDL-C to HDL-C ratio, raises triglycerides, and increases lipoprotein(a) — a combination that is more atherogenic than the effect of saturated fat at equivalent intakes ([Mozaffarian et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ejcn.1602973)). Quantitative reviews indicate that industrial *trans* fat produces larger adverse changes in the LDL-C/HDL-C ratio than ruminant *trans* fat at comparable intake levels, supporting a distinction between the two sources ([Brouwer et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0009434)).

Beyond the lipid panel, experimental studies indicate that iTFAs increase markers of systemic inflammation such as tumour necrosis factor-α, interleukin-6 and C-reactive protein, promote endothelial dysfunction, and may reduce insulin sensitivity ([Mozaffarian et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ejcn.1602973)). These findings matter because they suggest that iTFAs exert cardiovascular harm through pathways that are not fully captured by cholesterol measurement alone.

| Biomarker / pathway | Direction of effect of industrial *trans* fat | Comparative note |
|---|---|---|
| LDL cholesterol | Increased | Stronger adverse effect than ruminant *trans* fat ([Brouwer et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0009434)) |
| HDL cholesterol | Decreased | Reduces HDL-C, unlike saturated fat ([Mozaffarian et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ejcn.1602973)) |
| LDL-C : HDL-C ratio | Increased | Key predictor of coronary risk |
| Triglycerides | Increased | Observed in controlled trials |
| Lipoprotein(a) | Increased | Pro-thrombotic/pro-atherogenic marker |
| Inflammatory markers (TNF-α, IL-6, CRP) | Increased | Supports non-lipid mechanisms |
| Endothelial function | Impaired | Observed in short-term trials |

### Coronary Heart Disease and Mortality

The observational evidence linking iTFA intake to coronary heart disease (CHD) is unusually consistent for a dietary exposure. In a widely cited systematic review, a 2% increase in energy intake from *trans* fat was associated with a 23% higher incidence of CHD (pooled relative risk 1.23; 95% confidence interval 1.11–1.37) ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMra054035)). Subsequent work extended these findings to sudden cardiac death and total mortality, and modelling studies indicated that replacing iTFAs with polyunsaturated fats would lower cardiovascular risk substantially ([Mozaffarian et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ejcn.1602973)).

Critically, the dose–response relationship appears to be approximately linear at low intakes, without an obvious threshold below which harm disappears. This is the central scientific justification for population-level regulation rather than individual dietary advice alone: if the risk function is continuous, then eliminating the industrial source of the exposure is the only way to protect the entire distribution of consumers ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/teams/nutrition-and-food-safety/replace-trans-fat)).

### Other Outcomes and Uncertainty

Evidence for outcomes other than cardiovascular disease is weaker. Associations have been reported with ovulatory infertility and with markers of systemic inflammation, but findings for type 2 diabetes, cancer and cognitive decline are inconsistent across studies ([Mozaffarian et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ejcn.1602973)). A fair reading of the literature is that the cardiovascular case is established to a degree that justifies mandatory regulation, while other endpoints remain hypothesis-generating.

## The Regulatory Response

### The WHO Framework

In 2018, WHO launched the REPLACE action package, which set a target of eliminating industrially produced *trans* fat from the global food supply and laid out six action areas: review dietary sources; promote replacement with healthier oils; legislate or regulate; assess and monitor the fat content of the food supply; create awareness; and enforce compliance ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/teams/nutrition-and-food-safety/replace-trans-fat)). WHO defines two "best-practice" policy options: a mandatory national limit of 2 g of industrially produced *trans* fat per 100 g of total fat in all foods, and/or a mandatory national ban on the production and use of PHOs ([WHO, 2024](https://www.who.int/teams/nutrition-and-food-safety/replace-trans-fat)).

WHO's most recent global status report indicated that only a minority of the world's population was covered by such policies, with approximately 53 countries having best-practice policies in force, protecting roughly 3.7 billion people — about 46% of the global population — while several billion people, concentrated in low- and middle-income countries, remained unprotected ([WHO, 2024](https://www.who.int/teams/nutrition-and-food-safety/replace-trans-fat)). WHO has also begun formally validating national elimination, recognising a first group of countries — including Denmark, Lithuania, Poland, Saudi Arabia and Thailand — as having eliminated industrially produced *trans* fat ([WHO, 2024](https://www.who.int/teams/nutrition-and-food-safety/replace-trans-fat)).

### National and Regional Policy Instruments

|---|---|---|---|

| New York City, USA | 2006–2008 | Local regulation | Phase-out of *trans* fat in restaurant foods |
| Canada | 2018 | Notice of modification | Prohibition of PHOs in foods, with a one-year transition |
| United States | 2015 (effective 2018–2021) | Food additive determination | PHOs no longer generally recognised as safe ([FDA, 2015](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2015/06/17/2015-14883/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils)) |
| European Union | 2019 (applicable 2021) | Regulation (EU) 2019/649 | Maximum 2 g of *trans* fat per 100 g of fat in foods, excluding ruminant fat ([European Commission, 2019](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2019/649/oj)) |
| Codex Alimentarius | 2019 | Commodity standard amendment | *Trans* fat limit of 2% of total fatty acids in named vegetable oils ([FAO/WHO, 2019](https://www.fao.org/fao-who-codexalimentarius/codex-texts/list-standards/en/)) |

The U.S. case illustrates both the strength and the limits of the regulatory toolkit. The Food and Drug Administration's 2015 final determination revoked the generally recognised as safe status of PHOs, with a compliance date of 18 June 2018 and staggered extensions to 1 January 2020 and 1 January 2021 for certain specified uses ([FDA, 2015](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2015/06/17/2015-14883/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils)). The FDA estimated that the measure would prevent approximately 20,000 heart attacks and 7,000 coronary heart disease deaths each year in the United States ([FDA, 2015](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2015/06/17/2015-14883/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils)).

### Labelling Rules and Their Limits

Mandatory declaration of *trans* fat on nutrition labels — introduced in the United States in 2006 and in Canada shortly before — was an important early step, but it created a well-documented loophole: products containing less than 0.5 g of *trans* fat per serving may be labelled as containing "0 g" ([FDA, 2015](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2015/06/17/2015-14883/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils)). Because consumers may eat multiple servings, small per-serving amounts can accumulate, and the rounding rule also allowed reformulated products to retain PHOs without disclosure. This is one reason why nutrition labelling alone is not considered a best-practice policy by WHO, and why countries have moved toward compositional limits and bans ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/teams/nutrition-and-food-safety/replace-trans-fat)).

## Do the Regulations Work?

The evidence base for policy effectiveness, while smaller than that for biological harm, is consistent. A systematic review of policies to reduce *trans* fat consumption found that regulatory approaches — particularly mandatory limits and bans rather than voluntary reformulation — were associated with measurable reductions in the *trans* fat content of the food supply and in population intake ([Downs et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/cdn/nzz121)).

Two natural experiments are especially instructive. Restrepo and Rieger (2016) found that Denmark's 2003 restriction was associated with a substantial decline in cardiovascular disease mortality of roughly 14% relative to comparable countries ([Restrepo & Rieger, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2015.06.018)). In New York City, hospital admissions for myocardial infarction and stroke fell by an additional 6.2% beyond the pre-existing downward trend after the restaurant *trans* fat restrictions took effect ([Brandt, Myerson, Perraillon, & Polonsky, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamacardio.2017.0491)). These findings support the position that structural regulation — not consumer education alone — is the effective lever.

## Remaining Gaps and Challenges

Three problems persist. First, coverage is unequal: the countries with the highest estimated CHD mortality attributable to *trans* fat intake are disproportionately low- and middle-income countries that lack best-practice policies ([WHO, 2024](https://www.who.int/teams/nutrition-and-food-safety/replace-trans-fat)). Second, enforcement and monitoring capacity are weak in many settings; a limit on paper does not guarantee a limit in the frying oil ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/teams/nutrition-and-food-safety/replace-trans-fat)). Third, small residual exposures remain permissible under labelling thresholds, and some food categories — informal street-vended and bakery products — are difficult to regulate and monitor ([FDA, 2015](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2015/06/17/2015-14883/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils)).

## Conclusion

The established health effects of industrial *trans* fats are unambiguous and largely irreversible in their policy implications. At intakes of only 2% of energy, iTFAs raise LDL-C, lower HDL-C, raise triglycerides and lipoprotein(a), promote inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, and are associated with approximately a 23% higher incidence of coronary heart disease ([Mozaffarian et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMra054035); [Mozaffarian et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ejcn.1602973)). Because the exposure is a by-product of a manufacturing process rather than an intrinsic property of foods, it is fully removable, and WHO's estimate of up to 500,000 attributable premature deaths annually represents avoidable mortality rather than an inevitability ([WHO, 2018](https://www.who.int/teams/nutrition-and-food-safety/replace-trans-fat)).

My assessment is that the regulatory record points clearly to one conclusion: mandatory compositional limits of 2 g per 100 g of fat and/or statutory bans on PHOs outperform voluntary reformulation and nutrition labelling, and they deliver measurable cardiovascular benefit at low cost ([Downs et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/cdn/nzz121); [Restrepo & Rieger, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2015.06.018)). The unfinished agenda is therefore not scientific but political and administrative: extending best-practice legislation to the roughly half of the world's population still unprotected, closing the sub-0.5 g labelling loophole, and investing in enforcement and biomonitoring so that stated limits reflect actual intake ([WHO, 2024](https://www.who.int/teams/nutrition-and-food-safety/replace-trans-fat); [FDA, 2015](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2015/06/17/2015-14883/final-determination-regarding-partially-hydrogenated-oils)).
