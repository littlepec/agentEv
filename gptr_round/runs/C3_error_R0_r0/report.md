# The James Webb Space Telescope: Design, Instruments, and Purpose

## Introduction and Approach to the Evidence

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is one of the most consequential astronomical facilities of the current era, and an accurate description of it depends on the reliability of the evidence used. The material supplied for this report contains a fundamental tension: a document titled *error_material.txt* presents one portrait of the observatory, while two clearly attributed sources — a NASA mission page and the Wikipedia article on the telescope — present a radically different one. These accounts cannot both be correct, because they disagree on the telescope's mirror diameter, launch date, orbital location, servicing model, wavelength coverage, and instrument suite. This report therefore begins by establishing which sources are credible, then describes the telescope's design, instruments, and purpose using the verified material. The central finding is that the *error_material.txt* description is not merely imprecise but systematically false, and that any account of JWST built on it would misrepresent the observatory at every level.

The two reliable sources are NASA's mission page for Webb, a public-domain primary source published by the operating agency, and the Wikipedia article "James Webb Space Telescope," a CC BY-SA 4.0 tertiary source retrieved on the same date ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/); [Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). Both were retrieved on 14 September 2026. Where the two agree, confidence is high; where they add complementary detail — NASA on orbital mechanics, Wikipedia on mirror specifications and instrumentation — the combination yields a coherent picture. The unnamed *error_material.txt* document provides no URL, no author, no publication date, and no retrieval information, and its claims are contradicted on multiple independent points by the two attributed sources (error_material.txt, n.d.). Under any reasonable standard of source evaluation, it must be treated as unreliable.

## Launch and Orbital Architecture

According to NASA, "Webb launched on Dec. 25th 2021," and it "orbits the Sun 1.5 million kilometers ... away from the Earth at ... the second Lagrange point or L2" ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). The Wikipedia article corroborates the date and adds the launch vehicle and site: "The Webb was launched on 25 December 2021 on an Ariane 5 rocket from Kourou, French Guiana" ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). These two statements are mutually consistent and mutually reinforcing: an independent agency source and an encyclopedic source agree on the launch date, and one supplies the vehicle and location while the other supplies the destination orbit.

The orbital location is scientifically significant rather than incidental. L2 is a gravitational equilibrium point approximately 1.5 million kilometers from Earth on the anti-Sun side, and NASA describes Webb as orbiting the Sun at that distance ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). This is a fundamentally different operating regime from low Earth orbit. A telescope at L2 is far beyond the reach of the Space Shuttle or any crewed servicing mission, and no source in the provided material suggests that Webb is serviced. The contrast with the *error_material.txt* claim is total: that document asserts Webb was "launched in 2019 into low Earth orbit, where it is periodically serviced," a description that conflicts with both reliable sources on the launch year, the orbit, and the servicing model simultaneously (error_material.txt, n.d.).

## Optical Design and Mirror Assembly

The most striking design feature of JWST is its primary mirror. Wikipedia states that "Webb's primary mirror is a 6.5 m (21 ft)-diameter gold-coated beryllium reflector with a collecting area of 25.4 m2" ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). NASA independently describes the instrument as using a "segmented, gold-coated mirror" ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). Taken together, these two sources establish three design facts: the mirror is large, with a 6.5-meter diameter and 25.4 square meters of collecting area; it is gold-coated; and it is segmented, meaning it is composed of multiple smaller elements rather than a single monolithic surface.

| Mirror material | Gold-coated beryllium | Wikipedia ([2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)) |
| Collecting area | 25.4 m² | Wikipedia ([2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)) |
| Mirror architecture | Segmented | NASA ([2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)) |
| Launch date | 25 December 2021 | NASA ([2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)); Wikipedia ([2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)) |
| Launch vehicle / site | Ariane 5 / Kourou, French Guiana | Wikipedia ([2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)) |

The *error_material.txt* document describes a "2.4-meter ultraviolet-and-optical observatory" whose mirror design "reuses the Hubble Space Telescope's mirror design" (error_material.txt, n.d.). This is contradicted by the verified 6.5-meter gold-coated beryllium segmented reflector ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). The discrepancy is not a matter of rounding or interpretation; it is a difference of more than a factor of two in diameter and an order-of-magnitude difference in collecting area. Furthermore, the claim that Webb's orbital approach reuses Hubble's is directly contradicted by the L2 solar orbit documented by NASA ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)).

## The Scientific Instrument Suite

Webb carries four science instruments, identified by Wikipedia as "NIRCam, NIRSpec, MIRI, and FGS/NIRISS" ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). The provided material does not describe the individual capabilities of these instruments, and this report does not supply details beyond what the sources state. What can be said with confidence is that the suite is a four-instrument complement, and that its naming conventions — with "NIR" denoting near-infrared and "MIRI" denoting mid-infrared — align with the observatory's documented infrared specialization ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope); [NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)).

| Instrument | Attested in provided sources | Basis for identification |
|---|---|---|
| NIRCam | Yes | Listed among Webb's four science instruments ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)) |
| NIRSpec | Yes | Same source |
| MIRI | Yes | Same source |
| FGS/NIRISS | Yes | Same source |

This four-instrument list stands in direct opposition to the *error_material.txt* assertion that Webb's "primary science instrument is the Wide Ultraviolet Spectrograph (WUVS), designed to capture high-resolution ultraviolet spectra of nearby stars" (error_material.txt, n.d.). No source among the reliable materials mentions a WUVS instrument, and an ultraviolet spectrograph would be inconsistent with an observatory documented to observe from red visible light through the mid-infrared ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)).

## Wavelength Coverage and Scientific Purpose

Webb's purpose is defined first by its wavelength range. Wikipedia states that "Webb observes a lower frequency range, from long-wavelength visible light (red) through mid-infrared (0.6-28.5 um)" ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). NASA characterizes the same capability more simply, stating that Webb uses its segmented gold-coated mirror "to observe in infrared" ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). The two descriptions are compatible: the Wikipedia range begins at the red end of the visible spectrum and extends deep into the infrared, and NASA's summary emphasizes the infrared character that dominates that range.

This has a direct consequence for how the telescope's purpose should be understood. A facility optimized for long-wavelength visible and infrared light is, by design, an infrared observatory, and its 0.6–28.5 µm band is the operative definition of its scientific reach ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). The complementary design choices documented by the sources — a large 25.4 m² gold-coated beryllium collecting surface and a segmented architecture ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope); [NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)) — are consistent with a facility intended for sensitive infrared observations, since gold reflects infrared light efficiently and a large collecting area increases sensitivity.

In my assessment, the combination of an L2 orbit, a large segmented gold-coated mirror, and an infrared-optimized four-instrument suite is best read as a coherent design philosophy rather than a set of unrelated specifications. NASA's description of Webb as orbiting the Sun at L2 while observing in infrared, and Wikipedia's specification of a 6.5-meter, 25.4 m² mirror with a 0.6–28.5 µm range, describe a single integrated system ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/); [Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). The *error_material.txt* document, by contrast, describes a 2.4-meter ultraviolet-and-optical telescope in low Earth orbit with a single ultraviolet spectrograph (error_material.txt, n.d.). That description is not a simplified version of the verified one; it is a description of a different kind of observatory that bears almost no resemblance to the telescope the reliable sources document.

## Assessment: Distinguishing Verified Data from Misinformation

Because the source material contains a direct conflict, the report's most useful contribution is an explicit adjudication.

| Claim | *error_material.txt* | Verified sources | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mirror diameter | 2.4 m | 6.5 m | Contradicted ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)) |
| Mirror type | Reuses Hubble design | Segmented gold-coated beryllium | Contradicted ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/); [Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)) |
| Launch year | 2019 | 25 December 2021 | Contradicted ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)) |
| Orbit | Low Earth orbit | Sun orbit at L2, ~1.5 million km | Contradicted ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)) |
| Servicing | Periodically serviced | No servicing described | Unsupported (error_material.txt, n.d.) |
| Wavelengths | Ultraviolet and optical | 0.6–28.5 µm, infrared-dominant | Contradicted ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)) |
| Main instrument | WUVS | NIRCam, NIRSpec, MIRI, FGS/NIRISS | Contradicted ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)) |

The pattern is unambiguous. Every testable claim in *error_material.txt* fails against the attributed sources, and the failures are internally consistent with one another — a low Earth orbit explains the servicing claim, an ultraviolet band explains the ultraviolet spectrograph, and a small mirror explains the Hubble-reuse claim (error_material.txt, n.d.). This internal coherence makes the document look self-consistent, which is precisely what makes it dangerous as a source: it reads plausibly while being factually wrong throughout. My position is that it should be excluded from any factual account of JWST, and that the NASA and Wikipedia sources should be treated as the authoritative basis for description, with NASA carrying particular weight as the operating agency's own mission documentation ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)).

## Conclusion

The James Webb Space Telescope, as documented by its operating agency and corroborated by encyclopedic reference, launched on 25 December 2021 aboard an Ariane 5 rocket from Kourou, French Guiana, and now orbits the Sun approximately 1.5 million kilometers from Earth at the second Lagrange point ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/); [Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). Its primary mirror is a 6.5-meter-diameter, gold-coated beryllium segmented reflector with a collecting area of 25.4 m² ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope); [NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). It carries four science instruments — NIRCam, NIRSpec, MIRI, and FGS/NIRISS — and observes from long-wavelength red visible light through the mid-infrared, spanning 0.6 to 28.5 µm ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). Its purpose, as defined by that design, is infrared observation from a location far from Earth, using a large gold-coated aperture and a four-instrument suite to carry out that work. Any description of the telescope as a 2.4-meter ultraviolet observatory in low Earth orbit is contradicted by the available reliable evidence and should be rejected (error_material.txt, n.d.).
