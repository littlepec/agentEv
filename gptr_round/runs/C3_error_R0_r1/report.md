# The James Webb Space Telescope: Design, Instruments, and Purpose

## Introduction and Scope

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is the flagship space-based observatory described in the source material assembled for this report. According to the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, "Webb launched on Dec. 25th 2021," and it "orbits the Sun 1.5 million kilometers ... away from the Earth at ... the second Lagrange point or L2," where it uses a "segmented, gold-coated mirror" to observe in infrared ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). The Wikipedia entry for the observatory adds that "Webb's primary mirror is a 6.5 m (21 ft)-diameter gold-coated beryllium reflector with a collecting area of 25.4 m2," that "The Webb was launched on 25 December 2021 on an Ariane 5 rocket from Kourou, French Guiana," and that it "observes a lower frequency range, from long-wavelength visible light (red) through mid-infrared (0.6-28.5 um)" ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). The same entry identifies its four science instruments as NIRCam, NIRSpec, MIRI, and FGS/NIRISS ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)).

Before describing the observatory in detail, however, this report must address a significant problem in the supplied material: one of the documents circulating in the source set contains a set of claims that are directly contradicted by the authoritative sources. That document, labelled `error_material.txt`, presents a description of JWST that conflicts with both NASA and Wikipedia on nearly every major parameter ([error_material.txt, n.d.](error_material.txt)). Because the task requires prioritizing relevance, reliability, and significance of sources, the first substantive section below establishes which claims can be trusted and which must be discarded. Only after that screening does the report proceed to describe the design, instruments, and purpose of the telescope.

## Source Reliability Screening

### The Hierarchy of Evidence in the Supplied Material

The supplied material contains two categories of source. The first is a NASA mission page, retrieved 14 September 2026, which is a public-domain government source and therefore carries the highest evidentiary weight available within this document set ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). The second is the Wikipedia article "James Webb Space Telescope," retrieved 14 September 2026 under a CC BY-SA 4.0 licence, which is a tertiary source but which here provides quantitative specifics — mirror diameter, collecting area, launch vehicle, wavelength range, and the names of the four instruments — that the NASA page does not ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)).

The third document, `error_material.txt`, is untitled in terms of publication venue, carries no author, no date, no URL, and no retrieval information ([error_material.txt, n.d.](error_material.txt)). More importantly, its content is factually incompatible with the two other sources. Under any standard hierarchy of evidence, an unattributed document whose claims contradict a primary government source and an encyclopaedic secondary source must yield.

The pattern in Table 1 is not one of minor imprecision; the erroneous document describes a completely different class of observatory. A 2.4-metre ultraviolet-and-optical telescope in low Earth orbit with a single ultraviolet spectrograph is, in effect, a description of a Hubble-class mission ([error_material.txt, n.d.](error_material.txt)). The authoritative sources describe a 6.5-metre infrared observatory stationed 1.5 million kilometres from Earth with four instruments ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope); [NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). The remainder of this report therefore relies exclusively on the NASA and Wikipedia descriptions.

## Design of the Observatory

### Primary Mirror and Optical Assembly

The defining structural feature of JWST is its large segmented primary mirror. Wikipedia records a diameter of 6.5 metres, or 21 feet, with a collecting area of 25.4 m² ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). The surface is gold-coated beryllium, and NASA independently describes the mirror as "segmented" and "gold-coated" ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope); [NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)).

Three design consequences follow directly from those specifications. First, the collecting area of 25.4 m² is roughly an order of magnitude larger than that of a 2.4-metre-class mirror, which is why the erroneous document's figure of 2.4 metres is not merely a numerical slip but a fundamentally different instrument class ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope); [error_material.txt, n.d.](error_material.txt)). Second, the segmentation of the mirror is a departure from the monolithic mirror architecture; the claim that JWST "reuses the Hubble Space Telescope's mirror design" is therefore unsupported ([error_material.txt, n.d.](error_material.txt)). Third, the gold coating is consistent with the infrared observing regime described by both authoritative sources, since the observatory is characterised by NASA as using its gold-coated mirror "to observe in infrared" ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)).

### Orbit and Operating Environment

JWST "orbits the Sun 1.5 million kilometers ... away from the Earth at ... the second Lagrange point or L2" ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). This is a heliocentric halo-type location rather than an Earth-orbiting one, and it places the observatory roughly four times farther from Earth than the Moon. The claim in the erroneous document that JWST operates in low Earth orbit is incompatible with this figure and with the associated claim of periodic servicing, since no reliable source in the supplied material mentions servicing at all ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/); [error_material.txt, n.d.](error_material.txt)).

The choice of L2 matters for the observatory's function. A location 1.5 million kilometres from Earth, on the anti-Sun side, allows a single-sided sunshield architecture to shade the telescope consistently while the spacecraft orbits the Sun in step with Earth ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). The supplied sources state the orbital fact and the infrared observing regime but do not elaborate the thermal engineering, so this report confines itself to the documented orbital parameters.

### Launch and Deployment

The launch is documented with unusual precision across both authoritative sources. NASA states that "Webb launched on Dec. 25th 2021" ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)), and Wikipedia specifies that "The Webb was launched on 25 December 2021 on an Ariane 5 rocket from Kourou, French Guiana" ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). The convergence of a government primary source and a tertiary source on both date and vehicle provides strong corroboration. The erroneous document's assertion of a 2019 launch is unsupported by either ([error_material.txt, n.d.](error_material.txt)).

## Scientific Instrument Suite

### The Four Instruments

Wikipedia states that JWST carries four science instruments: NIRCam, NIRSpec, MIRI, and FGS/NIRISS ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). This directly contradicts the erroneous document's claim that the observatory's "primary science instrument is the Wide Ultraviolet Spectrograph (WUVS)" ([error_material.txt, n.d.](error_material.txt)).

The supplied sources identify the instruments by acronym only and do not describe their individual capabilities, modes, or wavelength channels. Table 2 therefore lists only what the sources support, with the acronym expansions offered as transparent inferences drawn from standard naming conventions and clearly marked as such rather than as sourced facts.

The naming pattern itself carries evidentiary weight. Three of the four instrument designations contain "NIR" — near-infrared — and the fourth begins with "MIR," mid-infrared ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). This is consistent with, and independent of, the stated 0.6–28.5 µm observing range, and it is inconsistent with the erroneous document's claim of an ultraviolet spectrograph as the primary instrument ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope); [error_material.txt, n.d.](error_material.txt)). However, because the supplied sources do not enumerate instrument functions, this report does not assign specific science tasks to specific instruments.

## Wavelength Coverage and Observing Regime

The observatory "observes a lower frequency range, from long-wavelength visible light (red) through mid-infrared (0.6-28.5 um)" ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). NASA's formulation is compatible: the telescope uses a segmented, gold-coated mirror "to observe in infrared" ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)).

The 0.6–28.5 µm band spans more than a factor of forty in wavelength. It begins at the red end of the visible spectrum and extends deep into the mid-infrared, meaning that JWST is not a general-purpose optical telescope with an infrared add-on, nor is it an ultraviolet observatory ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). The erroneous document's characterisation of JWST as "a 2.4-meter ultraviolet-and-optical observatory" whose instrument is "designed to capture high-resolution ultraviolet spectra of nearby stars" is contradicted on regime, aperture, and instrument simultaneously ([error_material.txt, n.d.](error_material.txt); [Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)).

## Scientific Purpose and Design Rationale

The supplied sources do not include a formal statement of JWST's science objectives, and this report does not invent one. What can be established from the material is the design logic that the sources describe.

First, the observatory is purpose-built for infrared astronomy: it observes from long-wavelength red light through the mid-infrared, and its mirror is gold-coated and described by NASA specifically in connection with infrared observation ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope); [NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). Second, its aperture is large — 6.5 metres with 25.4 m² of collecting area — which is the design response of an observatory intended to gather faint infrared signal ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). Third, its station at L2, 1.5 million kilometres from Earth, removes it from the thermal and brightening environment of low Earth orbit ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)).

Taken together, these three design decisions are coherent with a single purpose: sensitive infrared observation from a cold, stable, remote vantage point. That is the only purpose the supplied evidence directly supports. The claim that JWST is "often described as a direct successor that reuses the Hubble Space Telescope's mirror design and orbital approach" is not supported by NASA or Wikipedia, and on the specific points of mirror diameter, mirror architecture, wavelength regime, and orbit it is directly refuted ([error_material.txt, n.d.](error_material.txt); [Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope); [NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)).

## Discussion

The central analytical finding of this report is that the supplied document set is not merely incomplete but internally contradictory, and that the contradiction can be resolved decisively. Two independent sources — one a government agency page and one a tertiary encyclopaedia entry — agree on the launch date, the launch vehicle, the mirror diameter, the mirror material, the collecting area, the wavelength range, and the instrument roster ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/); [Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). The third document agrees with neither on any of these points ([error_material.txt, n.d.](error_material.txt)).

In my assessment, the appropriate treatment is unambiguous: the erroneous document should be excluded from the evidence base entirely, not qualified, not footnoted as a dissenting view, and not averaged against the reliable sources. A claim that JWST was launched in 2019 into low Earth orbit is not a matter of interpretive disagreement; it is a falsifiable assertion that fails against both corroborating sources. Where a document makes eight distinct falsifiable claims and all eight are contradicted by higher-quality evidence, the reasonable inference is that the document does not describe the object it purports to describe.

There is also a secondary observation worth recording. The erroneous description is not random noise; it describes a plausible alternative mission — a Hubble-like ultraviolet telescope. That resemblance is precisely why such material is hazardous in a research context. A reader who encountered only `error_material.txt` would come away with a coherent but entirely incorrect mental model of the observatory ([error_material.txt, n.d.](error_material.txt)). This is a reminder that corroboration across independent sources is not a formality but the mechanism by which errors of this kind are caught.

Finally, one limitation should be acknowledged honestly. The reliable sources in this set establish JWST's physical parameters, orbit, launch, wavelength band, and instrument names, but they do not detail the science cases each instrument was built to pursue ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/); [Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). This report has therefore confined itself to what the sources support and has flagged its acronym expansions as inferences rather than facts. A fuller account of purpose would require additional authoritative documentation beyond the material provided.

## Conclusion

The James Webb Space Telescope, as documented by NASA and Wikipedia, is a 6.5-metre gold-coated beryllium segmented-mirror observatory with 25.4 m² of collecting area, launched on 25 December 2021 aboard an Ariane 5 from Kourou, French Guiana, and stationed 1.5 million kilometres from Earth at the second Sun–Earth Lagrange point ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/); [Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). It observes from long-wavelength visible red light through the mid-infrared, 0.6–28.5 µm, using four instruments: NIRCam, NIRSpec, MIRI, and FGS/NIRISS ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). Its design is organised around infrared sensitivity from a cold, remote, stable orbit — not around ultraviolet spectroscopy from low Earth orbit, and not around a reused Hubble mirror architecture ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/); [error_material.txt, n.d.](error_material.txt)).
