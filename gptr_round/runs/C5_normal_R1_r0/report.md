## Introduction and Scope

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) are a drug class that mimics the action of the endogenous incretin hormone GLP-1. Since the approval of exenatide in 2005, the class has expanded from a second-line glucose-lowering option in type 2 diabetes (T2D) into a therapeutic platform with approved indications spanning chronic weight management, cardiovascular risk reduction, chronic kidney disease (CKD), metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH), and obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) ([U.S. Food and Drug Administration [FDA], 2021](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-new-drug-treatment-chronic-weight-management-first-2014); [FDA, 2024](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-treatment-reduce-risk-serious-heart-problems-specifically-adults-obesity-or)). This report reviews the physiology underpinning the class, the pharmacological mechanism, pharmacokinetic differentiation, approved indications with supporting outcome data, safety considerations, and clinical positioning.

## The Endogenous Incretin System

### The incretin effect

When glucose is administered orally rather than intravenously, the insulin response is substantially greater—historically described as the “incretin effect,” accounting for roughly 50–70% of postprandial insulin secretion. GLP-1 is secreted by enteroendocrine L-cells located predominantly in the distal ileum and colon in response to nutrient delivery ([American Diabetes Association Professional Practice Committee [ADA], 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care)).

### Physiological actions of native GLP-1

Native GLP-1 exerts four canonical actions: (1) glucose-dependent potentiation of insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells; (2) glucose-dependent suppression of glucagon secretion from α-cells; (3) slowing of gastric emptying; and (4) central reduction of appetite and food intake. Because insulinotropic activity is glucose-dependent, GLP-1 does not ordinarily cause hypoglycaemia when used as monotherapy ([ADA, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care)).

### The limitation of native GLP-1

Native GLP-1 has a plasma half-life of approximately 1.5–2 minutes, being rapidly cleaved by dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) and cleared renally. This pharmacokinetic fragility is the central engineering problem that GLP-1 RA development has solved, either through DPP-4-resistant amino acid substitutions, fatty-acid acylation promoting albumin binding, Fc-fusion technology, or absorption-enhancing co-formulations ([ADA, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care)).

## Mechanism of Action of GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

### Receptor pharmacology

The GLP-1 receptor (GLP-1R) is a class B G-protein-coupled receptor expressed on pancreatic islets, the gastrointestinal tract, the kidney, the heart, and multiple brain regions, including the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus, area postrema, and nucleus tractus solitarius. Agonist binding activates Gαs, elevating intracellular cyclic AMP and activating protein kinase A and EPAC2 signalling. In β-cells this closes ATP-sensitive potassium channels, depolarises the membrane, opens voltage-gated calcium channels, and triggers calcium-dependent exocytosis of insulin granules ([ADA, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care)).

### Pancreatic actions

GLP-1 RAs increase glucose-stimulated insulin secretion and suppress glucagon release in a glucose-dependent manner. The net effect is improved fasting and postprandial glycaemia with a low intrinsic risk of hypoglycaemia. Long-acting agents achieve HbA1c reductions of roughly 1.0–1.8% depending on agent, dose, and baseline HbA1c, with semaglutide 1 mg and tirzepatide 15 mg at the upper end of this range ([ADA, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care)).

### Gastrointestinal and central actions

Slowing of gastric emptying contributes to reduced postprandial glucose excursions; this effect is more pronounced with short-acting agents such as exenatide twice daily and lixisenatide, and attenuates with continuous long-acting exposure (tachyphylaxis). Centrally, GLP-1 RAs act on hypothalamic and hindbrain circuits to reduce hunger and food reward, producing the clinically dominant weight-loss effect. The weight loss observed with semaglutide 2.4 mg and tirzepatide is therefore best understood as a centrally mediated reduction in energy intake rather than a metabolic rate effect ([Wilding et al., 2021](https://www.nejm.org); [Jastreboff et al., 2022](https://www.nejm.org)).

### Extra-pancreatic and cardiometabolic effects

Proposed cardioprotective mechanisms include reduced inflammation, improved endothelial function, modest blood-pressure and lipid improvements, and attenuated platelet activation. Renal effects may include natriuresis and reduced oxidative and inflammatory injury. Importantly, these mechanistic hypotheses are supported chiefly by outcome-trial results rather than by established mediation analyses, and the precise contributors to cardiovascular benefit remain incompletely defined ([Lincoff et al., 2023](https://www.nejm.org); [Perkovic et al., 2024](https://www.nejm.org)).

## Classification and Pharmacokinetics

| Agent | Class | Typical dosing | Approximate half-life | Primary approved roles |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exenatide BID | Short-acting | 5–10 mcg twice daily | ~2.4 h | T2D |
| Lixisenatide | Short-acting | 10–20 mcg daily | ~3 h | T2D |
| Liraglutide | Long-acting (daily) | 0.6–1.8 mg (T2D); up to 3.0 mg (weight) | ~13 h | T2D; chronic weight management |
| Semaglutide SC | Long-acting (weekly) | 0.25–2.0 mg (T2D); up to 2.4 mg (weight) | ~165 h | T2D; weight; CV risk; CKD; MASH |
| Semaglutide oral | Long-acting (daily) | 3–14 mg | ~165 h | T2D; CV risk reduction |
| Dulaglutide | Long-acting (weekly) | 0.75–4.5 mg | ~5 days | T2D; CV risk reduction |
| Tirzepatide (GIP/GLP-1 dual agonist) | Dual incretin | 2.5–15 mg weekly | ~5 days | T2D; weight; OSA |

Long-acting agents are generally preferred when cardiovascular or weight benefit is a therapeutic goal, whereas short-acting agents produce a more pronounced gastric-emptying effect ([ADA, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care)).

## Approved Indications

### Type 2 diabetes

All agents in the class are approved as adjuncts to diet and exercise for glycaemic control in adults with T2D. Professional guidance now positions GLP-1 RAs with demonstrated cardiovascular benefit as preferred therapy in patients with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, heart failure, or CKD, independent of baseline HbA1c or metformin use ([ADA, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care)).

### Chronic weight management

Semaglutide 2.4 mg (Wegovy) received approval in June 2021 for chronic weight management in adults with obesity or overweight plus at least one weight-related comorbidity ([FDA, 2021](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-new-drug-treatment-chronic-weight-management-first-2014)). Liraglutide 3.0 mg (Saxenda) preceded it, and tirzepatide (Zepbound) was approved in November 2023. In STEP 1, semaglutide 2.4 mg produced mean weight loss of 14.9% versus 2.4% with placebo at 68 weeks ([Wilding et al., 2021](https://www.nejm.org)). In SURMOUNT-1, tirzepatide 15 mg produced 20.9% mean weight loss at 72 weeks ([Jastreboff et al., 2022](https://www.nejm.org)). In the head-to-head SURMOUNT-5 trial, tirzepatide produced greater weight loss than semaglutide 2.4 mg (approximately 20.2% vs 13.7% at 72 weeks), although both were highly effective ([Jastreboff et al., 2025](https://www.nejm.org)).

### Cardiovascular risk reduction

Cardiovascular outcome trials have established class-level and agent-specific benefits. Liraglutide reduced major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) by 13% (HR 0.87; 95% CI 0.78–0.97) in LEADER ([Marso et al., 2016a](https://www.nejm.org)); semaglutide 1.0 mg reduced MACE by 26% (HR 0.74; 95% CI 0.58–0.95) in SUSTAIN-6 ([Marso et al., 2016b](https://www.nejm.org)); and dulaglutide reduced MACE by 12% (HR 0.88; 95% CI 0.79–0.99) in REWIND ([Gerstein et al., 2019](https://www.thelancet.com)). In SELECT, semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced MACE by 20% (HR 0.80; 95% CI 0.72–0.90) in adults with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease without diabetes, supporting a dedicated approved indication ([Lincoff et al., 2023](https://www.nejm.org); [FDA, 2024](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-treatment-reduce-risk-serious-heart-problems-specifically-adults-obesity-or)). Oral semaglutide 14 mg reduced MACE by 14% (HR 0.86; 95% CI 0.77–0.96) in the SOUL trial reported in 2025 ([ADA, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care)).

### Chronic kidney disease

In the FLOW trial, semaglutide 1.0 mg reduced the composite kidney endpoint (kidney failure, ≥50% eGFR decline, or kidney- or cardiovascular-related death) by 24% (HR 0.76; 95% CI 0.66–0.88) in patients with T2D and CKD, forming the basis for a regulatory label expansion in 2025 ([Perkovic et al., 2024](https://www.nejm.org)).

### Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis

In 2025, semaglutide 2.4 mg received accelerated approval for non-cirrhotic MASH with moderate-to-advanced fibrosis (F2–F3), based on the ESSENCE trial, in which 62.9% of semaglutide-treated participants achieved resolution of steatohepatitis versus 34.3% with placebo, with fibrosis improvement in 36.7% versus 22.4% ([Sanyal et al., 2025](https://www.nejm.org)). Confirmatory outcome data remain required for full approval.

### Obstructive sleep apnoea

Tirzepatide was approved in December 2024 for moderate-to-severe OSA in adults with obesity, to be used alongside a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity—the first pharmacological approval for this indication ([FDA, 2024](https://www.fda.gov)).

## Safety, Tolerability, and Contraindications

Gastrointestinal adverse events dominate the tolerability profile: nausea (approximately 20–44%), vomiting, diarrhoea, constipation, and abdominal discomfort. These are typically transient and dose-related, mitigated by slow titration. Discontinuation due to adverse events in pivotal weight-management trials generally ranged from roughly 3–7% ([Wilding et al., 2021](https://www.nejm.org); [Jastreboff et al., 2022](https://www.nejm.org)).

A boxed warning regarding thyroid C-cell tumours observed in rodents applies to the class; GLP-1 RAs are contraindicated in patients with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2. Other recognised risks include acute pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, and—with semaglutide—early worsening of diabetic retinopathy in patients with baseline retinopathy. GLP-1 RAs should not be used in type 1 diabetes or for diabetic ketoacidosis. Because they delay gastric emptying, they may alter absorption of concomitantly administered oral drugs and have been associated with retained gastric contents and pulmonary aspiration risk under anaesthesia; perioperative guidance recommends disclosure of GLP-1 RA use to the anaesthesia team. When combined with insulin or sulfonylureas, hypoglycaemia risk increases and dose adjustment of the companion agent is required. GLP-1 RAs should be discontinued when pregnancy is recognised, and weight-management agents are generally advised to be stopped in advance of a planned pregnancy ([ADA, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care)). Following systematic review, the European Medicines Agency and FDA concluded that available evidence did not support a causal association between GLP-1 RAs and suicidal ideation ([European Medicines Agency, 2024](https://www.ema.europa.eu)).

## Clinical Positioning, Access, and Cost

Three practical tensions shape current use. First, cost and access: United States list prices historically exceeded approximately $900–$1,300 per month, creating substantial inequity; cash-pay programmes and 2025 policy agreements have begun to lower some prices, and Medicare/Medicaid coverage pathways have expanded. Second, supply: shortages of semaglutide and tirzepatide drove a market in compounded products, and regulators have warned against unapproved compounded versions. Third, durability: discontinuation is frequently followed by weight regain, so the class behaves more like chronic therapy than a time-limited intervention ([ADA, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care)).

## Emerging Directions

Next-generation agents include the GIP/GLP-1/glucagon triple agonist retatrutide, the amylin–GLP-1 combination cagrilintide–semaglutide, and orally available non-peptide GLP-1 RAs such as orforglipron, which reported roughly 12% weight loss at 72 weeks in a phase 3 trial. These agents may deliver greater efficacy, oral convenience, and lower manufacturing cost, but long-term outcome and safety data remain immature ([ADA, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care)).

## Author's Assessment

The evidence supports a clear and defensible position: GLP-1 RAs constitute the most consequential therapeutic advance in cardiometabolic medicine since statins, because they are the first class to simultaneously improve glycaemia, body weight, and hard cardiovascular and renal outcomes within a single mechanism. However, that conclusion must be qualified in three ways. First, benefit magnitude is agent- and dose-specific rather than a uniform class property, which argues against interchangeable prescribing. Second, tolerability, cost, gastrointestinal burden, and loss of lean mass mean that efficacy in trials does not automatically translate into effectiveness in practice, particularly where adherence is weak. Third, several approvals—notably for MASH—rest on surrogate endpoints and accelerated pathways, and long-term event data are still pending. The rational clinical stance is therefore enthusiastic but disciplined: preferential use of agents with proven outcome benefit in patients who match trial populations, explicit counselling about chronicity of therapy and gastrointestinal effects, and routine monitoring for pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, and retinopathy in at-risk individuals.

## Conclusion

GLP-1 RAs amplify a native incretin signal through a DPP-4-resistant, long-acting pharmacology that couples glucose-dependent insulinotropic action with centrally mediated appetite suppression. This dual mechanism has produced an unusually broad approved indication set covering T2D, chronic weight management, cardiovascular risk reduction, CKD, MASH, and OSA, supported by large outcome trials including LEADER, SUSTAIN-6, REWIND, SELECT, FLOW, STEP 1, SURMOUNT-1, and ESSENCE. Their principal limitations are gastrointestinal intolerance, cost and access barriers, contraindication in medullary thyroid carcinoma and MEN2 kindreds, and the requirement for sustained use. As oral and multi-agonist formulations mature, the central questions shift from efficacy to equitable access, long-term safety, and the preservation of lean mass and functional health.
