## Introduction

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) constitute a therapeutic class that has expanded from a niche injectable option for type 2 diabetes into one of the most consequential drug families in modern medicine. First introduced with exenatide in 2005, the class now spans agents administered daily, weekly, and orally, and its approved indications extend to chronic weight management, cardiovascular event reduction, kidney protection, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) ([American Diabetes Association, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/48/Supplement_1/S1/157557)). The trajectory of the class reflects a broader reconceptualisation of GLP-1 signalling as a systemic metabolic and anti-inflammatory axis rather than a purely glucose-lowering pathway.

## Native GLP-1 Physiology: The Rationale for Pharmacological Mimicry

GLP-1 is an incretin hormone produced by enteroendocrine L-cells of the distal small intestine and colon in response to nutrient ingestion. Its physiological actions include potentiation of glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, suppression of glucagon release, slowing of gastric emptying, and reduction of appetite via hypothalamic and hindbrain signalling ([American Diabetes Association, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/48/Supplement_1/S1/157557)).

Two pharmacological problems limit the therapeutic utility of native GLP-1. First, circulating GLP-1 has a half-life of roughly one to two minutes because it is rapidly cleaved by dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) and neutral endopeptidases. Second, supraphysiological receptor stimulation appears necessary to achieve meaningful weight reduction, since physiological postprandial GLP-1 concentrations are far below those required for substantial anorectic effect. The entire class is therefore engineered around two objectives: resistance to DPP-4 degradation and sufficiently high receptor occupancy at the target tissues.

## Mechanism of Action

### Pancreatic Effects

GLP-1 RAs bind the class B G protein-coupled GLP-1 receptor on pancreatic beta cells, activating adenylyl cyclase and raising intracellular cyclic AMP. Downstream effects include enhanced glucose-dependent insulin secretion, upregulation of insulin gene transcription, and beta-cell proliferative and anti-apoptotic signalling in preclinical models. Critically, the insulinotropic effect is glucose-dependent: insulin secretion increases under hyperglycaemic conditions but diminishes as glucose normalises, which explains the low intrinsic hypoglycaemia risk of the class when used as monotherapy ([American Diabetes Association, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/48/Supplement_1/S1/157557)). Alpha-cell glucagon secretion is simultaneously suppressed in a glucose-dependent manner, reducing hepatic glucose output.

### Extrapancreatic Effects

Beyond the pancreas, GLP-1 RAs act on the gastrointestinal tract to slow gastric emptying and reduce antral motility, contributing to postprandial glucose flattening and early satiety. Within the central nervous system, receptors in the arcuate nucleus, nucleus of the solitary tract, and area postrema mediate reduced food intake and altered reward signalling. This central component is now understood to be the dominant driver of weight loss rather than delayed gastric emptying alone ([American Diabetes Association, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/48/Supplement_1/S1/157557)).

Additional observed effects include reduced hepatic lipogenesis and steatosis, lowered systemic inflammation, and modulation of natriuresis — mechanisms invoked to explain the cardiovascular and renal benefits observed in outcome trials.

### Structural Classes and Signalling Behaviour

Two structural lineages dominate the class:

1. **Exendin-4–derived agents** (exenatide, lixisenatide) share approximately 53% sequence homology with human GLP-1 and resist DPP-4 cleavage by virtue of a glycine substitution at position 2.
2. **Human GLP-1 backbone analogues** (liraglutide, semaglutide, dulaglutide) are engineered through fatty acid acylation, albumin binding, or Fc fusion to extend half-life while retaining higher homology to the native peptide.

An important pharmacological distinction is that exendin-4–derived agonists tend to promote receptor internalisation and exhibit "biased" signalling favouring cAMP over beta-arrestin recruitment, whereas human GLP-1 analogues display more balanced signalling. The clinical consequences of this distinction remain debated, but it is frequently cited as a potential explanation for differences in gastrointestinal tolerability and tachyphylaxis of gastric emptying effects between agents.

Acylated analogues such as semaglutide achieve their prolonged half-life through high-affinity albumin binding, which both shields the peptide from DPP-4 and delays renal clearance. Absorption-enhancing technology (sodium N-[8-(2-hydroxybenzoyl) amino] caprylate) permits oral semaglutide delivery, though bioavailability remains low and administration must occur fasting with limited water.

## Approved Clinical Indications

### Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus

All agents in the class carry approvals as adjuncts to diet and exercise for glycaemic control in adults with type 2 diabetes, with several also approved for paediatric patients aged 10 years and older (liraglutide, exenatide ER, dulaglutide). In the ADA/EASD framework, GLP-1 RAs are positioned as preferred injectable agents ahead of basal insulin in most patients without severe hyperglycaemia, largely because of weight benefit and lower hypoglycaemia risk ([American Diabetes Association, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/48/Supplement_1/S1/157557)).

Semaglutide 1.0 mg achieved HbA1c reductions of approximately 1.5–1.8 percentage points in the SUSTAIN programme, with weight losses of 4–6 kg, and head-to-head data indicate superiority over dulaglutide 1.5 mg for both endpoints ([Ahmann et al., 2018](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/41/12/2586/36257)). A meta-analysis of 453 trials encompassing more than 270,000 participants found that GLP-1 RAs reduce HbA1c by a class-average of roughly 1.0 percentage point and body weight by approximately 2–5 kg relative to placebo ([Palmer et al., 2021](https://www.bmj.com/content/372/bmj.m4573)).

### Chronic Weight Management

Liraglutide 3.0 mg and semaglutide 2.4 mg are approved for chronic weight management in adults with obesity or overweight plus at least one weight-related comorbidity. In STEP 1, semaglutide 2.4 mg produced a mean weight reduction of 14.9% versus 2.4% with placebo over 68 weeks, with 86.4% of participants achieving ≥5% loss and 50.5% achieving ≥15% ([Wilding et al., 2021](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2032183)).

The comparative efficacy hierarchy within the incretin space was demonstrated in SURMOUNT-1, in which the dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist tirzepatide produced up to 20.9% weight reduction at 15 mg over 72 weeks, exceeding semaglutide's STEP-era results in a cross-trial comparison ([Jastreboff et al., 2022](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2206038)).

### Cardiovascular Risk Reduction

This indication represents the most significant evolution of the class. Liraglutide (LEADER), semaglutide subcutaneously (SUSTAIN-6), dulaglutide (REWIND), and oral semaglutide (PIONEER 6) all demonstrated non-inferiority or superiority for major adverse cardiovascular events in type 2 diabetes populations ([Marso et al., 2016a](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1603827); [Marso et al., 2016b](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1607141); [Husain et al., 2019](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(19)31149-3/fulltext)).

The pivotal expansion came with SELECT, which enrolled 17,604 adults with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease but *without* diabetes. Semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced the primary composite endpoint by 20% (hazard ratio 0.80; 95% CI 0.72–0.90) over a mean follow-up of 39.8 months ([Lincoff et al., 2023](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2307563)). This was the first demonstration that incretin-based therapy confers cardiovascular protection independently of glycaemic status.

### Chronic Kidney Disease

The FLOW trial randomised 3,533 participants with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease to semaglutide 1.0 mg or placebo. The trial was stopped early for efficacy after a 24% reduction in the composite primary kidney endpoint (hazard ratio 0.76; 95% CI 0.66–0.88), leading to a dedicated regulatory indication for reducing kidney disease progression in this population ([Perkovic et al., 2024](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2403347)).

### Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatohepatitis

In August 2025, the FDA granted accelerated approval to semaglutide 2.4 mg for non-cirrhotic MASH with moderate-to-advanced fibrosis (F2–F3), based on the ESSENCE trial, in which a significantly greater proportion of patients achieved resolution of steatohepatitis and improvement in fibrosis compared with placebo. This is the first GLP-1 RA approved for a liver indication and represents a meaningful expansion of the class into hepatology.

### Obstructive Sleep Apnoea

Tirzepatide received approval for moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnoea in adults with obesity, based on reductions in apnoea-hypopnoea index. While not a selective GLP-1 RA, this represents a class-adjacent expansion of incretin therapeutics into respiratory medicine.

## Safety Profile and Tolerability

### Gastrointestinal Adverse Events

Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, and constipation are the dominant adverse events, reported in 20–45% of treated participants across the pivotal trials, with nausea being most common ([Wilding et al., 2021](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2032183)). Events are typically transient, dose-dependent, and mitigated by slow titration. Gastrointestinal effects are the leading cause of discontinuation in real-world settings, where discontinuation rates exceed those observed in controlled trials.

### Pancreatitis and Gallbladder Disease

Acute pancreatitis is a labelled warning across the class, though causal attribution remains uncertain given confounding by hypertriglyceridaemia, gallstones, and obesity. Cholelithiasis and cholecystitis risk is modestly elevated, plausibly through gallbladder hypomotility and rapid weight loss.

### Thyroid C-cell Tumours

Rodent studies demonstrated dose-dependent thyroid C-cell hyperplasia and medullary carcinoma with liraglutide and semaglutide. These agents are contraindicated in patients with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2. Human epidemiological data to date have not confirmed a causal increase, but the boxed warning remains in place.

### Hypoglycaemia

Hypoglycaemia risk is low in monotherapy but rises substantially when GLP-1 RAs are combined with insulin or sulfonylureas, necessitating pre-emptive dose reduction of those agents.

### Diabetic Retinopathy

In SUSTAIN-6, semaglutide was associated with an increased rate of diabetic retinopathy complications (3.0% vs 1.8% with placebo), an effect attributed in part to rapid HbA1c reduction rather than a direct retinopathic mechanism ([Marso et al., 2016b](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1607141)).

### Delayed Gastric Emptying and Perioperative Considerations

Because gastric emptying is slowed, retained gastric contents have been reported during anaesthesia induction in patients on GLP-1 RAs. Multisociety guidance recommends informing the anaesthesia team, considering a liquid diet for 24 hours before elective procedures, and holding the drug where feasible.

### Contraindications

Absolute contraindications include personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma and MEN2, prior serious hypersensitivity to the agent, and (for some labels) pregnancy. The class is not recommended as first-line therapy in patients with a history of pancreatitis or severe gastroparesis.

## Therapeutic Positioning and Practical Considerations

Current guidance positions GLP-1 RAs as preferred agents in patients with type 2 diabetes and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, or obesity, independent of baseline HbA1c ([American Diabetes Association, 2025](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/48/Supplement_1/S1/157557)). The presence of a dual-indication agent such as semaglutide allows a single medication to address glycaemia, weight, cardiovascular risk, and kidney risk simultaneously, which has driven its rapid adoption.

Key practical considerations include:

- **Titration**: gradual escalation over 4–16 weeks substantially improves tolerability.
- **Cost and access**: list prices in the United States exceed $900–$1,300 per month for brand-name products, driving widespread use of compounded semaglutide. Regulators have cautioned that compounded versions are not approved and may contain impurities or incorrect concentrations.
- **Discontinuation**: weight regain of approximately two-thirds of lost weight within one year of stopping has been consistently observed, framing obesity as a chronic, treatment-dependent condition.
- **Muscle mass**: lean mass loss constitutes 20–40% of total weight lost, prompting recommendations for resistance training and adequate protein intake.

## Emerging Developments

The pipeline points toward multi-agonist pharmacology. Cagrilintide–semaglutide (CagriSema) combines amylin and GLP-1 receptor agonism and produced approximately 22.7% weight reduction in the REDEFINE-1 programme. Oral orforglipron, a non-peptide small-molecule GLP-1 RA, achieved roughly 12–14% weight reduction in Phase 3 ATTAIN data and offers the prospect of orally administered therapy without fasting restrictions. Retatrutide, a triple GIP/GLP-1/glucagon agonist, has reported weight reductions exceeding 24% in Phase 2. These agents are expected to intensify competition and expand indications further into MASH, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, and neurodegenerative disease.

## Conclusion

GLP-1 receptor agonists have evolved from a modest glucose-lowering adjunct into a platform therapeutic class addressing obesity, cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, and steatohepatitis through a shared mechanism of incretin receptor activation. The mechanism is well characterised — glucose-dependent insulinotropism, glucagon suppression, slowed gastric emptying, and centrally mediated appetite reduction — and the clinical evidence base is unusually robust, anchored in large cardiovascular and kidney outcome trials. The principal limitations remain gastrointestinal tolerability, cost and access, and the requirement for indefinite therapy. Ongoing multi-agonist and oral small-molecule development suggests that the class will continue to broaden rather than converge.
