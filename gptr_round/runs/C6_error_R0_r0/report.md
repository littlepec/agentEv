# The Montreal Protocol: Regulated Substances and Measured Effects on the Stratospheric Ozone Layer

## Scope and Purpose of This Report

This report summarizes the Montreal Protocol, identifies precisely which substances it regulates, and assesses its documented effect on the stratospheric ozone layer. The analysis is built on two authoritative, publicly available sources: the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) Ozone Secretariat's treaty page and the United States Environmental Protection Agency's (EPA) overview of international treaties and cooperation for stratospheric ozone protection ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)); ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). A third document supplied for this exercise contains assertions that contradict those authoritative sources in every material particular; it is examined explicitly in the section on source reliability and is excluded from the factual basis of this report.

## Evaluation of Source Reliability

### Verified authoritative sources

The two sources selected for the substantive findings are of the highest available evidentiary quality for this subject matter. The UNEP Ozone Secretariat is the treaty's own administrative body and therefore the primary institutional custodian of the Protocol's text, mandate, and implementation record ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The US EPA is a national environmental regulatory agency whose summary draws on the same multilateral process and is published as public-domain government material ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Both were retrieved on 14 September 2026, making them current as of the date of this report. Neither source is a news article, opinion piece, or secondary aggregation; both are institutional statements of fact. Where the two sources agree — on the Protocol's ozone-protection purpose and on the trajectory of ozone recovery — the convergence strengthens confidence in the finding.

### The disputed document and its errors

The document labeled "error_material.txt" makes four mutually reinforcing claims: that the Montreal Protocol was adopted in 1997; that it primarily caps carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions from industrial sources; that it sets national CO₂ budgets rather than addressing manufactured chemicals; and that the ozone layer has continued to thin steadily since adoption with no measurable recovery, rendering its atmospheric impact "negligible to date" (Untitled document [erroneous source material], n.d.). Each claim is contradicted by the authoritative sources. The EPA states that the original Montreal Protocol agreement dates to 1987, not 1997 ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). UNEP describes the Protocol as an agreement to protect the ozone layer "by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it," not as a CO₂-capping instrument ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). And UNEP states that "the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery," which directly negates the claim of continued thinning and negligible impact ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Because the disputed document is unattributed, undated, and unreferenced, and because its central factual propositions fail against both a treaty-body source and a national regulatory agency, it carries no weight in this assessment. Its inclusion is useful only as a cautionary illustration of how confidently stated misinformation can misdirect environmental policy analysis.

## What the Montreal Protocol Regulates

### Ozone-depleting substances and the production and consumption phase-out

The Montreal Protocol is, by its own definition, "a global agreement to protect the Earth's ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Its regulatory target is therefore a defined class of manufactured industrial chemicals — ozone-depleting substances (ODS) — rather than a naturally occurring or universally emitted greenhouse gas. The Protocol does not merely encourage reductions; it establishes a phase-out plan, and that plan is explicitly bilateral in scope, covering "both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This dual coverage is significant: regulating consumption alone would leave production capacity intact and permit leakage into unregulated markets, whereas regulating production alone would leave stockpiles and imported material in circulation. The Protocol's architecture addresses the full chemical lifecycle of controlled substances at the point of manufacture and the point of use.

Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) are the best-documented chemical class governed by the original instrument. Under the original Montreal Protocol agreement of 1987, developed countries were required to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993 ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The sequencing is notable: adoption in 1987, followed by a mandated start to phase-out in developed economies roughly six years later. This reflects the Protocol's design as a framework convention with binding, scheduled obligations rather than a purely aspirational declaration, and it also reflects the principle that industrialized parties with the greatest historical emissions and the greatest technical capacity for substitution would move first.

### The Kigali Amendment: hydrofluorocarbons

The Protocol's regulatory reach has been extended beyond the chemicals originally targeted. The Kigali Amendment of 2016 extended controls to phase down the production and consumption of hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). HFCs are not themselves ozone-depleting substances in the classic sense; they became prevalent largely as substitutes for CFCs and other ODS in refrigeration, air conditioning, and foam applications. The Kigali Amendment's significance is therefore twofold. First, it expands the Protocol's substance coverage by a distinct chemical family. Second, it demonstrates that the treaty functions as a living instrument, capable of being amended to capture chemicals that emerge as consequences of earlier compliance decisions. The same production-and-consumption architecture used for the original ODS is applied to HFCs, preserving regulatory consistency across regimes ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

### What the Protocol does not regulate

Neither authoritative source describes the Montreal Protocol as a carbon dioxide treaty or as an instrument establishing national CO₂ budgets. The claim that it "set[s] national CO₂ budgets rather than addressing specific manufactured chemicals" is precisely inverted relative to the documented record: the Protocol addresses specific manufactured chemicals, and the sources provided make no reference to national CO₂ budgeting under its provisions (Untitled document [erroneous source material], n.d.); ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). While the Protocol's implementation has produced incidental climate benefits — because many ODS are also potent greenhouse gases, and because HFCs are controlled for their warming potential — that is an ancillary consequence of an ozone-focused mandate, not the mandate itself. Conflating the two misrepresents both the Protocol and the separate international climate regime, and it obscures the specific causal mechanism by which the treaty produces its documented atmospheric effects.

## Effects on the Ozone Layer

### Evidence of recovery

The documented effect of the Protocol on the ozone layer is positive and directional. UNEP states plainly that "the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The phrasing matters analytically. "Well on its way" denotes a recovery already underway — a trend, not merely a forecast — and it is attributable in the institutional framing of the source to the phase-out of the chemicals that deplete the ozone layer ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The causal chain is straightforward and mechanistically coherent: if the Protocol reduces the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances, and those substances are the cause of stratospheric ozone depletion, then reducing them should permit the ozone layer to recover. UNEP's statement is consistent with that mechanism.

### Projected full recovery

Recovery is not instantaneous, and the Protocol's benefits are realized over a multi-decade horizon. EPA states that "the ozone layer is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century" ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Two features of this projection deserve emphasis. First, the timescale is short in atmospheric terms — decades rather than centuries — which indicates that the reduction in ozone-depleting substances has been sufficient to alter the trajectory of the stratosphere. A treaty whose impact was "negligible," as the disputed document asserts, could not plausibly produce a full-healing expectation within the present century (Untitled document [erroneous source material], n.d.). Second, the expectation of full healing constitutes a falsifiable, forward-looking benchmark. It can be tested against future measurement, which makes it a genuine policy commitment rather than a rhetorical flourish.

## Analytical Discussion

On the evidence available, the Montreal Protocol is best characterized as a targeted chemical-control treaty with a demonstrated and continuing effect on stratospheric ozone. Its regulatory object is a defined set of manufactured substances, its regulatory method is the coordinated phase-out of their production and consumption, and its regulatory reach has been extended once already through the Kigali Amendment to cover HFCs ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)); ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The trajectory of the ozone layer under this regime is one of recovery, with full healing projected for around the middle of the twenty-first century ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

The principal caution arising from this review is evidential rather than scientific. The disputed document's claims are not marginal misreadings; they invert the treaty's date, its subject matter, its regulatory instrument, and its observed effect simultaneously. That pattern is characteristic of fabrication or severe corruption rather than of ordinary error, and it illustrates why source provenance must be evaluated before substance in any environmental assessment. A claim that a treaty of global significance has had "negligible" impact, offered without attribution or data, should be treated as unverified until checked against the treaty body responsible for it and against national regulators with implementation experience.

## Conclusion

The Montreal Protocol is a global agreement adopted in 1987 to protect the Earth's ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it, with a phase-out plan covering both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances and, since the 2016 Kigali Amendment, the production and consumption of hydrofluorocarbons as well ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)); ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). It is not a carbon dioxide treaty, its adoption did not occur in 1997, and its effect on the ozone layer is not negligible: the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery, with full healing expected near the middle of the twenty-first century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)); ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The credible evidence therefore supports a clear position: the Protocol regulates specific manufactured ozone-depleting chemicals through scheduled production and consumption phase-outs, and that regulation has placed stratospheric ozone on a measurable recovery pathway.
