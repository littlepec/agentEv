# The Montreal Protocol: Regulating Ozone-Depleting Substances and Its Effect on the Ozone Layer

## Introduction

The Montreal Protocol is best understood as an international environmental agreement designed to protect the Earth’s ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). It is not, according to the authoritative sources provided, a carbon dioxide (CO2) cap-and-budget treaty. The original agreement dates to 1987, when developed countries were required to begin phasing out chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) in 1993 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The Protocol’s ozone-layer effect is positive: the ozone layer is described as “well on its way to recovery,” and it is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

This report summarizes what the Montreal Protocol regulates and what its measured or projected effect on the ozone layer has been. It prioritizes the UNEP Ozone Secretariat and the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) because they are authoritative, directly relevant, and consistent with each other. A separate provided item, “The Montreal Protocol and the Atmosphere,” contains major factual inconsistencies—such as a 1997 adoption date, a claimed CO2 cap, and a claim of continued ozone thinning with no recovery—and is therefore not treated as a reliable basis for this assessment. The authoritative record instead supports a clear conclusion: the Montreal Protocol regulates ozone-depleting substances, not CO2 budgets, and its effect on the ozone layer is recovery, not negligible decline.

## What the Montreal Protocol Regulates

### Core Regulatory Target

The Montreal Protocol is “a global agreement to protect the Earth’s ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it” ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Its central regulatory purpose is therefore chemical-specific: it targets manufactured substances that damage stratospheric ozone. The UNEP Ozone Secretariat states that the phase-out plan “includes both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances” ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This means the treaty does not merely encourage reductions; it establishes controls across the lifecycle of regulated chemicals, including their production and use.

The original Montreal Protocol agreement was adopted in 1987. Under that original agreement, developed countries were required to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). CFCs are therefore one of the key early regulated chemical groups under the treaty framework. The EPA’s description confirms that the Protocol’s original focus was on phasing out ozone-depleting chemicals, beginning with CFCs in developed countries ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

### The Kigali Amendment and HFCs

The Protocol has also been extended over time. The Kigali Amendment (2016) “extended controls to phase down the production and consumption of hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs)” ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This amendment is significant because it broadens the scope of the Montreal Protocol framework beyond the original CFC phase-out. The Kigali Amendment does not change the treaty into a CO2 cap; rather, it adds HFCs to the list of substances subject to production and consumption controls ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

### What It Does Not Regulate

The provided authoritative sources do not describe the Montreal Protocol as an agreement to cap carbon dioxide emissions from industrial sources or to set national CO2 budgets. Instead, UNEP describes it as a phase-out agreement for ozone-depleting chemicals, and EPA describes it as originally requiring CFC phase-outs and later extending controls to HFCs ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The regulated objects are specific manufactured chemicals—CFCs and, through Kigali, HFCs—not national carbon budgets. This distinction is essential for accurately summarizing the treaty.

## Effect on the Ozone Layer

### Recovery Status

The most important finding from the UNEP Ozone Secretariat is that “the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery” ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This statement directly contradicts any claim that the ozone layer has continued to thin steadily with no measurable recovery attributable to the agreement. According to UNEP, the Protocol’s phase-out framework is associated with recovery progress, not continued decline ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

### Projected Full Healing

The EPA provides a longer-term projection: “the ozone layer is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century” ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This is a concrete temporal benchmark. It indicates that the treaty’s effect is not framed as negligible or absent, but as a gradual recovery process with a projected completion point around the mid-2000s to 2050s. The EPA’s statement therefore reinforces the UNEP assessment that the ozone layer is recovering.

### Why the Effect Is Linked to the Regulated Chemicals

The recovery expectation follows logically from the treaty’s regulatory design. If the Protocol phases out the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances, then the atmospheric input of those substances should decline over time ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The EPA’s description of the CFC phase-out beginning in 1993 for developed countries shows that the treaty established an early timeline for reducing a major class of ozone-depleting chemicals ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The Kigali Amendment later extended controls to HFCs, showing that the treaty framework can be updated to address additional controlled substances ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

## Contrasting Mischaracterizations and Source Reliability

A key task in summarizing the Montreal Protocol is distinguishing reliable treaty descriptions from inaccurate claims. The reliable sources state that the Protocol is a global agreement to protect the ozone layer by phasing out ozone-depleting chemicals, that its phase-out plan covers production and consumption, and that the ozone layer is recovering ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The EPA adds that the original agreement was from 1987, that developed countries were required to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993, and that the Kigali Amendment extended controls to HFCs in 2016 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

By contrast, the unreliable provided document claims that the Montreal Protocol was adopted in 1997, that it primarily caps CO2 emissions from industrial sources, that it sets national CO2 budgets, and that the ozone layer has continued to thin with no measurable recovery. None of these claims are supported by the UNEP Ozone Secretariat or the EPA. The UNEP source explicitly describes a phase-out of ozone-depleting chemicals, not CO2, and the EPA source explicitly dates the original agreement to 1987 and describes CFC and HFC controls ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The UNEP and EPA sources also describe ozone recovery and projected healing, not continued thinning ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

For these reasons, the unreliable document should not be used to draw conclusions about the Montreal Protocol’s purpose or atmospheric impact. The UNEP Ozone Secretariat is an authoritative UN-affiliated source specifically focused on the treaty, and the EPA is a public-domain US government source that describes the treaty’s legal timelines and amendments ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). When source reliability is prioritized, the evidence points overwhelmingly toward the conclusion that the Montreal Protocol is an ozone-protection treaty that regulates ozone-depleting substances and HFCs, not CO2.

## Assessment and Implications

In my assessment, the Montreal Protocol should be summarized as a targeted chemical phase-out treaty with a demonstrable and projected positive effect on the ozone layer. Its regulatory scope is defined by manufactured substances that deplete ozone: the original agreement required developed countries to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993, and the Kigali Amendment later extended controls to HFC production and consumption ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The UNEP Ozone Secretariat confirms that the treaty’s phase-out plan includes both production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This is a concrete, enforceable regulatory model focused on specific chemicals.

The effect on the ozone layer is equally clear from the authoritative sources. UNEP states that the ozone layer is “well on its way to recovery,” and EPA projects full healing near the middle of the 21st century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). That means the treaty’s atmospheric impact is not negligible; it is associated with recovery. The absence of specific numerical ozone measurements in the provided excerpts is a limitation, but the qualitative and temporal statements from UNEP and EPA are consistent and sufficient to reject claims of continued decline.

The broader implication is that the Montreal Protocol should not be conflated with the Paris Agreement or with CO2 cap-and-trade systems. It regulates ozone-depleting chemicals and, through Kigali, HFCs. It does not, according to the provided authoritative sources, set national CO2 budgets or cap industrial CO2 emissions ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The treaty’s success story is therefore specifically atmospheric-ozone protection, with a long-term healing horizon around the middle of the 21st century.

## Conclusion

The Montreal Protocol is a global agreement to protect the Earth’s ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it, including controls on both production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The original agreement was adopted in 1987, and developed countries were required to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The Kigali Amendment of 2016 extended controls to HFCs ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Its effect on the ozone layer is recovery: UNEP describes the ozone layer as well on its way to recovery, and EPA expects full healing near the middle of the 21st century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Claims that the treaty caps CO2, sets national CO2 budgets, or has produced no measurable ozone recovery are unsupported by these authoritative sources and should be rejected.
