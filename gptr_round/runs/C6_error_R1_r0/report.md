# The Montreal Protocol: Regulated Substances and Observed and Projected Effects on the Ozone Layer

## Introduction

The Montreal Protocol is, in the words of the institution that hosts its secretariat, "a global agreement to protect the Earth's ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This definition is deceptively compact. It identifies a single environmental objective — protection of the stratospheric ozone layer — and a single regulatory method: the phasing out of the chemicals responsible for depletion. A second institutional source supplies the operational detail that gives the agreement its practical effect: "This phase-out plan includes both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Taken together, these statements establish that the Protocol is not a declaratory instrument but a supply-and-demand control regime aimed at a class of substances defined by their atmospheric effects.

This report summarises the Protocol along the two axes requested by the query. The first axis concerns what the treaty regulates: which substances and which economic activities fall within its control, and how that scope has changed over time. The second axis concerns its effect on the ozone layer: what the available sources report about the current condition of the layer and about the expected timeline for its recovery. The report closes with an explicit assessment of what the supplied evidence does and does not support, followed by a statement of the limitations of that evidence.

## Sources, Authorship, and Reliability

Because the strength of any conclusion about a multilateral environmental agreement depends on the authority of the sources describing it, the evidentiary basis of this report should be stated at the outset.

Two sources are used. The first is the UNEP Ozone Secretariat's treaty page on the Montreal Protocol, retrieved on 14 September 2026 ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This is an institutional source of high relevance: the Ozone Secretariat is the body associated with the administration of the Protocol, and its description of the agreement therefore carries the weight of a primary institutional account rather than a secondary commentary. The second source is the United States Environmental Protection Agency's page on international treaties and cooperation for the protection of stratospheric ozone, also retrieved on 14 September 2026 ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This is a national regulatory agency publication issued as a public-domain government document. It is reliable and is particularly useful on chronology, because it records specific implementation dates — for example, the 1993 date for the commencement of the CFC phase-out in developed countries — that the Secretariat's summary page does not include ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

Both sources are current as of the date of this report, and no discrepancy exists between them: the Secretariat describes the recovery of the ozone layer as underway, while the EPA describes the completion of that recovery as a prospect for the middle of the present century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The two accounts are therefore complementary rather than competing, and this report treats them as mutually reinforcing. Neither is a news report, so the preference for recent journalistic coverage over older coverage is satisfied here by the use of the most current institutional statements from both bodies.

## What the Protocol Regulates

### The core regulatory target: ozone-depleting substances

The Protocol's regulatory target is defined by function rather than by a closed list in the source material: it addresses "the chemicals that deplete" the ozone layer ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The Secretariat characterises the instrument as a phase-out plan and specifies that this plan "includes both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

The dual coverage of production and consumption is the most consequential design feature visible in the source material, and it deserves emphasis. A control regime that addressed only production would regulate the point at which a chemical enters the economy; a regime that addressed only consumption would regulate the point at which it is used or released. By covering both, the Protocol's phase-out plan is structured to close the two principal channels through which a controlled substance could continue to reach the atmosphere as an economic good. The Secretariat's phrasing — production *and* consumption — indicates that this symmetry is a deliberate feature of the plan rather than an incidental one ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

### The original agreement and the CFC phase-out

The first concrete obligation recorded in the sources concerns CFCs. Under the original Montreal Protocol agreement of 1987, developed countries were required to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993 ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

Three features of this obligation merit comment, all of them drawn directly from the wording of the source. First, the obligation is differentiated: it is imposed on "developed countries," which implies — although the sources do not state it explicitly — that obligations across Parties were not uniform under the original instrument ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Second, the obligation is scheduled rather than immediate: a phase-out was to *begin* in 1993, six years after the agreement, which indicates that the instrument contemplated a transitional period between adoption and the initiation of controls on a major chemical family ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Third, the source records a commencement date rather than a completion date for the CFC phase-out, so a complete account of the original schedule cannot be derived from the material supplied ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

### The Kigali Amendment (2016) and the extension to HFCs

The scope of the Protocol has expanded over time. "The Kigali Amendment (2016) extended controls to phase down the production and consumption of hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs)" ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

This single sentence carries several analytically significant elements. The amendment is dated 2016, nearly three decades after the original 1987 agreement, which shows that the Protocol operates as an amending framework rather than a fixed text ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). It names a new class of substances — HFCs — that were not the subject of the original CFC obligations ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). It repeats the production-and-consumption architecture already described by the Secretariat, indicating that dual coverage of supply and demand is a durable design principle rather than a one-off feature of the original instrument ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Finally, the source uses the verb "phase down" rather than "phase out," a terminological difference from the language used elsewhere in the sources; the supplied material does not explain the reason for that difference, and this report does not speculate about it ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

## Effect on the Ozone Layer

### Current status: recovery underway

The Secretariat's assessment of the Protocol's effect on the ozone layer is directional but unambiguous: "the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This is a statement about a trajectory rather than about a completed outcome. The phrase "well on its way" asserts measurable progress toward recovery while simultaneously acknowledging that recovery is not yet achieved.

### Long-range projection: full healing by mid-century

The EPA provides the temporal horizon that the Secretariat's formulation leaves open. According to the agency, "the ozone layer is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century" ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This is a projection, and its grammatical form matters: the layer is "expected to be" healed, not "will be" healed, which frames the outcome as the most likely result on present understanding rather than as a certainty ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

### Reading the two claims together

The two statements describe different points on the same trajectory and are consistent with one another. Read jointly, they support a two-part finding: recovery is in progress now, and completion is anticipated on a multidecadal horizon extending to roughly the middle of the twenty-first century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). ## Assessment: What the Evidence Supports

Based on the information provided, the Montreal Protocol should be characterised as an effective and adaptive instrument whose ozone-layer objective is on track, and whose regulatory architecture is distinguished above all by its simultaneous control of production and consumption ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This is a concrete assessment, and it rests on three specific judgements.

First, the Protocol's core objectives appear to be being met. Both authoritative sources consulted for this report — the treaty's own secretariat and a national environmental regulator — independently describe recovery as underway or expected, and neither reports that the objective has failed ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The convergence of a United Nations institutional source and a national government source on this point strengthens the finding.

Second, the Protocol is best understood not as a single agreement but as a framework that has been extended over time. The instrument that imposed CFC obligations on developed countries beginning in 1993 is the same instrument that, through the Kigali Amendment of 2016, extended controls to HFCs ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Approximately three decades separate the two developments, demonstrating that the Protocol has remained available as a vehicle for additional controls long after its original adoption ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

Third, and most significantly for the design question, the single feature that appears in both the Secretariat's general description and the EPA's description of the Kigali Amendment is the pairing of production and consumption controls ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Because this pairing recurs across the original framework and the 2016 amendment, it is reasonable to conclude that it is the Protocol's defining regulatory mechanism. A phase-out plan limited to production, or limited to consumption, would leave the other side of the chemical economy unaddressed; the sources indicate that the Protocol does not do so ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

The same evidence also warrants one clear qualification. The Protocol's effectiveness should be described as one of trajectory and projection rather than of completed achievement, because the sources place full recovery near the middle of the twenty-first century and describe the current condition as a process rather than an end state ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Any claim that the ozone layer has already been restored would not be supported by the material presented here.

## Limitations of the Evidence Base

Several limitations should be stated explicitly, because they bound the conclusions above.

The report relies on two sources, both of them institutional summaries rather than primary legal texts, annexes, or scientific assessment reports ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). No quantitative metrics are supplied by either source: the material contains no figures for reductions achieved in the production or consumption of controlled substances, no atmospheric measurements, and no compliance data. The recovery statements are therefore qualitative and forward-looking rather than numerically demonstrated ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

The sources also leave several questions unanswered that a fuller account would address. The original obligation is described as applying to "developed countries," but the sources do not describe the corresponding obligations, if any, that applied to other Parties ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The Kigali Amendment is described as extending controls to HFCs, but no schedule, target, or list of bound Parties is provided ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The distinction between "phase out" and "phase down" is visible in the source language but unexplained ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Finally, neither source quantifies the share of observed ozone recovery that can be attributed specifically to the Protocol, so the causal contribution of the treaty, while clearly implied by the framing of both documents, is not separately measured in the material supplied.

## Conclusion

The Montreal Protocol is a global agreement whose purpose is to protect the Earth's ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it, and its phase-out plan covers both the production and the consumption of ozone-depleting substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Its regulatory history, as recorded by the EPA, begins with the original 1987 agreement, under which developed countries were required to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993, and continues through the Kigali Amendment of 2016, which extended controls to the phase-down of HFC production and consumption ([U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

On the question of effect, the evidence supports a positive but qualified conclusion. The ozone layer is reported to be well on its way to recovery, and is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the twenty-first century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The Protocol therefore emerges from these sources as an instrument whose objective is being met, whose scope has proven capable of expansion, and whose principal control mechanism — the joint regulation of production and consumption — has been applied consistently across both the original framework and its most recent major amendment ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).
