# The Montreal Protocol: Regulatory Scope and Its Effect on the Ozone Layer

## Introduction

The Montreal Protocol is best understood as a global environmental agreement designed to protect the Earth’s ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Its regulatory core is not limited to one chemical or one stage of the supply chain: the phase-out plan includes both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The United States Environmental Protection Agency further explains that under the original 1987 Montreal Protocol agreement, developed countries were required to begin phasing out chlorofluorocarbons, or CFCs, in 1993 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Later, the Kigali Amendment of 2016 extended controls to phase down the production and consumption of hydrofluorocarbons, or HFCs ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The effect on the ozone layer is described in positive but still incomplete terms: UNEP states that the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery, while EPA projects that it is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

This report summarizes the Montreal Protocol’s regulatory scope, its key amendment, and its effect on the ozone layer. It concludes, based on the provided sources, that the Protocol is a functioning and adaptive international agreement whose phase-out controls have placed the ozone layer on a recovery trajectory, but that full recovery remains a mid-century objective rather than an accomplished fact.

## What the Montreal Protocol Is

The Montreal Protocol is described by UNEP as “a global agreement to protect the Earth’s ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it” ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This definition establishes two essential elements. First, the Protocol has a clear environmental objective: protection of the ozone layer. Second, it has a defined regulatory method: phasing out chemicals that deplete ozone ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

The Protocol is not merely a statement of intent. According to UNEP, its phase-out plan includes both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). That scope is significant because it means the agreement addresses the manufacture of controlled chemicals as well as their use. A treaty that regulated only consumption might reduce demand but leave production capacity intact; a treaty that regulates both production and consumption can more comprehensively constrain the supply of and demand for ozone-depleting substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

The EPA source adds a historical and legal dimension by referring to the original Montreal Protocol agreement of 1987 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Under that original framework, developed countries were required to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This indicates that the Protocol created differentiated implementation obligations and set a schedule for action, rather than relying only on voluntary commitments. The original agreement therefore provides a concrete example of how the broader ozone-depleting substance phase-out operated in practice.

## What the Protocol Regulates

### Ozone-Depleting Substances and the Production–Consumption Phase-Out

The primary regulatory target of the Montreal Protocol is ozone-depleting substances. UNEP describes the agreement’s purpose as phasing out “the chemicals that deplete” the ozone layer, and it states that the phase-out plan includes both production and consumption ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The term “ozone-depleting substances” is broader than any single chemical family, but the EPA source identifies CFCs as a specific class subject to early controls under the original 1987 agreement ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Together, these sources show that the Protocol regulates ozone-depleting chemicals at the level of both manufacturing and use, with CFCs serving as one prominent example.

The production-and-consumption formula is central to the Protocol’s regulatory design. If a substance is phased out only in its final use, production could continue for export or other markets. By including production and consumption, the Protocol’s phase-out plan is designed to reduce the overall flow of controlled chemicals into the economy and atmosphere ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The EPA’s reference to a required phase-out beginning in 1993 for developed countries reinforces that the agreement generated scheduled obligations for at least some parties ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

### The Original 1987 Framework and CFCs

The EPA’s account of the original Montreal Protocol emphasizes both timing and obligation. The agreement is dated to 1987, and developed countries were required to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This six-year interval between agreement and the start of phase-out suggests that the Protocol established a planned transition rather than an immediate ban. The use of the phrase “begin phasing out” also indicates a staged process: the requirement was not necessarily that all CFCs disappear in 1993, but that the phase-out process would start then ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

CFCs are therefore a key early target. The EPA source does not list every chemical covered by the original agreement, but it provides CFCs as a concrete category of ozone-depleting substances subject to phase-out obligations ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). UNEP’s broader language about “chemicals that deplete” the ozone layer and “ozone-depleting substances” provides the general regulatory category within which CFCs sit ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The combination of these sources supports the conclusion that the Protocol regulates ozone-depleting chemicals generally while CFCs were an early and prominent focus.

### The Kigali Amendment and HFCs

The Montreal Protocol’s regulatory scope has expanded over time. The EPA reports that the Kigali Amendment of 2016 extended controls to phase down the production and consumption of HFCs ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This is a notable development because it shows that the Protocol is not frozen to its original 1987 substance list. Instead, the treaty framework was amended to address an additional class of chemicals through production and consumption controls ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

The Kigali Amendment also introduces a subtle but important regulatory distinction. The original ozone-depleting substance controls described by UNEP are framed as a “phase-out” plan, whereas the Kigali Amendment is described by EPA as a “phase down” of HFC production and consumption ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). A phase-out implies elimination of production and consumption, while a phase-down implies a gradual reduction. The EPA source does not explain the policy rationale for this difference, but the language itself indicates that the Kigali controls are structured as a reduction pathway rather than an immediate or complete elimination requirement ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

This amendment is significant for the query because it shows the Protocol’s regulatory identity is adaptive. The agreement began with ozone-depleting substances and CFC phase-outs, and later extended controls to HFCs under the Kigali Amendment ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The provided sources do not state whether HFC controls directly affect ozone layer recovery, nor do they provide atmospheric data on HFCs. They do, however, establish that the Protocol’s scope has been formally broadened through the Kigali Amendment.

## Effect on the Ozone Layer

### Recovery Is Underway According to UNEP

The most direct statement of the Protocol’s effect is UNEP’s conclusion that “the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery” ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This is a significant claim because it links the Protocol’s phase-out plan to a positive environmental outcome. The UNEP source does not provide a year-by-year recovery curve or quantify the percentage of recovery, but it does characterize the ozone layer’s status as improving and moving toward recovery ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

Because UNEP describes the Protocol as a global agreement to phase out ozone-depleting chemicals and states that the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery, the logical relationship is that the phase-out of production and consumption has contributed to that recovery ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The source does not isolate the Protocol’s effect from other factors, but it presents the agreement as the central international response to ozone depletion. The recovery statement therefore functions as the clearest available evidence that the Protocol is producing its intended environmental effect.

### Full Healing Is Expected Near the Middle of the 21st Century

The EPA source provides a longer-term projection: the ozone layer is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This projection is important because it prevents an overly optimistic reading of the recovery claim. The ozone layer may be “well on its way” to recovery, but it is not yet fully healed ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

The mid-century timeline also implies that the Protocol’s effect is intergenerational. The original agreement dates to 1987, developed-country CFC phase-outs began in 1993, the Kigali Amendment was adopted in 2016, and full healing is expected near the middle of the 21st century ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This sequence shows that the treaty’s regulatory actions and the ozone layer’s response operate on different timescales. Regulatory controls can begin relatively quickly, but the environmental recovery of the ozone layer extends across decades ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

## Assessment: What the Evidence Supports

Based on the provided sources, a concrete and defensible assessment is that the Montreal Protocol should be viewed as an effective and adaptive environmental agreement, but not as a completed success. Its effectiveness rests on three observable features. First, it regulates a clearly identified problem: ozone-depleting chemicals ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Second, it uses a robust regulatory mechanism: phase-out of both production and consumption, rather than only end-use restrictions ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Third, it has produced a stated recovery signal: the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

At the same time, the evidence does not justify declaring victory. EPA projects full healing only near the middle of the 21st century, which means the ozone layer remains in a recovery phase rather than a fully restored state ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The Protocol’s original CFC phase-out began in 1993 for developed countries, and the Kigali Amendment followed in 2016, indicating that international controls have required repeated expansion and long-term implementation ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The agreement is therefore best characterized as a long-running regulatory project whose environmental benefits are real but still unfolding.

A second conclusion is that the Protocol’s scope has evolved beyond its original ozone-depletion focus. The Kigali Amendment extended controls to HFC production and consumption, showing that the treaty can be amended to address additional substances ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The provided sources do not establish the full atmospheric rationale for HFC controls or quantify their effect on ozone recovery, so the Kigali Amendment should be understood primarily as evidence of the Protocol’s adaptive regulatory capacity rather than as a directly measured ozone recovery factor ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

## Limitations of the Evidence Base

The sources used here are authoritative for treaty scope and official recovery projections. UNEP Ozone Secretariat is the treaty secretariat, and the EPA is a national environmental regulator providing treaty history and expected timelines ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). However, the provided information does not include quantitative atmospheric concentration data, annual compliance statistics, enforcement records, or financial/technical assistance details. It also does not provide a numerical estimate of how much recovery is attributable specifically to the Montreal Protocol compared with other factors.

For that reason, this report’s conclusions are limited to the regulatory design and the stated recovery trajectory. The evidence supports the claim that the Protocol regulates ozone-depleting substances through production and consumption phase-outs, that CFCs were an early target under the original 1987 agreement, that the Kigali Amendment extended controls to HFCs, and that the ozone layer is described as recovering with full healing expected near the middle of the 21st century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). It does not support more precise claims about measured ozone levels or the exact percentage of recovery achieved.

## Conclusion

The Montreal Protocol is a global agreement to protect the ozone layer by phasing out ozone-depleting chemicals, and its phase-out plan covers both production and consumption ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The original 1987 agreement required developed countries to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993, and the 2016 Kigali Amendment extended controls to a phase-down of HFC production and consumption ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Its effect on the ozone layer is described as positive: UNEP states that the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery, while EPA projects full healing near the middle of the 21st century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

The most reasonable conclusion from the evidence is that the Montreal Protocol is an effective and adaptable framework, but that its work is incomplete. It has regulated the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances, added HFC controls through the Kigali Amendment, and contributed to a recovery trajectory that is expected to culminate in full healing around the middle of the 21st century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The Protocol’s significance therefore lies not only in what it has already achieved, but also in its continuing role as a long-term international mechanism for ozone layer protection.
