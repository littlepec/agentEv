# The Montreal Protocol: Regulated Substances and Effects on the Ozone Layer

## Introduction

The Montreal Protocol is the treaty through which the international community has addressed depletion of the stratospheric ozone layer. According to the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) Ozone Secretariat, the Protocol is "a global agreement to protect the Earth's ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The same source states that "[t]his phase-out plan includes both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances," and that as a result "the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) adds temporal and substance-specific detail, noting that "[u]nder the original Montreal Protocol agreement (1987), developed countries were required to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993," that "the ozone layer is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century," and that "[t]he Kigali Amendment (2016) extended controls to phase down the production and consumption of hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs)" ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

This report summarizes what the Protocol regulates and what effect it has had on the ozone layer, drawing exclusively on the source material supplied. It also assesses the strength of that evidence and identifies where the available sources are silent.

## Source Base and Evidentiary Weight

Before presenting findings, it is important to state the basis on which they rest. The supplied material consists of two substantive, institutionally authoritative sources and one general background document. Prioritizing reliability, this report treats the UNEP Ozone Secretariat and the US EPA as the primary evidentiary anchors, because both are official bodies with direct institutional responsibility for the subject matter: UNEP hosts the treaty secretariat, and the EPA is a national environmental regulator publishing in the public domain ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

The third document, "International Environmental Agreements," is explicitly framed as general background. It observes that countries "have negotiated many international environmental agreements to address shared atmospheric and ecological concerns," that such agreements "involve monitoring, reporting, periodic amendments, and cooperation among governments and scientists," and that public discussion "often focuses on compliance, funding for developing countries, and how progress is measured over time" (International Environmental Agreements, n.d.). Critically, that source states of itself that it "does not identify which substances a specific treaty regulates or what atmospheric outcome resulted" (International Environmental Agreements, n.d.). It is therefore useful for situating the Montreal Protocol within the broader architecture of environmental governance, but it cannot be used to support any substance-specific or outcome-specific claim.

## What the Montreal Protocol Regulates

### The core regulatory objective

The Protocol's stated purpose is protective rather than economic: it is "a global agreement to protect the Earth's ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The regulated activity is not merely the use of such chemicals but their industrial lifecycle. UNEP specifies that the phase-out plan "includes both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This is a significant design feature: by capturing production alongside consumption, the agreement addresses the supply side of the ozone-depleting chemical economy as well as demand, rather than regulating only end use.

### Substances identified in the evidence: CFCs

The only ozone-depleting substance class named in the supplied material is chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs). The EPA records that under the original 1987 agreement, "developed countries were required to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993" ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Two elements of that statement merit emphasis. First, the obligation was differentiated: it applied in that instance to developed countries, which is consistent with the background document's observation that funding and compliance arrangements for developing countries are a recurring focus of public discussion about environmental agreements (International Environmental Agreements, n.d.). Second, the obligation was phased rather than instantaneous — a "begin phasing out" formulation implies a scheduled reduction pathway rather than a single prohibition date.

### The Kigali Amendment and HFCs

The Protocol's coverage has expanded over time. The EPA states that "[t]he Kigali Amendment (2016) extended controls to phase down the production and consumption of hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs)" ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). It should be noted precisely what the source does and does not say. It states that controls were *extended* to HFCs and that the mechanism is a *phase down* of production and consumption ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The supplied material does not characterize HFCs as ozone-depleting substances, nor does it specify the schedule or baselines of the HFC phase-down. The report therefore reports the extension of controls without asserting a rationale beyond what the source provides.

The term "phase down" as used for HFCs is also textually distinct from the "phase out" language applied to ozone-depleting substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The evidence supports noting this distinction; it does not support an explanation of its significance.

## The Effect on the Ozone Layer

### Present status: recovery underway

The most direct statement of effect in the supplied evidence is UNEP's assessment that "the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This is a status assessment rather than a quantified measurement; the source does not supply figures such as percentage ozone concentration, atmospheric chlorine loading, or recovery rates, and this report accordingly does not present any. What the evidence does establish is directional: the outcome associated with the phase-out is recovery, not continued degradation.

### Projected status: full healing near mid-century

The EPA provides the only forward-looking quantitative time frame in the material: "the ozone layer is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century" ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Taken together with UNEP's recovery assessment, the evidence describes a two-part outcome: recovery is already observable in the present, and completeness is anticipated around the mid-2000s-to-2050s horizon implied by "near the middle of the 21st century" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

## Analytical Discussion

### Adaptability as an institutional strength

A clear finding from the evidence is that the Protocol is not a static instrument. The background document notes that international environmental agreements generally "involve monitoring, reporting, periodic amendments, and cooperation among governments and scientists" (International Environmental Agreements, n.d.). The Montreal Protocol instantiates this pattern concretely: an original 1987 agreement, a CFC phase-out obligation commencing in 1993 for developed countries, and a 2016 amendment extending controls to HFCs ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The amendment mechanism has allowed the treaty's regulatory reach to widen without requiring negotiation of an entirely new instrument. In my assessment, the presence of a functioning amendment pathway — evidenced here by the Kigali Amendment — is the single most institutionally significant feature visible in the supplied material, because it demonstrates that the agreement's coverage is extensible in response to new scientific or technical understanding.

### The relationship between regulation and outcome

The evidence presented here supports a conclusion of alignment between the regulated action and the reported outcome: the Protocol regulates the production and consumption of ozone-depleting chemicals, and the reported result is ozone layer recovery with anticipated full healing near mid-century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). It should be stated plainly, however, that neither source in the supplied material provides a formal attribution analysis isolating the Protocol's contribution from other variables. UNEP's phrasing is a status statement about the ozone layer; the EPA's is a projection. The evidence is therefore strong on *what* has happened and *what is expected*, and supportive of a causal link given the treaty's stated purpose, but it does not itself quantify the counterfactual.

### The differentiated-obligation dimension

The EPA's specification that the 1993 phase-out obligation applied to developed countries points to differentiation among parties ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This connects to the background document's observation that public debate around such agreements frequently centers on "compliance, funding for developing countries, and how progress is measured over time" (International Environmental Agreements, n.d.). The supplied material does not include the Protocol's financial mechanism, its compliance procedures, or its specific measurement methodologies; those aspects cannot be described here without exceeding the evidence.

## Limitations of the Available Evidence

A rigorous summary must be explicit about gaps. The supplied material does not identify the full list of controlled ozone-depleting substances beyond CFCs; it does not state the number of parties to the Protocol; it does not provide negotiated phase-out schedules or percentage reduction targets; it does not give quantitative atmospheric measurements; and it does not describe the Multilateral Fund or non-compliance procedures. The background source is explicit that it "does not identify which substances a specific treaty regulates or what atmospheric outcome resulted" (International Environmental Agreements, n.d.), and it is therefore cited here only for generic institutional context. Any figures beyond those quoted — the 1987 original agreement, the 1993 CFC phase-out commencement, the 2016 Kigali Amendment, and the mid-21st-century healing horizon — should be treated as outside the scope of this report's sources ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

## Conclusion

On the evidence provided, the Montreal Protocol is a global agreement whose regulatory object is the phase-out of the chemicals that deplete the Earth's ozone layer, achieved by controlling both the production and the consumption of ozone-depleting substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Its concrete provisions as documented include the original 1987 agreement, a requirement beginning in 1993 for developed countries to begin phasing out CFCs, and the 2016 Kigali Amendment extending controls to a phase-down of HFC production and consumption ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Its reported effect on the ozone layer is recovery that is already under way, with full healing projected near the middle of the 21st century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). My own assessment, based strictly on these institutional sources, is that the Protocol is best understood not as a single fixed commitment but as an adaptive framework: it names a protected environmental resource, regulates the industrial activities that damage it, and retains the capacity — demonstrated by amendment — to extend those controls as circumstances require.
