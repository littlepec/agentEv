# The Montreal Protocol: Regulated Substances and Effects on the Stratospheric Ozone Layer

## Introduction

The Montreal Protocol is the central international legal instrument for protecting the Earth's stratospheric ozone layer. According to the UNEP Ozone Secretariat, it is "a global agreement to protect the Earth's ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it," and its control architecture "includes both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The same source states that, as a result of this regime, "the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The United States Environmental Protection Agency adds the temporal and substantive detail that, "under the original Montreal Protocol agreement (1987), developed countries were required to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993," that "the Kigali Amendment (2016) extended controls to phase down the production and consumption of hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs)," and that "the ozone layer is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century" ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

This report answers two connected questions: what the Protocol regulates, and what effect it has had on the ozone layer. It draws exclusively on the UNEP Ozone Secretariat, the US Environmental Protection Agency, and a general background text on international environmental agreements. The assessment is deliberately bounded by what these sources actually state, and it distinguishes clearly between documented outcomes and forward-looking projections.

## Background: How This Agreement Fits the Broader Category of Environmental Treaties

The Montreal Protocol belongs to a wider family of international environmental agreements, which, in general terms, "involve monitoring, reporting, periodic amendments, and cooperation among governments and scientists," with public discussion typically focusing on "compliance, funding for developing countries, and how progress is measured over time" ([International Environmental Agreements, n.d.](#references)). This general background is useful for framing the Protocol institutionally, but it is explicitly non-specific: it "does not identify which substances a specific treaty regulates or what atmospheric outcome resulted" ([International Environmental Agreements, n.d.](#references)). Consequently, the substantive answers in this report rest on the two authoritative institutional sources rather than on generic treaty literature.

Two features of that generic description are nonetheless directly observable in the Montreal Protocol as described by the authoritative sources. First, the treaty has a defined regulatory object — chemicals that deplete ozone — expressed functionally rather than as a closed list. Second, the treaty is amendable: the Kigali Amendment of 2016 extended its controls to a further class of substances ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)), which is a concrete instance of the "periodic amendments" characteristic noted in the background literature ([International Environmental Agreements, n.d.](#references)).

## What the Montreal Protocol Regulates

### The Core Regulatory Object

The Protocol's regulatory target is defined by effect rather than by chemical family alone. UNEP describes the agreement as phasing out "the chemicals that deplete" the ozone layer, and describes the phase-out plan as covering "both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This dual coverage of production and consumption is significant: it means the treaty does not merely restrict the manufacture of controlled substances, but also their use and commercial circulation within participating economies.

The functional definition is what allows the regime to remain relevant over time. Because the treaty is framed around substances that deplete ozone rather than around a static annex of chemicals agreed in 1987, the parties can extend controls as scientific understanding and industrial practice evolve — a mechanism illustrated concretely by the 2016 Kigali Amendment ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

### The Original 1987 Agreement and CFCs

The original instrument dates to 1987. Under it, "developed countries were required to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993," establishing a differentiated obligation in which the initial phase-out timetable applied to developed economies ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Chlorofluorocarbons therefore constitute the earliest explicitly identified class of controlled substances in the material available here, and the 1993 date marks the beginning of the phase-out rather than its completion.

It is worth being precise about what the sources do and do not establish. They confirm that CFCs were subject to a phase-out obligation beginning in 1993 for developed countries ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). They do not, in the material provided, supply the full list of controlled substances, the schedules for developing countries, or quantitative reduction percentages. Any report that asserted such figures here would be going beyond its evidence base.

### The Kigali Amendment (2016) and HFCs

The most recent extension described in the available sources is the Kigali Amendment of 2016, which "extended controls to phase down the production and consumption of hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs)" ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Two points deserve emphasis. First, the Kigali controls are described as a "phase down," whereas the core ozone-depleting substance regime is described as a "phase-out" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The language of the sources thus distinguishes a gradual reduction trajectory from a complete elimination trajectory. Second, the amendment extends controls over both production and consumption, mirroring the original architecture ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

The provided sources do not state the rationale for controlling HFCs or whether HFCs are themselves ozone-depleting. They record only that the amendment extended the Protocol's controls to these substances. This is an important epistemic limit: the fact that HFC controls sit within the Montreal Protocol framework does not, on the basis of these sources alone, establish that HFCs deplete stratospheric ozone.

## Effect on the Ozone Layer

### Documented Direction of Travel: Recovery Underway

The authoritative sources converge on a positive directional finding. UNEP states that "the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This is a statement about trajectory: the ozone layer is characterized as recovering, not as recovered. The EPA's formulation is complementary and explicitly forward-looking: "the ozone layer is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century" ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

Taken together, these two statements describe a two-stage outcome structure: a recovery process already in progress as of the 2020s, and a projected completion milestone approximately in mid-century. The logical link between the regulatory mechanism and the atmospheric outcome is explicit in the UNEP framing: the agreement protects the ozone layer "by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The regulatory instrument and the atmospheric result are presented as cause and consequence.

### Interpretation of the Evidence

Several features of the available evidence shape how the outcome should be characterized. First, both substantive sources are institutional and authoritative: the UNEP Ozone Secretariat is the treaty's own secretariat, and the EPA material is a national government publication issued as public-domain information ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). These are high-reliability sources for the questions at issue.

Second, the sources are qualitative rather than quantitative on the atmospheric side. The material provided does not include measured ozone concentrations, reduction percentages for controlled substances, or atmospheric chlorine or bromine loading figures. An objective report must therefore refrain from attaching numbers to the recovery claim. The verifiable content is directional and temporal: recovery is reported to be underway, with full healing anticipated near mid-century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

Third, there is an inherent time lag in the system being described. A phase-out that began for developed countries with CFCs in 1993 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)) is associated with a healing projection extending to roughly the middle of the twenty-first century. The gap of several decades between regulatory action and projected atmospheric recovery is a structural feature of the evidence as presented, and it cautions against expecting immediate atmospheric response to control measures.

## Assessment: What the Evidence Supports

Based on the information provided, a defensible position is that the Montreal Protocol constitutes a demonstrably effective and institutionally adaptive ozone-protection regime, with the caveat that its principal atmospheric payoff remains partly in the future.

The case for effectiveness rests on the convergence of an explicit causal mechanism with an explicit outcome statement. The treaty is defined by its function — phasing out ozone-depleting chemicals through controls on both production and consumption ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)) — and the competent authorities report that the ozone layer is recovering and is expected to be fully healed near mid-century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The pairing of a specific regulatory intervention with a specific atmospheric trajectory, reported by the treaty secretariat and a national environmental regulator, is the strongest form of evidence available within these sources.

The case for adaptiveness rests on the amendment record. The Kigali Amendment's extension of controls to HFCs in 2016 illustrates that the regime can enlarge its regulatory perimeter without being replaced ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This corresponds directly to the general observation that such agreements operate through "periodic amendments" ([International Environmental Agreements, n.d.](#references)). Adaptiveness matters analytically because it means the Protocol's relevance is not fixed at its 1987 scope.

Three caveats should accompany this assessment. First, the recovery finding is described with the phrase "well on its way," which denotes progress rather than completion ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Second, the mid-century healing target is described as an expectation, not an observed fact ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Third, the sources do not provide the compliance, financing, or measurement data that the generic literature identifies as central to public debate about environmental agreements ([International Environmental Agreements, n.d.](#references)); those dimensions are therefore not evaluated here, and no conclusion about them should be inferred from this report.

## Conclusion

The Montreal Protocol regulates ozone-depleting chemicals through a phase-out plan covering both production and consumption, beginning with CFC phase-out obligations for developed countries in 1993 under the 1987 agreement, and extended in 2016 by the Kigali Amendment to include a phase down of HFC production and consumption ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Its effect on the ozone layer is reported to be positive and ongoing: the layer is "well on its way to recovery," with full healing expected near the middle of the twenty-first century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Within the limits of the available evidence, the Protocol should be judged not as a completed success but as a functioning, amendable control regime whose atmospheric benefits are already observable in direction and credibly projected in magnitude over the coming decades.
