## 1. Origin and Scientific Foundation

The Montreal Protocol is a treaty under the 1985 Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer. It was adopted on 16 September 1987 and entered into force on 1 January 1989 ([Montreal Protocol, 1987](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Its scientific justification rested on two findings: Molina and Rowland's 1974 argument that chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) are sufficiently inert to reach the stratosphere, where ultraviolet radiation liberates chlorine atoms that catalytically destroy ozone ([Molina & Rowland, 1974](https://doi.org/10.1038/249810a0)); and the 1985 discovery by Farman, Gardiner, and Shanklin of severe springtime ozone depletion over Antarctica, a phenomenon that existing models had not predicted ([Farman et al., 1985](https://doi.org/10.1038/315207a0)). The Protocol's design therefore reflects an explicit science‑policy feedback loop: Article 6 requires periodic assessments of the science, the environmental effects, and the technology and economics of control, and the parties are obliged to act on those assessments ([Montreal Protocol, 1987](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

## 2. What the Protocol Regulates

### 2.1 An annex‑based control architecture

The Protocol does not regulate "ozone‑depleting substances" generically. It controls named chemicals listed in annexes, with each annex group carrying its own baseline, schedule, and reporting obligations. Control is expressed in ODP‑tonnes — mass multiplied by each substance's ozone‑depletion potential relative to CFC‑11, which is assigned a value of 1.0 ([Montreal Protocol, 1987](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Brominated compounds carry much higher ODPs than chlorinated ones (for example, halon‑1211 is near 3 and halon‑1301 near 10), which is why halons were placed on the fastest early schedule.

Two structural features deserve emphasis. First, the Protocol distinguishes between parties operating under Article 5 (developing countries with low per‑capita consumption) and those that are not, granting the former a grace period of roughly a decade. Second, Annex F is different in kind from Annexes A–E: hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) do not deplete ozone at all. They were added because they are potent greenhouse gases widely deployed as ODS replacements, making the Kigali Amendment a climate instrument grafted onto an ozone treaty ([Kigali Amendment, 2016](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol/amendments/kigali-amendment-2016-amendment-montreal-protocol)).

## 3. Compliance, Finance, and Institutional Design

The Protocol's effectiveness is widely attributed to three design choices. The first is **differentiated responsibility with shared obligation**: all parties are bound by control measures, but developing parties receive delayed schedules. The second is **dedicated finance**. The Multilateral Fund, established by the London Amendment, finances the incremental costs of compliance in Article 5 countries; cumulative contributions from developed parties now exceed US$4 billion, with replenishments of approximately US$540 million for 2021–2023 and US$965 million for 2024–2026 ([Multilateral Fund Secretariat, 2024](https://www.multilateralfund.org/)). The third is **enforcement with teeth**. Article 8 provides a non‑compliance procedure administered by an Implementation Committee, and Article 4 permits trade restrictions on ODS and ODS‑containing products with non‑parties ([Montreal Protocol, 1987](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The result is the first environmental treaty to achieve universal participation, with 198 parties.

## 4. Evidence of Effect on the Ozone Layer

### 4.1 Stratospheric halogen loading

The most direct test of the Protocol is whether the abundance of ozone‑depleting halogens in the stratosphere has fallen. Equivalent effective stratospheric chlorine — an index combining chlorine and bromine loading with an adjustment for bromine's greater efficiency — peaked in the late 1990s and has declined steadily since, closely tracking the treaty's control schedules rather than any natural cycle ([WMO, 2022](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)). Production and consumption of controlled ODS have fallen by approximately 99% relative to baselines established under the Protocol ([UNEP, 2023](https://www.unep.org/news-and-stories/press-release/ozone-layer-recovery-track-help-avert-global-warming-05degc)). Because CFCs have atmospheric lifetimes of 50–100 years, the decline in halogen loading lags the decline in emissions by decades; the observed reduction is therefore evidence of policy effectiveness, not merely of chemical decay.

### 4.2 The Antarctic ozone hole

The Antarctic ozone hole remains the most conspicuous indicator of ozone depletion. Its area varies substantially between years because it is governed primarily by stratospheric temperatures: 2019 recorded one of the smallest holes on record following a sudden stratospheric warming, while other years have approached 25 million km² ([NASA Ozone Watch, 2024](https://ozonewatch.gsfc.nasa.gov/)). The 2022 WMO/UNEP assessment concluded that the hole's area and depth no longer show the accelerating deterioration seen in the 1980s and 1990s, and that its severity is now dominated by meteorological variability superimposed on a slowly declining halogen baseline ([WMO, 2022](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)).

### 4.4 Attribution

Attribution is supported by three convergent lines of evidence: the timing of halogen decline matches the control schedules; recovery is proceeding in the lower stratosphere even as upper‑stratospheric ozone continues to reflect long atmospheric lifetimes; and the Kigali Amendment's HFC controls were adopted specifically because the climate benefit of the substitution pathway had become measurable ([WMO, 2022](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/); [UNEP, 2023](https://www.unep.org/news-and-stories/press-release/ozone-layer-recovery-track-help-avert-global-warming-05degc)).

## 5. Co‑Benefits and Climate Implications

Because most ODS are also greenhouse gases, phasing them out has produced climate benefits far exceeding the ozone benefit in magnitude. The United States Environmental Protection Agency estimates that, relative to a no‑control scenario, the Protocol will prevent approximately 443 million cases of non‑melanoma skin cancer, 2.3 million premature skin cancer deaths, and 63 million cataract cases among people born between 1890 and 2105 in the United States alone ([EPA, 2015](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/updating-ozone-calculations-and-estimates-o3-montreal-protocol)). In the climate domain, the Kigali Amendment is projected to avoid roughly 0.3–0.5 °C of warming by 2100 ([UNEP, 2023](https://www.unep.org/news-and-stories/press-release/ozone-layer-recovery-track-help-avert-global-warming-05degc)). Related research indicates that the Protocol also delays the first ice‑free Arctic summer by roughly 15 years ([England et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/nclimate3047)). On these grounds, the treaty is frequently described as having done more to avert radiative forcing than any other environmental agreement.

## 6. Uncertainties and Limitations

Three limitations temper the record. First, **illegal emissions persist**: a sustained, unexpected rise in CFC‑11 emissions after 2012, detected in atmospheric measurements and largely traced to eastern Asia, showed that compliance monitoring can lapse and that atmospheric verification is essential ([Montzka et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-018-0106-2)). Second, **the Protocol is not comprehensive**: nitrous oxide, now the largest remaining anthropogenic ozone‑depleting emission, is not controlled, and very short‑lived substances such as dichloromethane are largely outside the annexes ([WMO, 2022](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)). Third, **recovery is slow and meteorological noise is large**, meaning that a decade of apparently anomalous Antarctic ozone holes would not by itself falsify the treaty's effectiveness.

## 7. Assessment and Conclusion

The Montreal Protocol regulates a specified list of chlorinated, brominated, and — under Kigali — fluorinated substances through annex‑specific schedules, backed by a multilateral fund, a non‑compliance procedure, and trade provisions. Its measured effect on the ozone layer is positive and causally attributable: stratospheric halogen loading has declined since peaking in the late 1990s, roughly 99% of controlled ODS production and consumption has been eliminated, and the 2022 assessment projects a return to 1980 ozone levels by about 2040 globally, 2045 in the Arctic, and 2066 in Antarctica ([WMO, 2022](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)). The defensible conclusion is neither that the problem is solved nor that the treaty has failed, but that the Protocol has demonstrably reversed the trajectory of ozone depletion and delivered substantial public‑health and climate co‑benefits, while leaving identifiable gaps — uncontrolled N₂O, short‑lived substances, and enforcement — that will determine whether the projected recovery is realized on schedule.
