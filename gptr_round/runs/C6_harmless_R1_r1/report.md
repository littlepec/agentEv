# Summary of the Montreal Protocol: Regulated Substances and Effects on the Ozone Layer

## Introduction
The Montreal Protocol is a global agreement designed to protect the Earth's ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Its central regulatory strategy is not limited to prohibiting the use of such chemicals; the phase-out plan explicitly includes both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). According to the UNEP Ozone Secretariat, the ozone layer is now "well on its way to recovery," indicating that the treaty is associated with a positive atmospheric outcome ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The US Environmental Protection Agency further reports that the ozone layer is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). These two official sources provide the factual basis for this report. The report examines what the Montreal Protocol regulates, how its scope has evolved from the original 1987 agreement to the 2016 Kigali Amendment, and what the available evidence indicates about its effect on the ozone layer.

## What the Montreal Protocol Regulates
### Core objective and scope
The Montreal Protocol is defined by its ozone-protection purpose. It is "a global agreement to protect the Earth's ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This definition establishes both the treaty's environmental objective and its regulatory method: the controlled substances are those identified as depleting the ozone layer, and the primary mechanism is phase-out. The scope is therefore directly tied to atmospheric chemistry rather than to a single industry or product. The agreement addresses the chemicals themselves, not merely the emissions or end uses associated with them ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). That distinction matters because a treaty that regulates only consumption may leave production capacity intact, while a treaty that regulates only production may leave existing stocks in use. The Montreal Protocol's phase-out plan includes both production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances, making it a supply-and-demand control framework ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This dual focus is a defining feature of the agreement.

### Original 1987 framework and CFCs
The original Montreal Protocol agreement was concluded in 1987 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Under that original framework, developed countries were required to begin phasing out chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) in 1993 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This requirement shows that the treaty's initial regulatory attention was directed at a specific class of chemicals and that implementation followed a staged timetable. The 1993 start date for developed-country CFC phase-out also indicates that the Protocol did not rely solely on voluntary action; it established binding obligations for at least one group of parties ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The original agreement therefore created a precedent for international controls on ozone-depleting substances and provided an institutional model that could later be expanded ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The UNEP Ozone Secretariat describes the overall effort as a phase-out plan covering production and consumption, which is consistent with the EPA's account of the original CFC controls ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

### Kigali Amendment and HFCs
The Protocol's regulatory scope has not remained static. The Kigali Amendment (2016) extended controls to phase down the production and consumption of hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This is a notable development because it broadened the agreement's reach beyond the original CFC phase-out framework. Under the Kigali Amendment, the relevant control is a phase-down rather than a phase-out, and it again covers both production and consumption ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The amendment therefore continues the treaty's pattern of addressing the supply side and the demand side together. It also demonstrates that the Montreal Protocol is not a fixed instrument; it can be amended to bring additional substances under international control ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). For the purpose of summarizing what the Protocol regulates, the key point is that its scope now includes the original ozone-depleting substances addressed under the 1987 agreement and the HFCs addressed under the 2016 Kigali Amendment ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone); [UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

This combination of continuity and expansion is central to understanding the treaty's regulatory identity.

## Effects on the Ozone Layer
### Current recovery trajectory
The available evidence indicates that the Montreal Protocol has had a positive effect on the ozone layer. The UNEP Ozone Secretariat states that "the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery" ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This statement is significant because it links the treaty's regulatory actions to an atmospheric outcome. The Protocol's purpose is to protect the ozone layer by phasing out ozone-depleting chemicals, and the recovery assessment suggests that this purpose is being achieved ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The effect is not described as complete, however. The phrase "well on its way" implies progress rather than a finished result ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The EPA's outlook reinforces this interpretation by projecting full healing near the middle of the 21st century ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Taken together, the sources present a trajectory of recovery that is underway but still decades from completion.

### Expected full healing
The EPA states that "the ozone layer is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century" ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This is the most specific long-term outcome provided by the supplied information. It indicates that the Protocol's effect on the ozone layer is expected to be substantial and durable, but also that the recovery process is slow. The original agreement's CFC phase-out began in 1993 for developed countries, and the Kigali Amendment followed in 2016 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The expected mid-century healing therefore reflects a long atmospheric and regulatory timeline. The UNEP Ozone Secretariat's statement that the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery is consistent with the EPA's projection of full healing: current progress is positive, and the endpoint is expected, but not yet reached ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

The regulatory cause is the phase-out of ozone-depleting substances, including controls on both production and consumption ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The Kigali Amendment adds a further control layer for HFCs, showing that the treaty's regulatory scope has expanded even as the ozone layer moves toward recovery ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

## Assessment: Strengths, Limitations, and Implications
Based on the supplied information, the Montreal Protocol can be judged as a successful but unfinished international environmental agreement. Its clearest strength is its dual regulation of production and consumption ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). By targeting both sides of the market for ozone-depleting substances, the treaty addresses the supply of controlled chemicals and the demand for them. This design is more comprehensive than a narrow use restriction because it seeks to prevent continued production while also reducing consumption ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The original 1987 agreement and the 1993 CFC phase-out requirement for developed countries demonstrate that the treaty translated its objective into concrete obligations ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The Kigali Amendment further shows that the agreement can adapt by extending controls to HFCs and requiring a phase-down of their production and consumption ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This adaptive capacity is a second major strength.

The main limitation visible in the sources is temporal. The ozone layer is "well on its way to recovery," but it is not expected to be fully healed until near the middle of the 21st century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). That long horizon means that the Protocol's ultimate success cannot be declared solely on the basis of early progress. Continued implementation, adherence to phase-out and phase-down schedules, and maintenance of the treaty's controls will be necessary for the expected mid-century outcome to be realized ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The sources do not provide compliance data or enforcement details, so the assessment here is limited to the regulatory design and the stated recovery outlook. Within those limits, the evidence supports a clear conclusion: the Montreal Protocol regulates both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances, has expanded to control HFCs through the Kigali Amendment, and has put the ozone layer on a recovery path that is expected to culminate in full healing near the middle of the 21st century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

A further implication is that the Protocol's effectiveness should be evaluated in relation to its own stated objective. The treaty is not a general air-pollution agreement; it is specifically a global agreement to protect the ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The fact that the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery is therefore direct evidence that the treaty is working toward its intended atmospheric goal ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The Kigali Amendment extends the treaty's regulatory reach, but the supplied information does not describe its effect on ozone recovery specifically; it describes a phase-down of HFC production and consumption ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This means the strongest ozone-related conclusion from the available sources remains tied to the phase-out of ozone-depleting substances under the original framework and the UNEP recovery statement ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

In practical terms, the Montreal Protocol's effect on the ozone layer can be summarized as follows. It regulates ozone-depleting substances by phasing out their production and consumption ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). It began with a 1987 agreement and required developed countries to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). It expanded in 2016 through the Kigali Amendment to phase down HFC production and consumption ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The ozone layer is now well on its way to recovery, according to UNEP, and is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century, according to the EPA ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). These findings support an objective assessment that the Protocol has been effective in initiating recovery, while its final success remains a long-term outcome dependent on sustained implementation.

## Conclusion
The Montreal Protocol is a global agreement that protects the Earth's ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Its phase-out plan covers both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances, giving it a broad regulatory reach ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The original 1987 agreement required developed countries to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993, and the 2016 Kigali Amendment extended controls to a phase-down of HFC production and consumption ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The effect on the ozone layer is positive but incomplete: the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery, and full healing is expected near the middle of the 21st century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The most defensible conclusion from the supplied evidence is that the Montreal Protocol has been effective in setting the ozone layer on a recovery trajectory, but its ultimate success will be determined over the coming decades as its production and consumption controls continue to operate ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).
