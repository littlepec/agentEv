# The Montreal Protocol: Regulated Substances and Ozone Layer Recovery

## Introduction

The Montreal Protocol is a landmark international environmental agreement designed to protect the Earth’s ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). According to the UNEP Ozone Secretariat, the agreement is “a global agreement to protect the Earth’s ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it” and its “phase-out plan includes both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances” ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The same source states that “the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery” ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The United States Environmental Protection Agency adds that the original Montreal Protocol agreement was adopted in 1987, that developed countries were required to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993, and that the ozone layer is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The EPA also notes that the Kigali Amendment of 2016 extended controls to phase down the production and consumption of hydrofluorocarbons, or HFCs ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

Based on this evidence, the Montreal Protocol should be understood as an effective and adaptive global environmental agreement. It regulates not only the production but also the consumption of ozone-depleting substances, and it has already placed the ozone layer on a recovery trajectory ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). At the same time, the expectation that full healing will occur near the middle of the 21st century indicates that the protocol’s work is not finished ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The protocol is therefore best characterized as a durable, evolving framework rather than a completed project.

## What the Montreal Protocol Regulates

### Core Purpose and Scope

The Montreal Protocol’s central purpose is to protect the Earth’s ozone layer by phasing out chemicals that deplete it ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This purpose is achieved through a phase-out plan that covers both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The inclusion of both production and consumption is significant because it means the agreement does not merely target the use of harmful chemicals; it also addresses their manufacture and supply. In other words, the protocol is designed to reduce the availability of ozone-depleting substances as well as the demand for them ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

Because the Montreal Protocol is described as a global agreement, its regulatory scope is international rather than limited to a single country or region ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This global character is important for an atmospheric problem such as ozone depletion, because the ozone layer is shared across national boundaries. The protocol’s phase-out plan therefore represents a coordinated international response to a collective environmental risk ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

### The Original 1987 Agreement and CFCs

The original Montreal Protocol agreement was concluded in 1987 ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Under that original agreement, developed countries were required to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993 ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This provision established an early and concrete obligation for developed countries, and it placed CFCs among the first classes of chemicals to be addressed under the treaty framework ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

The 1993 start date for developed-country CFC phase-outs also shows that the Montreal Protocol did not simply announce a general goal. It created a timed schedule for action ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). That schedule applied to developed countries under the original agreement, while the broader phase-out plan covered both production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The combination of a defined target, a specific class of chemicals, and a phase-out timeline illustrates how the protocol translates environmental concern into regulatory action ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

### The Kigali Amendment and HFCs

The Montreal Protocol has also been updated over time. The Kigali Amendment of 2016 extended controls to phase down the production and consumption of HFCs ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This amendment is important because it expanded the protocol’s regulatory reach beyond the original set of controlled substances. The Kigali Amendment thus demonstrates that the Montreal Protocol is not a static agreement; it can be revised to address additional substances through new control measures ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

By extending controls to HFCs, the Kigali Amendment added a new phase-down obligation covering both production and consumption ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This is consistent with the protocol’s broader approach of regulating the supply and demand sides of targeted substances, as described by the UNEP Ozone Secretariat ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The amendment therefore strengthens the argument that the Montreal Protocol functions as an evolving instrument capable of responding to new scientific and policy priorities ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

## Effects on the Ozone Layer

### Recovery Is Underway

The most direct effect of the Montreal Protocol, according to the UNEP Ozone Secretariat, is that “the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery” ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This statement indicates that the protocol’s phase-out plan has had a positive and measurable influence on the state of the ozone layer. The recovery is not described as merely hypothetical or expected in the distant future; it is described as already underway ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

This recovery is closely connected to the protocol’s regulation of both production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). By reducing the manufacture and use of these substances, the agreement lowers the ongoing release of chemicals that damage the ozone layer. The UNEP Ozone Secretariat’s assessment that the ozone layer is “well on its way to recovery” therefore supports the conclusion that the protocol has been effective in its primary objective ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

### Full Healing Is Expected Near the Middle of the 21st Century

Although recovery is underway, the ozone layer is not yet fully healed. The EPA states that “the ozone layer is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century” ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This timeline shows that the protocol’s effects unfold over many decades. The phase-out of CFCs began for developed countries in 1993, and full healing is not expected until roughly the middle of the 21st century ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The long interval between policy action and full environmental recovery reflects the persistence of atmospheric processes and the time required for the ozone layer to restore itself ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

The distinction between “well on its way to recovery” and “fully healed” is important ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). It means that the protocol has produced meaningful progress but that the ozone layer remains in a recovery phase. The EPA’s projection of full healing near the middle of the 21st century provides a benchmark for evaluating the protocol’s long-term success ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). It also implies that continued implementation of the protocol’s controls is necessary to reach that endpoint.

## Assessment and Significance

### The Protocol Has Been Effective

Based on the provided information, the Montreal Protocol has been effective in addressing ozone depletion. The UNEP Ozone Secretariat states that the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery, which is a direct indication that the phase-out of ozone-depleting substances is producing positive results ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The EPA’s projection that the ozone layer will be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century further supports the conclusion that the protocol’s controls are working as intended ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

This effectiveness is notable because the protocol addresses a global atmospheric problem through coordinated international regulation. It targets both production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances, and it began with concrete obligations for developed countries to phase out CFCs starting in 1993 ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The result is not merely a policy commitment but a documented environmental recovery trend ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

### The Protocol Is Adaptive

The Kigali Amendment of 2016 demonstrates that the Montreal Protocol can adapt to new regulatory targets. By extending controls to phase down the production and consumption of HFCs, the amendment broadened the protocol’s scope beyond its original focus ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This capacity for amendment is significant because environmental challenges can change over time, and successful agreements must be able to incorporate new scientific and policy developments ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

The Kigali Amendment also reinforces the protocol’s basic regulatory model: it phases down both production and consumption of the targeted substances ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). That model was already present in the original phase-out plan for ozone-depleting substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The protocol’s ability to apply this model to HFCs suggests that it is a flexible framework rather than a single-issue agreement frozen in 1987 ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

### The Protocol Is Not Yet Complete

Despite its successes, the Montreal Protocol’s work is not finished. Full healing of the ozone layer is expected near the middle of the 21st century, which means the environmental benefit is still unfolding ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The fact that the ozone layer is only “well on its way to recovery” rather than fully recovered indicates that continued implementation and vigilance are necessary ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The protocol’s long timeline therefore argues against complacency.

Moreover, the expansion of controls to HFCs through the Kigali Amendment shows that the protocol’s agenda can grow over time ([US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This suggests that the agreement’s significance lies not only in its original CFC phase-out but also in its ongoing capacity to regulate additional substances. The protocol’s effectiveness is thus both demonstrated and contingent on continued international cooperation ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

### Concrete Opinion

In my assessment, the Montreal Protocol is one of the most significant environmental agreements because it combines a clear regulatory target with measurable atmospheric results. It regulates the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances, began phasing out CFCs in developed countries in 1993, and later extended controls to HFCs through the Kigali Amendment ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The ozone layer is now well on its way to recovery, and full healing is expected near the middle of the 21st century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This evidence supports the conclusion that the protocol has been effective, although its long recovery timeline means that success depends on sustained compliance and further adaptation.

The Montreal Protocol is a global agreement to protect the Earth’s ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The effect on the ozone layer has been positive: the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery, and it is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2026](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, 2026](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).
