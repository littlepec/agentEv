# The Montreal Protocol: Regulated Substances and Ozone Layer Recovery

## Introduction

The Montreal Protocol is a global environmental agreement designed to protect the Earth’s ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it, and its phase-out plan includes controls on both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). According to the UNEP Ozone Secretariat, “the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery,” which indicates that the treaty has already contributed to measurable or projected improvement in stratospheric ozone ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency further states that “the ozone layer is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century,” suggesting that recovery is underway but not yet complete ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

This report summarizes what the Montreal Protocol regulates and its effect on the ozone layer. It draws on two authoritative sources: the UNEP Ozone Secretariat, which presents the treaty’s global scope and recovery status, and the US EPA, which provides key regulatory dates and the extension of controls under the Kigali Amendment. In my assessment, the evidence supports the conclusion that the Montreal Protocol is an effective and adaptive environmental treaty because it regulates both the supply and demand sides of ozone-depleting substances, has been amended to address additional chemicals, and is associated with a projected recovery of the ozone layer by the middle of the 21st century. At the same time, because full healing is expected only near mid-century, the treaty’s success should be understood as an ongoing process rather than a completed achievement.

## What the Montreal Protocol Regulates

### Core Objective: Phasing Out Ozone-Depleting Chemicals

The Montreal Protocol is fundamentally a treaty about chemicals. It is described as “a global agreement to protect the Earth’s ozone layer by phasing out the chemicals that deplete it” ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This definition matters because it identifies the treaty’s regulatory target: not the ozone layer directly, but the human-produced substances that damage it. The protocol therefore operates by controlling the chemicals that cause ozone depletion, with the expectation that reducing their release will allow the ozone layer to recover ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

The treaty’s scope is not limited to a single chemical or a single stage of the economic chain. According to UNEP, “this phase-out plan includes both the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances” ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). That dual focus is significant. It means the agreement addresses not only the use or consumption of ozone-depleting chemicals but also their manufacture. By covering both production and consumption, the protocol creates a more comprehensive framework for reducing the amount of ozone-depleting substances that can enter the atmosphere ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

### The Original 1987 Agreement and CFC Controls

The original Montreal Protocol agreement was concluded in 1987. Under that original agreement, developed countries were required to begin phasing out chlorofluorocarbons, or CFCs, in 1993 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This date is important because it marks the beginning of a formal, binding phase-out process for a major class of ozone-depleting chemicals. The EPA’s phrasing—that developed countries were required to “begin” phasing out CFCs in 1993—indicates that the original protocol established a schedule rather than an immediate ban ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

The 1987 agreement therefore created the initial regulatory architecture. It identified CFCs as a target and set developed countries on a path toward phasing them out beginning in 1993 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). At the same time, UNEP’s description clarifies that the protocol’s broader phase-out plan covers ozone-depleting substances generally, and includes both production and consumption ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The original CFC provision is thus best understood as an initial step within a wider and later-expanded treaty framework.

### The Kigali Amendment (2016) and HFCs

A major development came with the Kigali Amendment in 2016. According to the EPA, “the Kigali Amendment (2016) extended controls to phase down the production and consumption of hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs)” ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This amendment expanded the protocol’s regulatory reach beyond its original focus on CFCs. It also repeated the dual structure of control: production and consumption were both subject to the new phase-down measures ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

The Kigali Amendment is significant for two reasons. First, it shows that the Montreal Protocol is not a static agreement. It can be amended to address additional substances as scientific and policy understanding develops ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). Second, it demonstrates that the treaty’s basic regulatory model—controlling production and consumption—can be extended to new chemical classes ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The EPA’s use of the term “phase down” rather than “phase out” for HFCs also indicates that the Kigali Amendment introduced a differentiated control approach, though the source does not provide a detailed schedule for that phase-down ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

## Effect on the Ozone Layer

### Current Status: Well on the Way to Recovery

The most direct statement about the protocol’s effect comes from UNEP, which states that “the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery” ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This is a positive assessment, but it is also carefully worded. “Well on its way” does not mean fully healed. It suggests that the ozone layer is improving or is expected to improve, but that the recovery process is still in progress ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The statement therefore supports the view that the Montreal Protocol has had a beneficial effect, while also implying that continued implementation is necessary.

### Projected Full Healing by the Middle of the 21st Century

The EPA provides a longer time horizon: “the ozone layer is expected to be fully healed near the middle of the 21st century” ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). This projection is important because it shows that the protocol’s effect is not instantaneous. The treaty was adopted in 1987, CFC phase-out began in developed countries in 1993, and the Kigali Amendment was adopted in 2016; yet full recovery is not expected until around the middle of the 21st century ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). That long timeline reflects the nature of atmospheric recovery, which depends on the gradual decline of ozone-depleting substances and the slow healing of the ozone layer.

The two sources together present a coherent picture. UNEP states that recovery is underway, while the EPA states that full healing is expected near mid-century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). In my assessment, this combination is significant because it indicates that the protocol is not merely aspirational. It is associated with a recovery trajectory that credible environmental authorities describe as progressing toward full healing by around the middle of the 21st century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

## Assessment and Opinion

### The Protocol’s Dual Controls Are a Major Strength

In my assessment, one of the Montreal Protocol’s most important design features is its control of both production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). If the treaty regulated only consumption, production could potentially continue for other markets or uses. By including both production and consumption, the protocol addresses the supply of ozone-depleting chemicals as well as the demand for them ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This makes the phase-out plan more comprehensive and likely more effective than a narrowly consumption-focused approach would be.

### The Kigali Amendment Shows Adaptive Governance

The Kigali Amendment of 2016 is also strong evidence that the Montreal Protocol is adaptive. The original agreement focused on CFCs, but the Kigali Amendment extended controls to HFCs by requiring a phase-down of their production and consumption ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). In my opinion, this ability to expand the treaty’s scope is a decisive factor in its long-term relevance. A treaty that could not be updated would become less useful as new chemicals and new scientific information emerged. The Kigali Amendment demonstrates that the Montreal Protocol can evolve to address additional substances within its framework ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

### Global Participation and Transboundary Environmental Protection

UNEP describes the Montreal Protocol as “a global agreement” ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This global character is important because ozone depletion is a transboundary problem. Chemicals released in one region can affect the stratospheric ozone layer that protects the entire planet. A purely national or regional approach would be less effective because production or consumption could shift to areas outside the agreement. The Montreal Protocol’s global scope therefore matches the global nature of the problem it addresses ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The EPA’s title and content also frame the issue as one of international treaties and cooperation, reinforcing that collective action is central to protecting stratospheric ozone ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

### Recovery Is Progress, Not Completion

Despite the positive recovery outlook, the EPA’s projection that full healing will occur near the middle of the 21st century means that success is still ahead ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). UNEP’s statement that the ozone layer is “well on its way to recovery” similarly implies that the process is ongoing ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). In my view, the Montreal Protocol should therefore be judged as a long-term success in progress. It has changed the trajectory of ozone depletion, but it has not yet finished the job. Continued compliance with the original protocol, the CFC phase-out, and the Kigali Amendment will be necessary to ensure that the projected full healing actually occurs ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

## Conclusion

The Montreal Protocol is a global agreement that regulates ozone-depleting chemicals by phasing out their production and consumption ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Under the original 1987 agreement, developed countries were required to begin phasing out CFCs in 1993 ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The 2016 Kigali Amendment extended controls to phase down the production and consumption of HFCs ([US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)). The effect on the ozone layer is positive but incomplete: UNEP states that the ozone layer is well on its way to recovery, while the EPA expects full healing near the middle of the 21st century ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).

In my assessment, the evidence supports a clear conclusion: the Montreal Protocol is an effective and adaptive treaty because it controls both production and consumption, has been extended to new chemicals through the Kigali Amendment, and is associated with a credible recovery trajectory for the ozone layer. Its main limitation is time. Full recovery is still decades away, so the treaty’s ultimate success depends on sustained global implementation ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, n.d.](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation-about-protection-stratospheric-ozone)).
