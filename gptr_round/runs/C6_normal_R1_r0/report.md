## Introduction

The Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer was adopted on 16 September 1987 and entered into force on 1 January 1989 ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.-a](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). It has been ratified by 197 UN member states plus the European Union — 198 Parties in total — making it the only environmental treaty to have achieved universal participation ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.-a](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). The Protocol is a protocol to the 1985 Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer, which established a framework for cooperation and information exchange but contained no binding controls on substances ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.-b](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/vienna-convention)). Its design — a ratcheting set of control measures, differentiated obligations, a dedicated financial mechanism, and a recurring scientific assessment process — distinguishes it from most multilateral environmental agreements and is central to explaining its measured results.

## The Scientific Foundation

### The Rowland–Molina Hypothesis

The Protocol rests on a mechanistic claim first advanced in 1974. Molina and Rowland proposed that chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), being chemically inert and volatile, would survive transport to the stratosphere, where ultraviolet radiation would photolyse them, releasing chlorine atoms that catalytically destroy ozone ([Molina & Rowland, 1974](https://www.nature.com/articles/249810a0)). The catalytic cycle is efficient: a single chlorine atom can destroy many thousands of ozone molecules before being removed from the stratosphere, and bromine compounds are substantially more destructive per atom still ([WMO/UNEP, 2022](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)).

### Discovery of the Antarctic Ozone Hole

Direct observational confirmation came in 1985, when Farman, Gardiner, and Shanklin of the British Antarctic Survey reported a roughly 40 percent depletion of springtime total column ozone over Halley Bay, Antarctica — a phenomenon not predicted by any model of the time ([Farman et al., 1985](https://www.nature.com/articles/315207a0)). The finding converted a contested hypothesis into a regulatory emergency and supplied the political momentum for the 1987 negotiations. Ozone is measured in Dobson Units (DU); the global mean total column is approximately 300 DU, and the conventionally defined "ozone hole" is the region where the column falls below 220 DU ([NASA Ozone Watch, n.d.](https://ozonewatch.gsfc.nasa.gov/)).

## What the Protocol Regulates

The Protocol does not regulate substances in a single instrument. It is amended, and each amendment adds or tightens controls. The controlled substances are recorded in annexes, and the amendments are the operative mechanism.

*Sources: ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.-a](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol); [US EPA, 2024](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection)).*

The London Amendment (1990) added methyl chloroform and carbon tetrachloride, accelerated the CFC and halon schedules, and created the Multilateral Fund. The Copenhagen Amendment (1992) added methyl bromide and HBFCs and set firm phase-out dates. The Montreal Amendment (1997) established the methyl bromide schedules and required parties to operate import and export licensing systems. The Beijing Amendment (1999) added bromochloromethane and introduced controls on HCFC production, not merely consumption ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.-a](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

### The Kigali Amendment and Hydrofluorocarbons

The Kigali Amendment (2016) is structurally different from its predecessors because hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) are not ozone-depleting. HFCs were the principal substitutes for CFCs and HCFCs, and are potent greenhouse gases; the Kigali Amendment adds Annex F to control them under the Protocol's institutional machinery as a climate measure ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2016](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol/amendments/kigali-amendment-2016-amendment-montreal-protocol)). It entered into force on 1 January 2019.

|---|---|---|---|
| Non-Article 5 | 2011–2013 HFC average + 15% of 1989 HCFC baseline | 2019 | 85% below baseline by 2036 |
| Article 5, Group 1 | 2020–2022 HFC average + 65% of HCFC baseline | 2024 | 80% below baseline by 2045 |
| Article 5, Group 2 | 2024–2026 HFC average + 65% of HCFC baseline | 2028 | 85% below baseline by 2047 |

*Source: ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, 2016](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol/amendments/kigali-amendment-2016-amendment-montreal-protocol)).*

Intermediate non-Article 5 steps are 90 percent of baseline in 2024, 60 percent in 2029, 30 percent in 2034, and 15 percent from 2036. Article 5 Group 2 comprises a defined list of high-ambient-temperature countries for which technically feasible alternatives arrived later.

## Governance, Finance, and Compliance

### The Multilateral Fund

The London Amendment created the Multilateral Fund to meet the agreed incremental costs of compliance in Article 5 Parties. It is administered by a 14-member Executive Committee and implemented through UNEP, UNDP, UNIDO, and the World Bank. The Fund has approved more than US$4 billion in activities since 1991, including funding for the phase-out of CFC production facilities in China and other large producers ([Multilateral Fund, n.d.](https://www.multilateralfund.org/)). The Fund is widely identified as the mechanism that made differentiated obligations acceptable to developing countries.

### The Non-Compliance Procedure

The Protocol's non-compliance procedure and an Implementation Committee were established at the Fourth Meeting of the Parties in Copenhagen (1992) ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.-c](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol/non-compliance-procedure)). The Committee is non-judicial, investigative, and aimed at restoring compliance rather than punishing it. This design has kept non-compliance cases few relative to the treaty's 198 Parties, and the Protocol operates a mandatory data-reporting system under Article 7.

## Effects on the Ozone Layer

### Observed Recovery

Measured outcomes support the conclusion that the Protocol is working. Global production and consumption of ODS have fallen by more than 99 percent relative to 1986 levels ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.-a](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Stratospheric halogen loading — the reservoir of chlorine and bromine available for catalytic ozone destruction — peaked in the late 1990s and has been declining since ([WMO/UNEP, 2022](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)). The largest single-day Antarctic ozone hole extent recorded was approximately 29.9 million km² on 9 September 2000; the smallest in the satellite era was roughly 16.4 million km² in 2019, while 2020 produced one of the deepest and longest-lasting holes on record ([NASA Ozone Watch, n.d.](https://ozonewatch.gsfc.nasa.gov/)).

The WMO/UNEP Scientific Assessment of Ozone Depletion: 2022 projects recovery of total column ozone to 1980 pre-hole benchmarks on the following schedule:

| Region | Projected return to 1980 values |
|---|---|
| Global (60°N–60°S) | ~2040 |
| Arctic | ~2045 |
| Antarctic | ~2066 |

*Source: ([WMO/UNEP, 2022](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)).*

### The Counterfactual

The strongest evidence for the Protocol's effect is comparative-modelling rather than observational. Newman et al. (2009) simulated a world without the Protocol and found that global ozone depletion by 2050 would have been roughly ten times greater than observed, with a near-permanent Antarctic hole and, by 2065, severe depletion across populated latitudes ([Newman et al., 2009](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/9/2113/2009/)). Public-health estimates follow from the UV-B exposure pathway: the U.S. EPA estimates the Protocol prevents 443 million skin cancer cases and 63 million cataract cases among people born in the United States between 1890 and 2100 ([US EPA, 2024](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection)).

## Climate Co-Benefits

ODS are also greenhouse gases, so phasing them out delivered climate benefits unintended by the original negotiators. Velders et al. (2007) estimated that the Protocol's ODS reductions avoided radiative forcing equivalent to roughly 10 Gt CO₂-eq per year, a magnitude larger than the annual reduction target of the Kyoto Protocol at the time ([Velders et al., 2007](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0702872104)). The Kigali Amendment adds a further, deliberate climate benefit: UNEP projects that HFC phase-down avoids 0.3–0.5 °C of warming by 2100 ([UNEP, 2023](https://www.unep.org/news-and-stories/press-release/ozone-layer-recovery-track-helping-avoid-global-warming)).

## Residual Risks and Caveats

Recovery is contingent on sustained compliance, and compliance has been imperfect. Montzka et al. (2018) reported an unexpected slowdown in the decline of atmospheric CFC-11 after 2012, and modelling traced a substantial share of the excess emissions to eastern China; reported emissions subsequently declined ([Montzka et al., 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0106-2)). The episode is significant because it was detected through atmospheric monitoring rather than through the Protocol's self-reporting system, and because CFC production for feedstock use is difficult to distinguish from emissive use. Additional uncertainties include the influence of stratospheric cooling, volcanic water-vapour injection, and future HFC substitute choices on recovery timing ([WMO/UNEP, 2022](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)).

## Analytical Assessment

My assessment is that the Montreal Protocol's success is attributable less to the stringency of its original 1987 targets than to four structural features: (1) a ratchet mechanism that allowed controls to be tightened as scientific evidence firmed and substitute technologies matured; (2) differentiated obligations paired with a funded compliance mechanism, which removed the equity objection that has stalled other agreements; (3) an institutionalised science–policy feedback loop, in which quadrennial assessments feed directly into Meetings of the Parties; and (4) a non-punitive compliance regime that prioritised restoring compliance over sanctioning. Conversely, the CFC-11 anomaly demonstrates a genuine structural weakness: the regime's detection capability rests substantially on non-party monitoring networks, and enforcement responses to deliberate circumvention remain slow relative to the damage timescale. A defensible conclusion is that the Montreal Protocol is the most effective environmental treaty on record on measured outcomes, that its architecture is exportable, but that its compliance assurance is weaker than its compliance record suggests and should not be assumed to transfer automatically to the Kigali Amendment's HFC controls, where the economic incentives to evade are shaped by very different refrigerant markets.

## Conclusion

The Montreal Protocol regulates the production and consumption of CFCs, halons, carbon tetrachloride, methyl chloroform, methyl bromide, HBFCs, bromochloromethane, HCFCs, and — since the Kigali Amendment — HFCs. It has achieved universal ratification by 198 Parties and reduced ODS production and consumption by more than 99 percent relative to 1986 ([UNEP Ozone Secretariat, n.d.-a](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). Stratospheric halogen loading has declined since its late-1990s peak, and total column ozone is projected to return to 1980 benchmarks around 2040 globally, 2045 in the Arctic, and 2066 over Antarctica ([WMO/UNEP, 2022](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)). The treaty's effect on the ozone layer is therefore both directionally and quantitatively established, while remaining contingent on continued compliance and on monitoring that can detect deliberate departures from it.
