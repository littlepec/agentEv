# The Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer: Regulatory Scope and Its Effect on the Ozone Layer

## Introduction

The Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer is the international treaty that governs the production, consumption, and trade of chemicals capable of destroying stratospheric ozone. It was adopted on 16 September 1987 and entered into force on 1 January 1989, operating under the framework of the 1985 Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer ([UNEP, n.d.-b](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). As of the present date the treaty has 198 parties — 197 states plus the European Union — making it the first environmental agreement in United Nations history to achieve universal ratification ([UNEP, n.d.-b](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

The Protocol is widely regarded as the most effective multilateral environmental agreement ever concluded, having phased out roughly 99 per cent of the ozone-depleting substances (ODS) it controls ([UNEP, n.d.-b](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)). This report summarises what the Protocol regulates, how its control architecture operates, and what the scientific evidence shows about its effect on the ozone layer.

## The Scientific Foundation of the Treaty

The Protocol rests on two bodies of evidence. The first is the theoretical work of Molina and Rowland, who demonstrated in 1974 that chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), chemically inert in the lower atmosphere, could survive transport to the stratosphere, where ultraviolet radiation would liberate chlorine atoms capable of catalytically destroying ozone in repeating cycles ([Molina & Rowland, 1974](https://doi.org/10.1038/249810a0)). A single chlorine atom can destroy tens of thousands of ozone molecules before being removed, which is why relatively small concentrations of ODS produce disproportionate ozone loss.

The second is the empirical discovery of the Antarctic "ozone hole." Farman, Gardiner, and Shanklin reported in 1985 that total column ozone over Halley Bay, Antarctica, had fallen by more than 40 per cent during austral spring, far beyond what existing models predicted ([Farman et al., 1985](https://doi.org/10.1038/315207a0)). The mechanism — heterogeneous chemistry on the surfaces of polar stratospheric clouds that converts inactive chlorine reservoirs such as hydrogen chloride and chlorine nitrate into active forms, followed by rapid catalytic ozone destruction inside the polar vortex — is now well established and underpins the timing of the Protocol's control schedules ([WMO, 2023](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)).

## What the Montreal Protocol Regulates

### The Original Scope (1987)

The original treaty, as adopted in 1987, controlled a limited set of substances listed in Annex A:

- **Group I — CFCs:** CFC-11, CFC-12, CFC-113, CFC-114 and CFC-115, with an ozone-depleting potential (ODP) of 0.8–1.0.
- **Group II — Halons:** halon-1211, halon-1301 and halon-2402, with ODPs ranging from 3 to 10, making them the most destructive class of substances ever controlled.

These substances were used primarily as refrigerants, aerosol propellants, foam-blowing agents, solvents, and fire-extinguishing agents. The initial obligations were modest: a freeze at 1986 consumption levels followed by a 50 per cent reduction in CFC consumption by 1999 ([UNEP, n.d.-b](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

### Expansion through Amendments and Adjustments

A distinguishing feature of the Protocol is its ability to be strengthened without renegotiating the entire text. "Adjustments" to control schedules automatically bind all parties; "amendments" add new substances and must be ratified. Through successive meetings of the parties, the treaty's scope expanded to approximately 100 controlled substances across nine annexes ([UNEP, n.d.-b](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)):

| Instrument | Year | Principal additions or changes |
|---|---|---|
| Montreal Protocol | 1987 | CFCs and halons; freeze and 50 per cent CFC reduction |
| London Amendment | 1990 | Carbon tetrachloride, methyl chloroform, HCFCs; established the Multilateral Fund |
| Copenhagen Amendment | 1992 | Methyl bromide, HBFCs; accelerated CFC phase-out to 1996 |
| Vienna Adjustment | 1995 | Tightened methyl bromide schedule |
| Montreal Amendment | 1997 | Licensing systems for imports and exports of ODS |
| Beijing Amendment | 1999 | Bromochloromethane; controls on HCFC trade |
| Kigali Amendment | 2016 | Phase-down of hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) |

### From Ozone Depletion to Climate Protection: The Kigali Amendment

The Kigali Amendment is the most significant expansion of the treaty since 1992. It targets hydrofluorocarbons, which replaced CFCs and HCFCs in refrigeration and air conditioning but are potent greenhouse gases rather than ozone-depleting substances — HFC-134a has a 100-year global warming potential (GWP) of roughly 1,300–1,500, and HFC-23 of approximately 14,800 ([IPCC, 2021](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/)). Because HFCs have zero ODP, they lay outside the Protocol's original ozone mandate; Kigali brings them within it on climate grounds. UNEP estimates that full implementation of the Kigali Amendment could avoid up to 0.5 °C of global warming by 2100 ([UNEP, n.d.-a](https://www.unep.org/topics/ozone-and-ozone-depletion/about-kigali-amendment)).

The Kigali phase-down schedule differentiates parties into three groups:

| Group | Baseline | Freeze | Reduction milestones |
|---|---|---|---|
| Non-Article 5 (developed) | 2011–2013 average | 2019 | 10% by 2019; 40% by 2024; 70% by 2029; 80% by 2034; 85% by 2036 |
| Article 5 Group 1 (most developing countries) | 2020–2022 average + 65% of HCFC baseline | 2024 | 10% by 2029; 30% by 2035; 50% by 2040; 80% by 2045 |
| Article 5 Group 2 (high-ambient-temperature countries) | As above | 2028 | 10% by 2032; 20% by 2037; 30% by 2042; 80% by 2047 |

([UNEP, n.d.-a](https://www.unep.org/topics/ozone-and-ozone-depletion/about-kigali-amendment))

### Phase-Out Schedules for Ozone-Depleting Substances

The core ozone-related obligations distinguish between developed parties (non-Article 5) and developing parties (Article 5), the latter receiving a grace period and financial support:

| Substance group | Developed parties: complete phase-out | Article 5 parties: complete phase-out |
|---|---|---|
| CFCs (Annex A/I) | 1996 | 2010 |
| Halons (Annex A/II) | 1994 | 2010 |
| Carbon tetrachloride | 1996 | 2010 |
| Methyl chloroform | 1996 | 2015 |
| Methyl bromide | 2005 | 2015 |
| HCFCs | 2030 (with limited servicing allowance to 2030) | 2040 |

([UNEP, n.d.-b](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol))

### Compliance Architecture

The Protocol's effectiveness derives less from its targets than from the machinery supporting them. Article 5 grants developing parties delayed schedules and access to the Multilateral Fund, established in 1991, which has disbursed more than US$4 billion to finance technology transfer, conversion of industrial facilities, and institutional strengthening in developing countries ([Multilateral Fund, n.d.](http://www.multilateralfund.org/aboutMLF/default.aspx)). Article 4 restricts trade in controlled substances with non-parties, creating an incentive for universal participation. Article 4B requires parties to operate import and export licensing systems, and Article 7 mandates annual reporting of production, consumption, and trade data. A dedicated non-compliance procedure, together with the Scientific, Technology and Economic, and Environmental Effects Assessment Panels, gives the treaty a permanent, evidence-based review mechanism ([UNEP, n.d.-b](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol)).

## Effect on the Ozone Layer

### Observed Trends

The Protocol's effect on the atmosphere is measurable. Atmospheric concentrations of the most abundant ODS peaked in the stratosphere in the late 1990s and have declined steadily since; the 2022 Scientific Assessment of Ozone Depletion concluded that the decline in stratospheric chlorine and bromine is "unequivocally attributable" to the Protocol's controls ([WMO, 2023](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)). Signs of ozone recovery — an increase in total column ozone in the upper stratosphere and a slow reduction in the size and depth of the Antarctic ozone hole since around 2000 — have been documented across multiple independent datasets ([WMO, 2023](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)).

Individual years remain variable because ozone loss is strongly modulated by stratospheric temperature and polar vortex dynamics. The Antarctic ozone hole of 2024, for example, was ranked the seventh smallest since recovery began in 1992, reflecting a warmer-than-average stratosphere rather than a departure from the long-term trend ([NASA, 2024](https://www.nasa.gov/earth/nasa-noaa-rank-2024-ozone-hole-as-7th-smallest-since-recovery-began/)).

### Projected Recovery

Assuming continued compliance, the 2022 assessment projects a return of ozone to 1980 benchmark levels as follows:

| Region | Expected return to 1980 levels |
|---|---|
| Global average (60°S–60°N) | around 2040 |
| Arctic | around 2045 |
| Antarctic | around 2066 |

([WMO, 2023](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/))

The Antarctic recovery date is latest because the polar vortex and polar stratospheric clouds sustain efficient chlorine activation for as long as chlorine and bromine remain elevated, which is why the ozone hole is expected to continue forming each spring until roughly the 2060s ([WMO, 2023](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)).

### The Counterfactual: What the Protocol Averted

The clearest demonstration of the Protocol's value is the "world avoided." Modelling studies indicate that without regulation, global ozone depletion would have intensified dramatically through the twenty-first century, with severe ozone loss extending well beyond polar regions and ultraviolet radiation at mid-latitudes roughly doubling by the 2060s ([Newman et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.5194/acp-9-2113-2009)). The associated consequences would have included substantially elevated rates of skin cancer and cataracts, damage to terrestrial vegetation and agricultural yields, and disruption of marine phytoplankton at the base of ocean food webs ([US EPA, 2024](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation)). The US Environmental Protection Agency estimates that the Protocol will prevent approximately two million cases of skin cancer annually by 2030 relative to an uncontrolled scenario ([US EPA, 2024](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/international-treaties-and-cooperation)).

### Climate Co-Benefits

Because CFCs and HCFCs are also potent greenhouse gases, the Protocol has delivered climate mitigation as a by-product of ozone protection. Velders and colleagues estimated that the reduction in radiative forcing achieved by the Montreal Protocol exceeded the emission reductions targeted by the first commitment period of the Kyoto Protocol, effectively delaying climate change by roughly a decade ([Velders et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0610328104)). The Kigali Amendment is projected to add substantial further mitigation, in addition to avoiding up to 0.5 °C of warming by 2100 ([UNEP, n.d.-a](https://www.unep.org/topics/ozone-and-ozone-depletion/about-kigali-amendment)).

## Challenges and Remaining Risks

Four issues temper the Protocol's success record.

**Nitrous oxide.** The 2022 assessment identifies nitrous oxide (N₂O) as the largest remaining anthropogenic emission that depletes ozone, and projects that it will remain so for the rest of the century. N₂O is not controlled by the Protocol because it is not an industrial ODS but a by-product of agriculture and combustion, meaning that the treaty's full recovery projections are contingent on emissions it does not regulate ([WMO, 2023](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)).

**Non-compliance and rogue emissions.** In 2018, researchers detected an unexpected and persistent rise in global CFC-11 emissions after 2012 from eastern Asia, in breach of the global phase-out ([Montzka et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-018-0106-2)). Subsequent monitoring showed the emissions declined markedly after 2019, but the episode demonstrated both the vulnerability of a production ban to enforcement failures and the value of atmospheric measurement networks as an independent verification tool ([WMO, 2023](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)).

**Banks and feedstock uses.** Large quantities of ODS remain contained in existing refrigeration, foam, and fire-protection equipment. Leakage from these "banks" continues to release controlled substances long after production has ended, sustaining stratospheric chlorine loading and slowing recovery ([WMO, 2023](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)).

**Climate–ozone interactions.** Greenhouse-gas-driven changes in stratospheric temperature and circulation affect ozone recovery rates, and the Kigali Amendment's benefits depend on the availability of low-GWP substitutes and effective management of their energy efficiency and safety profiles ([WMO, 2023](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)).

## Assessment

The Montreal Protocol's record supports a clear conclusion: it is the most demonstrably successful instrument of international environmental governance in existence, and its success is attributable to a specific institutional design rather than to favourable circumstances. Three features appear decisive. First, the treaty is built on a formal scientific assessment process — the quadrennial Scientific Assessment of Ozone Depletion — that gives the parties a shared, authoritative evidentiary basis and legitimises the ratcheting-up of obligations; the 1990 London and 1992 Copenhagen Amendments, which moved phase-out dates forward from 1999 to 1996, would have been politically implausible without that common factual foundation ([WMO, 2023](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)). Second, the treaty institutionalises differentiation: Article 5 parties receive both longer schedules and dedicated finance through the Multilateral Fund, which converts formally universal obligations into practically achievable ones ([Multilateral Fund, n.d.](http://www.multilateralfund.org/aboutMLF/default.aspx)). Third, control measures are tied to independent atmospheric monitoring and a non-compliance procedure, which together allowed the CFC-11 breach to be detected and, apparently, brought under control ([Montzka et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-018-0106-2)).

The honest caveat is that the Protocol's own science now defines the limit of its reach. Because it exercises jurisdiction over molar industrial substances, it cannot address nitrous oxide, which the 2022 assessment identifies as the dominant remaining ozone-depleting emission, nor can it directly regulate the climate dynamics that modulate recovery timing ([WMO, 2023](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/)). Full restoration of the ozone layer therefore depends on policy domains beyond the treaty — agricultural emissions management and climate mitigation foremost among them. Furthermore, the projected recovery dates of roughly 2040 globally and about 2066 over Antarctica are conditional forecasts, not guarantees; they presuppose sustained compliance, continued financing of the Multilateral Fund, and prompt ratification and implementation of the Kigali Amendment by all parties ([UNEP, n.d.-a](https://www.unep.org/topics/ozone-and-ozone-depletion/about-kigali-amendment)).

In short, the Montreal Protocol regulates approximately 100 ozone-depleting substances across nine annexes and, since 2016, a further class of potent greenhouse gases. Its effect on the ozone layer is twofold: it has arrested and begun to reverse the growth of stratospheric chlorine and bromine, and it has placed the global ozone layer on a trajectory toward 1980 levels within the second half of this century — an outcome that credible counterfactual modelling indicates would not otherwise have occurred, and one whose completion now depends on the treaty's ability to sustain compliance while other environmental stressors are addressed.
