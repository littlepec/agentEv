The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is NASA’s flagship infrared space observatory, developed with the European Space Agency and the Canadian Space Agency. It is designed to observe the universe in infrared light, from about 0.6 to 28 micrometers, and is optimized to study the early universe, galaxy formation, star and planet formation, and exoplanet atmospheres. It launched on 25 December 2021 and operates about 1.5 million km from Earth at the Sun–Earth Lagrange point L2. [1][2]

## Design

- **Primary mirror:** 6.5-meter-diameter segmented mirror made of 18 gold-coated beryllium hexagonal segments. The segments can be individually adjusted in orbit to keep the mirror precisely aligned. Collecting area is about 25.4 m². [1][3]
- **Optics:** The telescope uses a three-mirror anastigmat design, with a secondary mirror on a tripod and a fine-steering mirror for precise pointing. It is optimized for infrared wavelengths and must be kept extremely cold to reduce its own infrared glow. [1][3]
- **Sunshield:** A five-layer, tennis-court-sized Kapton sunshield about 21 m × 14 m keeps the telescope and instruments shaded from the Sun, Earth, and Moon. This allows the telescope to cool passively to below about 50 K, with most instruments below 40 K. [1][3]
- **MIRI cooling:** The Mid-Infrared Instrument requires additional cooling to about 7 K, provided by a mechanical cryocooler. [1][4]
- **Orbit:** JWST orbits L2, not Earth. This keeps the Sun, Earth, and Moon on one side of the sunshield and provides a stable thermal environment. [1][2]
- **Launch and mass:** It launched on an Ariane 5 rocket, with a mass of about 6.5 tonnes, and deployed its mirror and sunshield in a complex sequence after launch. [1]

## Main instruments

JWST has four main science instruments, plus a fine guidance sensor:

1. **NIRCam — Near-Infrared Camera**
   - Wavelength range: about 0.6–5.0 µm.
   - Provides deep infrared imaging and coronagraphy. It also plays a key role in wavefront sensing and mirror alignment. [3][4]

2. **NIRSpec — Near-Infrared Spectrograph**
   - Wavelength range: about 0.6–5.3 µm.
   - Measures spectra to determine chemical composition, temperature, and velocity. It uses a microshutter array to observe up to about 100 objects simultaneously, and also supports integral-field and fixed-slit spectroscopy. [3][4]

3. **MIRI — Mid-Infrared Instrument**
   - Wavelength range: about 5–28 µm.
   - Contains a camera, coronagraphs, a low-resolution spectrometer, and a medium-resolution integral-field spectrograph. It is especially important for studying cool objects, dust-enshrouded regions, and highly redshifted early galaxies. [3][4]

4. **NIRISS — Near-Infrared Imager and Slitless Spectrograph**
   - Wavelength range: about 0.8–5.0 µm.
   - Supports slitless spectroscopy, exoplanet transit spectroscopy, imaging, and aperture-masking interferometry. [3][4]

5. **FGS — Fine Guidance Sensor**
   - Provides precise pointing and guiding for the observatory. It is integrated with NIRISS in the same instrument module. [3][4]

## Purpose

JWST’s main scientific goals are to:

- Observe the first galaxies and the era of reionization, when the universe was only a few hundred million years old.
- Study how galaxies assemble and evolve over cosmic time.
- Investigate star formation, protoplanetary disks, and the life cycles of stars.
- Characterize exoplanet atmospheres and search for potential biosignatures.
- Observe objects in our own solar system, including planets, moons, comets, and asteroids. [1][2][3]

Infrared is essential for these goals because the expansion of the universe stretches light from distant objects into infrared wavelengths, and because cool objects such as planets and dust clouds emit mostly infrared light. JWST is not simply a replacement for Hubble; it is a complementary observatory that extends Hubble’s reach into the infrared and works alongside other missions such as Hubble and Spitzer. [1][2]
