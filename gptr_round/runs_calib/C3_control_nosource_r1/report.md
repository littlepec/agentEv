The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is a large infrared observatory built by NASA with ESA and CSA, launched on 25 December 2021 and beginning science observations in 2022. It is designed to see farther and deeper into the infrared than Hubble, and to study the early universe, galaxy formation, stars and planets, and exoplanet atmospheres.

## Design

- **Primary mirror:** 6.5 m diameter, made of 18 hexagonal gold-coated beryllium segments. It collects about 25 m² of light and folds for launch, then unfolds in space. The gold coating reflects infrared light well.
- **Sunshield:** A five-layer, tennis-court-sized Kapton sunshield about 21 m × 14 m. It keeps the telescope’s optics and instruments below about 50 K (−220 °C) by blocking heat from the Sun, Earth, and Moon.
- **Orbit:** JWST orbits the Sun–Earth Lagrange point 2 (L2), about 1.5 million km from Earth. This allows the sunshield to block sunlight continuously and provides a stable thermal environment.
- **Cooling:** The telescope is mostly passively cooled by the sunshield. The Mid-Infrared Instrument (MIRI) needs extra cooling and uses a mechanical cryocooler to reach about 7 K (−266 °C).
- **Wavelength range:** Roughly 0.6 to 28.5 micrometres, covering visible red through mid-infrared.

## Main instruments

1. **NIRCam — Near-Infrared Camera**
   - Wavelengths: about 0.6–5 µm.
   - JWST’s primary imager. It takes deep images of galaxies, stars, and planetary systems, and includes coronagraphs to block bright starlight so faint companions can be seen. It also helps align the telescope’s mirror segments.

2. **NIRSpec — Near-Infrared Spectrograph**
   - Wavelengths: about 0.6–5 µm.
   - Measures spectra to determine composition, temperature, and motion. Its microshutter array can observe many objects at once. It supports low-, medium-, and high-resolution spectroscopy, plus integral-field spectroscopy.

3. **MIRI — Mid-Infrared Instrument**
   - Wavelengths: about 5–28 µm.
   - Provides both imaging and spectroscopy/coronagraphy in the mid-infrared. It is especially important for studying cool objects, dust-obscured regions, and high-redshift galaxies. MIRI is contributed by NASA and ESA and requires active cooling.

4. **FGS/NIRISS — Fine Guidance Sensor / Near-Infrared Imager and Slitless Spectrograph**
   - Wavelengths: about 0.8–5 µm.
   - The Fine Guidance Sensor keeps JWST pointed precisely. NIRISS provides slitless spectroscopy for exoplanet atmospheres and other targets, plus imaging and wide-field spectroscopy.

## Purpose

JWST’s main goals are to:

- Observe the **first galaxies and stars** that formed after the Big Bang, and study how the universe became reionized.
- Trace **galaxy formation and evolution** over cosmic time.
- Study **star and planet formation**, including protostars, protoplanetary disks, and dust in the interstellar medium.
- Characterize **exoplanet atmospheres** through transit and eclipse spectroscopy, looking for molecules such as water, carbon dioxide, and methane.
- Observe **solar system objects** such as planets, moons, comets, and asteroids in infrared light.

Because infrared light penetrates dust and because the expansion of the universe stretches distant starlight into the infrared, JWST is uniquely suited to studying the distant, early universe and cool, dusty objects that Hubble and other observatories cannot see as well.
