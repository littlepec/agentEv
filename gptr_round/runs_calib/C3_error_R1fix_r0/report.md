# James Webb Space Telescope: Design, Instruments, and Purpose

## Introduction

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is a space-based astronomical observatory designed to observe the universe in infrared light. According to NASA, Webb launched on December 25, 2021, and it “orbits the Sun 1.5 million kilometers … away from the Earth at … the second Lagrange point or L2,” using a “segmented, gold-coated mirror” to observe in infrared ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). The Wikipedia entry on the telescope adds that Webb was “launched on 25 December 2021 on an Ariane 5 rocket from Kourou, French Guiana,” and that it “observes a lower frequency range, from long-wavelength visible light (red) through mid-infrared (0.6-28.5 um)” ([Wikipedia contributors, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). Together, these sources present Webb as a large, infrared-optimized space observatory with a complex mirror system and a four-instrument science payload. This report describes its design, main instruments, and purpose using only the provided source material, while also noting where the evidence is incomplete.

## Design and Architecture

### Launch and deployment context

The launch of Webb is one of the most clearly documented facts in the provided sources. NASA states that Webb launched on December 25, 2021 ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). Wikipedia specifies the vehicle and site: an Ariane 5 rocket from Kourou, French Guiana ([Wikipedia contributors, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). These two details are consistent: both sources agree on the date, while the Wikipedia entry supplies the launch vehicle and geographic origin. The launch date is therefore a reliable anchor for any discussion of the mission’s timeline.

### Orbit and observational location

Webb does not orbit Earth in the same manner as many earlier space telescopes. NASA describes its orbit as solar, with the observatory positioned about 1.5 million kilometers from Earth at the second Lagrange point, or L2 ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). The source does not explain the physical rationale for this location, but it does identify L2 as the mission’s orbital destination. For the purposes of this report, the key design fact is that Webb operates at a distant solar orbit rather than in low Earth orbit or geostationary orbit. The NASA description also links this location to the telescope’s infrared mission, though it does not provide a detailed thermal or engineering explanation ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)).

### Optical system and primary mirror

The optical design of Webb is centered on a large, segmented, gold-coated mirror. NASA describes the telescope as using a “segmented, gold-coated mirror” to observe in infrared ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). Wikipedia provides more specific technical figures: “Webb's primary mirror is a 6.5 m (21 ft)-diameter gold-coated beryllium reflector with a collecting area of 25.4 m2” ([Wikipedia contributors, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). These two descriptions are complementary. NASA identifies the mirror as segmented and gold-coated, while Wikipedia specifies its diameter, material, and collecting area. The primary mirror is therefore a 6.5-meter gold-coated beryllium reflector, and the overall mirror system is segmented. The 25.4-square-meter collecting area is a measure of how much light the telescope can gather, and it is one of the most important quantitative design specifications available in the provided sources.

### Wavelength coverage

Webb’s observational range is another defining design feature. Wikipedia states that Webb “observes a lower frequency range, from long-wavelength visible light (red) through mid-infrared (0.6-28.5 um)” ([Wikipedia contributors, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). This range begins at the red end of the visible spectrum and extends into the mid-infrared. In wavelength terms, 0.6 micrometers is at the long-wavelength edge of visible red light, while 28.5 micrometers is in the mid-infrared. The phrase “lower frequency range” is consistent with this: longer wavelengths correspond to lower frequencies. The source does not list separate wavelength bands for each instrument, nor does it explain why this particular range was chosen. What it does establish is that Webb is not a general visible-light telescope; its design is explicitly tied to infrared and long-wavelength visible observations.

These are the only instrument-level details provided in the source material. The NASA source does not list the instruments individually, nor does it describe their capabilities. Therefore, the most defensible statement is that Webb carries four named science instruments and that these instruments form the payload through which the telescope’s infrared observations are conducted.

## Purpose and Scientific Role

### Stated observational purpose

The clearest statement of purpose in the provided material is that Webb observes in infrared. NASA states that Webb uses a segmented, gold-coated mirror to observe in infrared ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). Wikipedia adds that its wavelength range extends from long-wavelength visible red through mid-infrared, from 0.6 to 28.5 micrometers ([Wikipedia contributors, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). Taken together, these statements define Webb’s purpose as infrared astronomy: it is built to collect and analyze infrared light from astronomical sources.

### Purpose inferred from design

The design features described by the sources are consistent with this purpose. The 6.5-meter primary mirror and 25.4-square-meter collecting area provide a large light-gathering surface ([Wikipedia contributors, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). The gold coating and beryllium material are part of the mirror’s construction ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/); [Wikipedia contributors, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). The L2 orbit places the observatory 1.5 million kilometers from Earth ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). The four instruments provide the scientific channels for observation ([Wikipedia contributors, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). A reasonable inference from these facts is that Webb is intended to perform infrared observations of celestial targets with a large aperture and a multi-instrument payload. However, the sources do not state specific mission goals such as cosmology, exoplanet characterization, or galaxy formation, so those goals should not be attributed to Webb on the basis of this evidence alone.

### Concrete assessment

A concrete and valid opinion based on the provided information is that Webb is best understood as a large, infrared-optimized space observatory. Its most consequential design feature is the combination of a 6.5-meter segmented gold-coated beryllium mirror, a 25.4-square-meter collecting area, and a wavelength range spanning 0.6 to 28.5 micrometers. This combination supports the conclusion that Webb was built to observe astronomical sources in infrared and long-wavelength visible light, and that its four instruments are the means by which those observations are carried out. The provided sources do not support claims about specific discoveries, sensitivity limits, or comparative superiority over other telescopes. They do, however, support a clear characterization of Webb’s purpose as infrared observation from a distant solar orbit.

A balanced approach is therefore to treat NASA as the primary source for mission status and orbital facts, and Wikipedia as a secondary but useful source for mirror and instrument specifications, while recognizing that Wikipedia is a tertiary source and would normally require confirmation from primary engineering or mission documentation.

## Conclusion

The James Webb Space Telescope, as described by the provided sources, is a space-based infrared observatory launched on December 25, 2021, on an Ariane 5 rocket from Kourou, French Guiana ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/); [Wikipedia contributors, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). It orbits the Sun 1.5 million kilometers from Earth at the second Lagrange point, L2 ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). Its primary mirror is a 6.5-meter gold-coated beryllium reflector with a collecting area of 25.4 square meters, and the overall mirror system is segmented and gold-coated ([NASA, n.d.](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/); [Wikipedia contributors, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). Webb observes from 0.6 to 28.5 micrometers, covering long-wavelength visible red through mid-infrared, and it carries four science instruments: NIRCam, NIRSpec, MIRI, and FGS/NIRISS ([Wikipedia contributors, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). Based on these facts, Webb’s purpose is best characterized as infrared observation using a large, segmented, gold-coated mirror and a multi-instrument science payload. The sources do not support more specific claims about its scientific goals or achievements, but they clearly establish its design, orbital location, wavelength range, and instrument manifest.
