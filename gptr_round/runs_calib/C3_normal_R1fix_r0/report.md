# The James Webb Space Telescope: Design, Instruments, and Purpose

## Introduction

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is a space-based astronomical observatory designed to observe the universe in the infrared portion of the electromagnetic spectrum. According to NASA, the telescope "launched on Dec. 25th 2021" and "orbits the Sun 1.5 million kilometers ... away from the Earth at ... the second Lagrange point or L2," where it uses a "segmented, gold-coated mirror" to conduct infrared observations ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). The same launch date is confirmed independently by the Wikipedia entry on the telescope, which states that "The Webb was launched on 25 December 2021 on an Ariane 5 rocket from Kourou, French Guiana" ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). This report describes the observatory's design, its four science instruments, and its purpose, drawing exclusively on the two sources supplied, and it evaluates the reliability of those sources where they bear on specific claims.

## Design of the Observatory

### The primary mirror and optical architecture

The most frequently cited element of Webb's design is its large primary mirror. Wikipedia documents that "Webb's primary mirror is a 6.5 m (21 ft)-diameter gold-coated beryllium reflector with a collecting area of 25.4 m2" ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). NASA's account is less quantitative but architecturally explicit, describing the observatory as using a "segmented, gold-coated mirror" ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). Read together, the two sources describe three distinct properties of the same optical element: its physical size (6.5 m in diameter), its material composition (beryllium with a gold coating), its light-gathering capacity (25.4 m² of collecting area), and its construction method (segmented rather than monolithic).

Neither source alone supplies the complete picture. The NASA page states the segmentation and the gold coating but not the diameter, the substrate material, or the collecting area. The Wikipedia entry supplies the dimensions and materials but does not emphasize the segmented architecture. The complementarity of the two accounts means that a description built from both is materially richer than a description built from either one alone, while no contradiction between them arises.

An arithmetic observation based on the cited figures is instructive. A fully filled circular aperture of 6.5 m diameter would present a collecting area of approximately 33.2 m², whereas the documented collecting area is 25.4 m² ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). The documented area therefore corresponds to roughly three-quarters of a filled circle of that diameter. This gap is consistent with the segmented architecture that NASA describes ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)), since a segmented mirror necessarily includes gaps between segments and may depart from a strictly circular outline. The sources supplied do not themselves explain the relationship between the two figures, so this reconciliation should be treated as an inference drawn from the documented numbers rather than as a statement made by either source.

### The orbital and thermal environment

Webb does not orbit the Earth. NASA states that it "orbits the Sun 1.5 million kilometers ... away from the Earth at ... the second Lagrange point or L2" ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). This single sentence carries several distinct design implications. First, the observatory is a heliocentric rather than a geocentric mission. Second, its distance from Earth is fixed at approximately 1.5 million kilometers — for scale, that distance is roughly one percent of the mean Earth–Sun distance, an illustrative comparison derived arithmetically from the cited figure rather than a claim made by the sources. Third, the station is the second Lagrange point, a gravitational equilibrium region that the source names explicitly ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)).

The emphasis NASA places on the L2 location, alongside the infrared wavelength range documented by Wikipedia, indicates that the orbital environment is treated by the sources as integral to the observatory's function rather than as an incidental operational detail. The supplied sources, however, do not describe the spacecraft's thermal-control hardware, nor do they state how the L2 environment is used to maintain low temperatures. Any account of Webb's thermal design would therefore require documentation beyond the two sources used here.

### Launch and deployment

Both sources agree on the launch. NASA states that Webb "launched on Dec. 25th 2021" ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)), and Wikipedia adds the vehicle and site: "The Webb was launched on 25 December 2021 on an Ariane 5 rocket from Kourou, French Guiana" ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). The agreement of two independently maintained sources on this date is significant for reliability assessment, as discussed below. Wikipedia's entry is the more detailed of the two on launch logistics, supplying the launch vehicle (Ariane 5) and the launch site (Kourou, French Guiana), neither of which appears in the NASA page provided.

## The Science Instruments

Webb carries four science instruments. Wikipedia identifies them as "NIRCam, NIRSpec, MIRI, and FGS/NIRISS" ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). This is the only instrument-level information available in the supplied sources; the NASA page describes the observatory's mirror and orbit but does not enumerate the instrument suite, and Wikipedia lists the instruments only by name without describing the individual capabilities, detectors, fields of view, or operational roles of each ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/); [Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)).

Because of this limitation, the instrument suite can be assessed only collectively. The names themselves are suggestive but not equivalent to documentation: "MIRI" and the "NIR" prefixes in the other designations point toward infrared functionality, and the presence of designations including "Spec" and "Cam" suggests that spectroscopy and imaging functions are represented, while "FGS" suggests a fine-guidance function. These readings are inferences from terminology; the supplied sources do not state them, and they should not be presented as documented findings.

What the sources do establish is that the four instruments operate within the telescope's documented wavelength span, which runs "from long-wavelength visible light (red) through mid-infrared (0.6-28.5 um)" ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). This range spans more than a factor of forty in wavelength, from 0.6 micrometres at the red end of the visible band to 28.5 micrometres in the mid-infrared. The breadth of this span implies that the four-instrument suite covers a wide spectral territory rather than duplicating a single narrow band — an inference supported by the fact that four distinct instruments are documented for a range roughly two orders of magnitude wide.

## Purpose

The stated purpose of the observatory, as documented in the supplied sources, is astronomical observation in the infrared. NASA frames this directly: the telescope uses a "segmented, gold-coated mirror" to observe in infrared ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). Wikipedia expresses the same capability in spectral terms: "Webb observes a lower frequency range, from long-wavelength visible light (red) through mid-infrared (0.6-28.5 um)" ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)).

It is worth noting what these sources do not provide. Neither the NASA page nor the Wikipedia entry, as supplied here, enumerates specific science objectives such as particular classes of astronomical targets or observational programmes. Their description of purpose is expressed through capability — what Webb observes and how it observes it — rather than through a list of scientific goals. On the evidence available, therefore, the defensible characterisation of Webb's purpose is that it is an infrared observatory positioned away from Earth's immediate vicinity, equipped with a large segmented mirror and four instruments, and capable of collecting light across the 0.6–28.5 µm range ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/); [Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)).

## Reliability and Prioritisation of Sources

The two sources differ substantially in type and authority. NASA's mission page is a primary agency source published under public-domain terms and is authoritative for mission-level facts such as launch date, orbital location, and the general description of the optics ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). Wikipedia's article on the telescope is a collaboratively edited tertiary source distributed under a CC BY-SA 4.0 licence; its strength in this instance is quantitative consolidation, supplying the mirror diameter, collecting area, wavelength range, and instrument list that the NASA page does not ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)).

Neither source is older than the other in any meaningful sense: both are recorded as retrieved on 2026-09-14, so the instruction to prefer newer material cannot discriminate between them here. Prioritisation therefore rests on source type rather than recency. On that basis, NASA should be preferred for mission identity, launch, and orbital facts, while Wikipedia should be used for the structured physical specifications it uniquely provides in this source set — with the caveat that its figures should ideally be corroborated against a second authoritative source before being relied upon in a technical context.

## Synthesis and Assessment

Based on the information supplied, a concrete position can be stated: the documented features of the James Webb Space Telescope are not a list of independent specifications but a coherent set of interdependent design commitments oriented toward infrared observation. The collecting area of 25.4 m² and the 6.5 m diameter of the primary mirror define how much light the observatory can gather ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)); the 0.6–28.5 µm range defines what portion of the spectrum that light occupies ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)); the L2 station 1.5 million kilometers from Earth defines the environment in which the observation takes place ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)); and the four instruments named as NIRCam, NIRSpec, MIRI, and FGS/NIRISS define the detection layer that converts collected light into data ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). Each element presupposes the others: a mirror of that size has no purpose without instruments spanning that range, and an instrument suite of that breadth has no purpose without an aperture and environment able to supply it with infrared light.

Equally, the sources impose clear limits on what can responsibly be claimed. The supplied material does not document the number of mirror segments, the dimensions of any sunshield, the operating temperature, the individual capabilities of the four instruments, or the specific scientific objectives of the mission. A complete technical account of Webb would require additional authoritative documentation. The principal risk in the present source set is therefore omission rather than contradiction — the two accounts are consistent, but neither is exhaustive.

Two further conclusions follow. First, the NASA page, as a primary agency source, should anchor any factual statement about the mission's identity, launch, and orbit ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). Second, the Wikipedia entry is indispensable in this source set for the quantitative optical and spectral parameters, but its figures should be treated as reported rather than as independently verified ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)).

## Conclusion

The James Webb Space Telescope, launched on 25 December 2021 aboard an Ariane 5 from Kourou, French Guiana, is a Sun-orbiting infrared observatory stationed 1.5 million kilometers from Earth at the second Lagrange point ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/); [Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). Its optical system is a segmented, gold-coated beryllium reflector of 6.5 m diameter and 25.4 m² collecting area, feeding four science instruments — NIRCam, NIRSpec, MIRI, and FGS/NIRISS — across a spectral range extending from red visible light through the mid-infrared at 0.6 to 28.5 micrometres ([Wikipedia, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Webb_Space_Telescope)). Its documented purpose is infrared astronomical observation from a vantage point far from Earth ([NASA, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/)). The two sources agree where they overlap and complement each other where they do not, though neither documents the mission's thermal architecture, mirror segment count, instrument-level capabilities, or specific scientific objectives.
