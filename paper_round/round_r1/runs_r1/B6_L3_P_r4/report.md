# What Datasets Are Used? A Detailed Review of the Speech Corpora Behind Cross-Lingual Parkinson's Disease Classification

## Introduction

The assessment of Parkinson's disease (PD) from speech has become an active area of research, largely because speech provides a non-invasive, low-cost, and repeatable window into the motor and non-motor impairments associated with the disease. PD patients develop a range of speech impairments, often grouped under hypokinetic dysarthria, including vocal fold rigidity, bradykinesia, reduced control of the muscles involved in speech production, increased acoustic noise, reduced intensity, harsh and breathy voice quality, increased nasality, monopitch, monoloudness, speech rate disturbances, imprecise articulation of consonants, and involuntary pauses ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Automatically measuring these phenomena requires appropriate, well-characterized speech datasets.

The purpose of this report is to identify, describe, and critically evaluate the datasets used in the study "Convolutional Neural Networks and a Transfer Learning Strategy to Classify Parkinson's Disease from Speech in Three Different Languages." The study proposes a methodology to classify PD from speech in three languages — Spanish, German, and Czech — using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on time-frequency representations and a transfer learning strategy among the three languages ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Understanding the datasets is essential, because the validity, generalizability, and comparability of the reported results depend directly on the corpora that were assembled.

Two source documents inform this report: the primary study document and a third-party research note that summarizes and interprets the dataset composition ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The third-party note explicitly addresses key questions about PD speech datasets for cross-lingual transfer learning, CNN input representations, speech recordings of PD patients and healthy controls in multiple languages, transfer learning for speech classification across languages, and clinical speech corpora with PD speech tasks ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)).

## Overview of the Dataset Landscape

The study uses speech recordings of patients in three different languages: Spanish, German, and Czech ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Across all three datasets, the recordings were captured in noise-controlled conditions, and the speech signals were down-sampled to 16 kHz ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Importantly, the patients in the three datasets were evaluated by a neurologist expert according to the third section of the Movement Disorder Society's Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS-III), which provides a common clinical anchor for the data ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). All three corpora are described as supplying speech recordings of Parkinson's disease patients and healthy control (HC) speakers across multiple languages, thereby forming the base for the CNN and transfer learning strategy ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)).

## The Spanish Dataset: PC-GITA

The Spanish portion of the data comes from the PC-GITA corpus, a well-known Spanish speech corpus for the analysis of people suffering from Parkinson's disease ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The corpus contains utterances from 50 PD patients and 50 healthy control speakers, all Colombian Spanish native speakers ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The speaker composition of the Spanish dataset is therefore 50 PD patients and 50 healthy control speakers, giving a total of 100 speakers, and this corpus provides the Spanish portion of the cross-lingual speech data ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)).

The speech tasks performed by Spanish participants were comparatively extensive: participants pronounced a total of 10 sentences; performed the rapid repetition of the syllable sequences /pa-ta-ka/, /pe-ta-ka/, /pa-ka-ta/, /pa/, /ta/, and /ka/; read one text containing 36 words; and produced a monologue ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). One clinically relevant detail is that all Spanish patients were in the ON state at the time of the recording, that is, under the effect of their daily medication ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). This is an important design feature because medication status can substantially affect speech symptoms in PD.

The PC-GITA corpus has an established lineage in the literature. Prior work cited in the primary study reported accuracies of up to 77% using Gaussian mixture models and support vector machines on utterances from the PC-GITA database, formed with utterances from 50 PD patients and 50 HC subjects ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Another study using forced alignment and GMMs was tested with Colombian Spanish utterances from PC-GITA and Czech data, reporting accuracies of up to 81% for the Spanish data ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). These prior results help establish that PC-GITA is a mature, widely reused resource rather than a purpose-built corpus for this single experiment.

## The German Dataset

The German data consist of speech recordings collected in Germany. The third-party research note states clearly that the German data consist of speech recordings of 44 PD patients and 44 HC speakers, and that the German dataset description reports this composition ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). However, the primary document's text, as rendered in the available machine-readable version, states that speech recordings of "88 PD patients and 88 HC speakers from Germany" were considered ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). This is a material discrepancy that must be flagged: the two readings differ by a factor of two (44 + 44 = 88 speakers in total versus 88 + 88 = 176 speakers in total). Given that the third-party note explicitly and repeatedly reports 44 PD patients with 44 HC speakers, and that the primary document exhibits pervasive optical character recognition (OCR) degradation (for example, numbers are split as "8 8," "5 0," and "1 0 0" throughout), the most defensible interpretation is that the German cohort comprised 44 PD patients and 44 HC speakers, totaling 88 speakers, and that the "88 PD / 88 HC" rendering reflects a digit-level OCR artifact ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)).

The German participants performed four speech tasks: the rapid repetition of /pa-ta-ka/, five sentences, one text with 81 words, and a monologue ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). The German dataset's provenance is attributed to prior work on vowel articulation in Parkinson's disease ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Notably, the German data behaved differently from the other two languages in the experiments: the highest accuracy for the German language was obtained with the baseline (hand-crafted feature) model rather than with the CNN, and the German results were later improved substantially through transfer learning from Spanish ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)).

## The Czech Dataset

The Czech dataset contains a total of 100 native Czech speakers, comprising 50 PD patients and 50 healthy controls ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The speech tasks performed by Czech participants include the rapid repetition of the syllables /pa-ta-ka/, a read text with 80 words, and a monologue ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). The Czech corpus is attributed to work on detecting speech disorders in early Parkinson's disease by acoustic analysis ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)).

A notable feature of the Czech language in this research context is that Czech and German are phonologically richer than Spanish in terms of consonant production, which may make it easier for Czech PD patients to produce consonant sounds than for Spanish PD patients ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). The authors explicitly discuss this language-dependent issue and argue that despite such differences, cross-lingual classification could be improved with transfer learning ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). For Czech, the CNN produced the highest accuracy among the per-language models, and fine-tuning from Spanish improved accuracy further ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Prior Czech data had also been used in related work, with reported accuracies of up to 94% for the Czech data in a forced-alignment study ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)).

## Comparative Overview of the Three Datasets

Table 1 consolidates the core characteristics of the three datasets as reported across the two sources.

| Language | Corpus / source | PD speakers | HC speakers | Total speakers | Speech tasks | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spanish | PC-GITA corpus | 50 | 50 | 100 | 10 sentences; /pa-ta-ka/, /pe-ta-ka/, /pa-ka-ta/, /pa/, /ta/, /ka/; 36-word text; monologue | Colombian Spanish native speakers; all patients in ON medication state |
| German | German speech recordings | 44 (per third-party note); text rendering suggests 88 | 44 (per third-party note); text rendering suggests 88 | 88 (per third-party note) | /pa-ta-ka/ repetition; 5 sentences; 81-word text; monologue | Discrepancy between sources; baseline model performed best |
| Czech | Czech speech recordings | 50 | 50 | 100 | /pa-ta-ka/ repetition; 80-word read text; monologue | CNN performed best among per-language models |

Sources: ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)).

The three datasets are heterogeneous in task composition. Spanish participants produced the largest and most varied set of tasks (10 sentences, six syllable-repetition patterns, a 36-word text, and a monologue), whereas German participants produced five sentences and an 81-word text, and Czech participants produced an 80-word read text ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). All three datasets shared the rapid repetition of /pa-ta-ka/ and a monologue, which supports a degree of cross-linguistic comparability ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Nevertheless, the differences in read-text length and the number of sentence stimuli mean that the amount of data per speaker is not fully balanced across languages, a factor that could contribute to differences in model performance.

## From Raw Recordings to Model Inputs: Derived Data Representations

Beyond the raw corpora, the study generates a specific set of derived data representations. These are not separate datasets in the conventional sense, but they define the effective data used to train and evaluate the models, and therefore merit description.

| Processing stage | Specification |
|---|---|
| Segmentation | Automatic detection of onset and offset transitions based on the presence of the fundamental frequency in short-time frames; 80 ms taken to the left and right of the voiced/unvoiced boundary, forming 160 ms segments |
| Baseline features | 12 MFCCs with first and second derivatives, plus log energy distributed into 22 Bark bands = 58 descriptors; four statistical functionals (mean, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis) → 232-dimensional feature vector per utterance |
| Baseline classifier | Radial basis function SVM, margin parameter C = 10, Gaussian kernel γ = 0.0001; 10-fold speaker-independent cross-validation |
| CNN input | STFT with 256 frequency bins, 16 ms window, 4 ms step-size → 41 time frames; Mel-scale transformation with 80 filters → 80 × 41 spectrogram |
| CNN architecture | Four convolutional and max-pooling layers, dropout for regularization, two fully connected layers (320 → 128 → 64), output layer with softmax |
| Optimization | Cross-entropy loss, Adam optimizer |
| Aggregation | Final decision per speaker by majority voting across the different speech exercises |

Sources: ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)).

The segmentation strategy is theoretically motivated: it models the difficulty of PD patients in starting and stopping the vibration of the vocal folds, and it was previously shown to be informative for PD detection ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). The baseline model and the CNN thus operate on the same underlying 160 ms transition segments, which makes the comparison between hand-crafted features and learned representations reasonably controlled ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)).

## Experimental Use of the Datasets and Reported Results

The datasets were used in two stages. First, the baseline and CNN models were trained individually for each language. Second, the trained CNNs for each language were used as base models in the transfer learning strategy, in which a CNN trained with utterances from one language is used to initialize and fine-tune a model for another language ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). All speech exercises performed by the participants were considered for the classification strategy, and the final decision for each speaker was obtained by majority voting across the different speech exercises ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)).

The results indicate that transfer learning improved accuracy by up to 8% when the base model used to initialize the weights of the classifier was robust enough ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). More specifically, accuracy improved over 8% for German, from 69.3% in the baseline to 77.3% when the model was fine-tuned from Spanish, and over 4.1% for Czech, from 68.5% with the initial CNN to 72.6% when the model was fine-tuned from Spanish ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). The highest accuracy for German and Czech was obtained when the base language was Spanish, which the authors explain by noting that Spanish speakers have the best initial separability, so the other two languages benefit from the best initial model ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)).

The results obtained after transfer learning were also more balanced in terms of specificity and sensitivity than those trained without transfer learning, and the standard deviation of the transferred CNNs was lower, which the authors associate with improved generalization ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Analysis of the ROC curves showed that the area under the curve (AUC) for a Spanish target language was slightly higher when the base language was Czech, whereas for German and Czech target languages, the highest AUC was obtained when the base model was trained with Spanish utterances ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)).

## Data Quality, Reliability, and Limitations

Several caveats deserve emphasis. First, the primary source document is severely degraded by OCR: numbers are fragmented throughout, and this materially affects the reliability of any single figure taken from it ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). The most consequential example is the German cohort size, where the primary text's "88 PD patients and 88 HC speakers" conflicts with the third-party note's explicit statement of 44 PD patients and 44 HC speakers ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Any downstream use of the German cohort size should therefore verify the original published version rather than rely on the machine-readable extraction.

Second, the sample sizes are modest: at most 100 speakers per language, and possibly 88 for German ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). While speaker-independent cross-validation mitigates some concerns, small corpora limit the statistical power of language-comparative conclusions and increase the risk of overfitting, particularly for deep architectures.

Third, the datasets differ in clinically relevant ways. The Spanish patients had, on average, higher MDS-UPDRS-III scores than the German and Czech patients, meaning that the Spanish data contain patients with greater disease severity ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). This severity difference plausibly explains why the Spanish model had the best initial separability and why it served as the most effective base model for transfer learning ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). It also means that cross-linguistic comparisons are partly confounded with disease-severity differences.

Fourth, medication state is only partially controlled. All Spanish patients were recorded in the ON state ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)), but the two sources do not provide an equally explicit statement for the German and Czech cohorts. Since dopaminergic medication can noticeably alter speech symptoms, this is a limitation for cross-corpus harmonization.

Fifth, prior literature on which the study builds has been criticized for optimistic results. The authors note that some earlier studies reported accuracies from 80% to 94% depending on language, but that these results were optimistic because hyperparameters were optimized on the test set ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). In addition, in a language-independent scenario, where a CNN is trained with utterances from one language and tested on the remaining two, prior results were not satisfactory, with accuracy below 60% ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). This underscores that single-language results should not be over-interpreted as evidence of generalized PD detection.

Finally, the study itself reports that per-language baseline and CNN results were unbalanced toward one of the two classes according to specificity and sensitivity values ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Unbalanced class performance is a common issue in clinical classification and is directly relevant to the practical utility of these datasets and models.

## Conclusion

The study draws on three language-specific speech datasets: the Spanish PC-GITA corpus (50 PD patients and 50 healthy controls, Colombian Spanish speakers), a German dataset (44 PD patients and 44 healthy controls according to the third-party research note, though the primary text rendering suggests 88/88), and a Czech dataset (50 PD patients and 50 healthy controls, 100 native Czech speakers in total) ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). All recordings were captured in noise-controlled conditions and down-sampled to 16 kHz, and all patients were clinician-evaluated with the MDS-UPDRS-III ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The corpora vary in task composition and in disease severity, and the German cohort size is reported inconsistently across the available sources. These datasets collectively enable a genuinely cross-lingual investigation of PD classification from speech and support the study's central finding: transfer learning from a robust base model, particularly the Spanish model, improved accuracy by up to about 8% for German and about 4.1% for Czech while producing more balanced sensitivity–specificity trade-offs ([Vásquez-Correa et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). The dataset limitations — small samples, heterogeneous tasks, severity differences, medication-state considerations, and inconsistent reporting — should temper strong generalizations and motivate careful verification of the original published corpus documentation in future work.
