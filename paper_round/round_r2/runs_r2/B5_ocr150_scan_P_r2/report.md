# How Much Improvement Do the Introduced Models Achieve Compared to the Previous Models?

## Executive Summary

The paper introduces two recurrent neural network architectures, the Long Short-Term Memory Recurrent Neural Tensor Network (LSTMRNTN) and the Gated Recurrent Unit Recurrent Neural Tensor Network (GRURNTN), and compares them against previous baseline models: the standard LSTM RNN (LSTMRNN) and the standard GRU RNN (GRURNN) ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). The main improvement claim is that combining gating mechanisms with tensor-product operations yields lower test scores on both character-level and word-level language modeling tasks on the Penn Treebank (PTB) corpus. On character-level language modeling, GRURNTN reduces bits-per-character (BPC) from 1.39 to 1.33, an absolute reduction of 0.06 and a relative reduction of 4.32% over GRURNN; LSTMRNTN reduces BPC from 1.37 to 1.34, an absolute reduction of 0.03 and a relative reduction of 2.22% over LSTMRNN ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). On word-level language modeling, the primary paper reports that GRURNTN reduces perplexity (PPL) from 97.78 to 87.38, an absolute reduction of 10.4 and a relative reduction of 10.63% over GRURNN; LSTMRNTN reduces PPL from 108.26 to 96.97, an absolute reduction of 11.29 and a relative reduction of 10.42% over LSTMRNN ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). A third-party research note reports a smaller GRURNTN word-level improvement, reducing PPL from 97.78 to 92.98, which is 4.8 absolute and 4.91% relative over GRURNN ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The discrepancy is noted later in this report. Overall, the introduced models achieve consistent improvements over their matched baselines, with the largest relative gains appearing in word-level perplexity and the more modest gains appearing in character-level BPC.

## Background: Why the Introduced Models Should Improve Performance

The motivation for LSTMRNTN and GRURNTN is that standard RNNs often struggle to capture long-term dependencies because gradients can vanish or explode during backpropagation through time ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Gating mechanisms, as used in LSTM and GRU units, help RNNs remember and forget previous information and can mitigate these optimization problems. However, the paper argues that gating alone does not provide a more powerful way to model the direct interaction between the current input and the previous hidden layer. Most RNN interactions use linear projection, addition, and a nonlinear activation function, which the authors describe as a shallow transition ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Tensor-product operations, by contrast, allow second-degree polynomial interactions between the input and hidden representations and can therefore learn more complex relationships ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)).

The proposed architectures therefore combine both ideas. GRURNTN applies a tensor product between the current input \(x_t\) and the previous hidden layer multiplied by the reset gate \(r_t\) when calculating the candidate hidden layer ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). LSTMRNTN applies the tensor product between the current input and the previous hidden layer to calculate the current memory cell ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). The authors state that to their knowledge, none of the previous works combined gating mechanisms and tensor products into a single RNN architecture ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). This design difference is the primary reason given for the observed improvements.

## Evaluation Setup and Fairness of Comparison

The experiments use the Penn Treebank corpus, a standard benchmark for statistical language modeling. The dataset is divided into a training set from sections 0–20 with approximately 930,000 words, a validation set from sections 21–22 with approximately 74,000 words, and a test set from sections 23–24 with approximately 82,000 words ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). The vocabulary is limited to the 10,000 most common words, and all other words are mapped to an unknown token. Word-level language modeling is measured using perplexity (PPL), where lower is better, and character-level language modeling is measured using bits-per-character (BPC), where lower is better ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)).

An important aspect of the comparison is that the authors constrained the baseline models to have a similar number of parameters as the proposed models. For word-level language modeling, GRURNTN and LSTMRNTN used 256 hidden units, while GRURNN used 860 and LSTMRNN used 740; all models used 128-dimensional word embeddings ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). The total number of free parameters was about 12 million for the GRU-based pair and about 13 million for the LSTM-based pair ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). For character-level language modeling, GRURNTN and LSTMRNTN used 256 hidden units, while GRURNN used 820 and LSTMRNN used 600; all models used 32-dimensional character embeddings ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). The total number of free parameters was about 2.2 million for the GRU-based pair and about 2.6 million for the LSTM-based pair ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). This parameter matching strengthens the internal validity of the comparison: the improvements are not simply due to larger models. All scenarios used AdaGrad with mini-batches of 15 sentences, a learning-rate decay of 0.5 when validation cost increased, gradient rescaling when the norm exceeded 5, and orthogonal weight initialization ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)).

## Character-Level Language Modeling Improvements

The character-level results show consistent but relatively small improvements. Table 1 summarizes the test BPC values and the improvements.

| Model Pair | Baseline BPC | Proposed BPC | Absolute Improvement | Relative Improvement |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| GRURNN → GRURNTN | 1.39 | 1.33 | 0.06 | 4.32% |
| LSTMRNN → LSTMRNTN | 1.37 | 1.34 | 0.03 | 2.22% |

**Table 1.** Character-level language modeling improvements on the Penn Treebank test set. Data from ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)).

The GRU-based tensor model, GRURNTN, achieved the larger character-level gain in both absolute and relative terms. It reduced BPC by 0.06, or 4.32%, compared with the matched GRURNN baseline. The LSTM-based tensor model, LSTMRNTN, achieved a smaller but still positive reduction of 0.03 BPC, or 2.22%, compared with the matched LSTMRNN baseline ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). The paper also reports that both proposed models produced lower BPC than their baselines from the first epoch to the last epoch ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). In terms of convergence behavior, GRURNN made faster initial progress than LSTMRNN, but LSTMRNN eventually converged to a better development-set BPC. GRURNTN made faster and quicker progress than LSTMRNTN and converged to a similar BPC in the final epoch ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Overall, GRURNTN slightly outperformed the baselines on the character-level task, while LSTMRNTN's advantage was smaller ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)).

A 0.03–0.06 BPC reduction may appear small in absolute terms, but character-level language modeling is a fine-grained task where even small BPC reductions can be meaningful. The consistency of the improvement across epochs and the matched parameter budgets support the conclusion that the tensor-product gating combination contributes to better performance on this task. Nevertheless, the character-level gains are modest compared with the word-level gains discussed next.

## Word-Level Language Modeling Improvements

The word-level results show much larger relative improvements. Table 2 summarizes the improvements as reported in the primary paper.

| Model Pair | Baseline PPL | Proposed PPL | Absolute Improvement | Relative Improvement |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| GRURNN → GRURNTN | 97.78 | 87.38 | 10.40 | 10.63% |
| LSTMRNN → LSTMRNTN | 108.26 | 96.97 | 11.29 | 10.42% |

**Table 2.** Word-level language modeling improvements on the Penn Treebank test set according to the primary paper. Data from ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)).

According to the primary paper, GRURNTN reduced perplexity from 97.78 to 87.38, which is a 10.4 absolute reduction and a 10.63% relative reduction over the GRURNN baseline ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). LSTMRNTN reduced perplexity from 108.26 to 96.97, which is an 11.29 absolute reduction and a 10.42% relative reduction over the LSTMRNN baseline ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). These are substantial improvements in language modeling, where perplexity reductions of this magnitude are typically considered meaningful. The paper further states that GRURNTN outperformed all baseline models as well as other models by a large margin, and that LSTMRNTN improved over LSTMRNN while closely resembling the baseline GRURNN in performance ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). In the epoch-wise comparison, GRURNN made faster progress than LSTMRNN, but GRURNTN's progress was better than LSTMRNTN's, and GRURNTN consistently had lower PPL than the other models ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Thus, on word-level language modeling, GRURNTN is the strongest of the two proposed models in the primary source.

The third-party research note reports a different value for GRURNTN on the word-level task. It states that GRURNTN reduced PPL from 97.78 to 92.98, which is 4.8 absolute and 4.91% relative improvement over GRURNN ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). This conflicts with the primary paper's reported 87.38 PPL and 10.63% relative improvement. The third-party note also states that LSTMRNTN reduced PPL from 108.26 to 96.97, which matches the primary paper exactly ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Because the LSTM-based number is consistent across sources but the GRU-based word-level number differs, the most plausible interpretation is that the third-party note contains a transcription or reporting error for GRURNTN's word-level PPL. The primary source should be given greater weight for this specific figure. Even under the more conservative third-party figure, however, GRURNTN still improves over its baseline by 4.8 PPL, so the direction of the effect is unchanged. Under the primary paper's figure, the improvement is more than twice as large.

## Comparative Magnitude and Practical Significance

The improvements can be compared in several ways. First, word-level improvements are much larger in relative terms than character-level improvements. The word-level relative reductions are approximately 10.4–10.63% according to the primary paper, whereas the character-level relative reductions are 2.22–4.32% ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). This suggests that the tensor-product gating combination is particularly beneficial for modeling word-level dependencies, where longer-range semantic and syntactic relationships may be more important. Second, GRURNTN generally shows stronger performance than LSTMRNTN in the primary paper. On character-level, GRURNTN's 4.32% relative improvement exceeds LSTMRNTN's 2.22% ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). On word-level, GRURNTN's 10.63% relative improvement slightly exceeds LSTMRNTN's 10.42% in the primary paper, although the absolute PPL reduction is larger for LSTMRNTN because its baseline starts at a higher perplexity ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). If one instead uses the third-party note's smaller GRURNTN word-level improvement, then LSTMRNTN would show the larger relative word-level gain (10.42% vs. 4.91%) ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). This is another reason the discrepancy matters for ranking the two proposed models.

Third, the improvements are achieved with similar parameter counts. The authors explicitly constrained the baselines to have similar numbers of parameters as the proposed models for a fair comparison ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). This means the gains are not simply due to increased model capacity. Instead, the gains are associated with the architectural change: adding tensor-product interactions inside the gating equations. That is a stronger claim than merely showing that a larger model performs better.

Fourth, the proposed models are not necessarily state-of-the-art against all published results. For example, in the character-level table, other published configurations list lower BPC values such as 1.24 and 1.26, which are lower than the proposed 1.33 and 1.34 ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Similarly, the paper notes that some published baselines used adaptive noise regularization or dynamic evaluation, whereas the proposed models did not use dynamic evaluation ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Therefore, the correct claim is that GRURNTN and LSTMRNTN improve over their matched, parameter-constrained baselines, not that they are universally superior to all prior work. This distinction is important for an objective assessment.

## Why the Improvements Occur: Architectural Interpretation

The paper attributes the gains to two complementary mechanisms. Gating units allow the model to learn long-term dependencies by controlling information flow across time steps. Tensor products allow the model to represent interactions between the current input and previous hidden state using second-degree polynomial terms rather than only linear projection and addition ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). In GRURNTN, the tensor product is applied between the current input and the reset-gated previous hidden layer, which means the reset gate determines which previous hidden information is used in the tensor interaction ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). In LSTMRNTN, the tensor product is applied to the memory-cell calculation, which means the richer interaction directly affects what is stored in the cell state ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). The authors also derived backpropagation-through-time updates for the tensor weights and noted that the derivative with respect to the tensor weights depends on the values of the input and hidden layers, so each tensor slice is learned more directly from those values than in standard addition-based RNNs ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). This provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for why the proposed models improve over the baselines.

## Discrepancy, Limitations, and Interpretation

The main inconsistency in the provided information concerns the GRURNTN word-level perplexity. The primary paper reports 87.38 PPL and a 10.63% relative improvement ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). The third-party note reports 92.98 PPL and a 4.91% relative improvement ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Because the third-party note is a secondary source and because its LSTMRNTN word-level result exactly matches the primary paper, the primary source is more reliable for the GRURNTN word-level figure. However, a conservative reading should acknowledge both numbers. Even the smaller reported improvement of 4.8 PPL is positive and meaningful. The discrepancy does not change the overall conclusion that the introduced models outperform their baselines; it only changes the estimated size of the GRURNTN word-level gain.

Other limitations should also be noted. The character-level improvements are small: 0.03 and 0.06 BPC. The paper does not report statistical significance tests, so it is not possible to determine whether these differences are statistically reliable beyond the reported epoch-wise consistency. The proposed models also introduce tensor operations, which can increase computational and memory costs relative to standard gated RNNs, although parameter counts were matched. The experiments are limited to the Penn Treebank corpus and to language modeling; the authors suggest future work on other temporal and sequential tasks such as speech recognition and video recognition ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). Finally, the baselines were constrained to similar parameter counts, which is good for fairness but means the absolute performance of the baselines may not represent their best possible performance under different hyperparameter settings. The paper also notes that dynamic evaluation and adaptive noise regularization were not used in the proposed model experiments, even though some published baselines used them ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). This limits direct comparison with the strongest published numbers.

## Conclusion

The introduced models achieve consistent improvements over the previous gated RNN baselines on both evaluated tasks. On character-level language modeling, GRURNTN reduces BPC from 1.39 to 1.33, a 0.06 absolute and 4.32% relative improvement over GRURNN, while LSTMRNTN reduces BPC from 1.37 to 1.34, a 0.03 absolute and 2.22% relative improvement over LSTMRNN ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). On word-level language modeling, the primary paper reports that GRURNTN reduces PPL from 97.78 to 87.38, a 10.4 absolute and 10.63% relative improvement over GRURNN, and that LSTMRNTN reduces PPL from 108.26 to 96.97, an 11.29 absolute and 10.42% relative improvement over LSTMRNN ([Tjandra et al., n.d.](document_1.txt)). A third-party note reports a smaller GRURNTN word-level improvement of 4.8 absolute and 4.91% relative, while agreeing with the LSTMRNTN word-level result ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Taking the primary source as authoritative, the largest improvements are in word-level perplexity, where relative reductions exceed 10%, and the smallest are in character-level BPC, where relative reductions are between roughly 2% and 4%. Because the baselines were constrained to similar parameter counts, these gains are attributable to the architectural combination of gating and tensor products rather than to increased model size alone. The evidence therefore supports the paper's conclusion that GRURNTN and LSTMRNTN significantly improve performance over their corresponding baselines, with GRURNTN generally being the stronger of the two proposed models in the primary reported results.
