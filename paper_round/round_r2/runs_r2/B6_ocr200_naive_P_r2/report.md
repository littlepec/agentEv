# Datasets Used in “Convolutional Neural Networks and a Transfer Learning Strategy to Classify Parkinson’s Disease from Speech in Three Different Languages”

## Introduction

The study by Vasquez-Correa et al. (2020) investigates the automatic classification of Parkinson’s disease (PD) from speech using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and a cross-lingual transfer learning strategy ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The core empirical contribution of the paper depends on three language-specific speech datasets: Spanish, German, and Czech. These datasets provide the speech recordings of PD patients and healthy control (HC) speakers that are used for training, testing, and transfer learning among languages ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). A third-party research note further summarizes and clarifies the dataset composition, especially the speaker counts for the German and Czech portions ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). This report answers the query “What datasets are used?” by identifying each dataset, describing its composition, speech tasks, clinical evaluation, preprocessing, and role in the experimental design.

## Overview of the Three Primary Datasets

The study uses speech recordings in three different languages: Spanish, German, and Czech ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). All recordings were captured in noise-controlled conditions, and the speech signals were down-sampled to 16 kHz ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The patients in all three datasets were evaluated by a neurologist expert according to the third section of the Movement Disorder Society–sponsored revision of the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS-III) ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The following table summarizes the dataset composition.

| Language | Dataset / Corpus Name | PD Speakers | HC Speakers | Total Speakers | Native Speaker Group | Clinical Evaluation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spanish | PC-GITA corpus | 50 | 50 | 100 | Colombian Spanish | MDS-UPDRS-III |
| German | German speech recordings | 44 | 44 | 88 | German | MDS-UPDRS-III |
| Czech | Czech speech recordings | 50 | 50 | 100 | Czech | MDS-UPDRS-III |

*Sources: ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)).*

Across the three datasets, the study therefore includes 144 PD patients and 144 healthy controls, totaling 288 speakers. The Spanish and Czech datasets each contain 50 PD patients and 50 HC speakers, while the German dataset contains 44 PD patients and 44 HC speakers ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). It is important to note that the primary source’s Table 1 contains some formatting irregularities in the provided text; for example, the German speaker counts are not consistently legible. However, the third-party research note explicitly states that the German data consist of 44 PD patients and 44 HC speakers, and this count is treated as authoritative for the present report ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)).

## Spanish Dataset: PC-GITA Corpus

The Spanish data come from the PC-GITA corpus, which is the most clearly named and documented dataset in the study ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). PC-GITA contains utterances from 50 PD patients and 50 HC Colombian Spanish native speakers, all of whom are native speakers of Colombian Spanish ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). This corpus provides the Spanish portion of the cross-lingual speech data and is used both for individual language classification and as a base language in transfer learning experiments ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)).

The speech tasks performed by Spanish participants include the pronunciation of a total of 10 sentences, the rapid repetition of /pa-ta-ka/, /pe-ta-ka/, /pa-ka-ta/, /pa/, /ta/, and /ka/, one text with 36 words, and a monologue ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). All Spanish PD patients were in the ON state at the time of recording, meaning they were under the effect of their daily medication ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). This is a relevant clinical detail because medication state can influence speech symptoms and thus the acoustic characteristics of the recordings.

The primary source reports demographic and clinical information for the Spanish dataset, including age ranges, mean ages, time since diagnosis, and MDS-UPDRS-III scores ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). For Spanish PD patients, the mean MDS-UPDRS-III score was 37.8 (SD = 22.1) for male patients and 37.6 (SD = 14.1) for female patients. The mean time after diagnosis was 12.6 years (SD = 11.6) for male patients and 8.7 years (SD = 5.9) for female patients ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). These values indicate that the Spanish PD cohort had comparatively higher disease severity and longer disease duration than the German and Czech cohorts, a factor that the paper later uses to explain why Spanish utterances provided the best base model for transfer learning ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)).

## German Dataset

The German data consist of speech recordings from 44 PD patients and 44 HC speakers ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Unlike the Spanish PC-GITA corpus, the German dataset is not given a formal corpus name in the provided text; it is described simply as German speech recordings ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The German dataset supplies the German portion of the cross-lingual speech data and is used for both individual language classification and transfer learning ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)).

The speech tasks performed by German participants include four tasks: the rapid repetition of /pa-ta-ka/, five sentences, one text with 81 words, and a monologue ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The primary source’s Table 1 reports demographic and clinical information for German participants, although the provided text is partially garbled. According to the table, the mean MDS-UPDRS-III score was 22.1 (SD = 9.9) for male patients and 23.3 (SD = 12.0) for female patients. The mean time after diagnosis was 7.0 years (SD = 5.5) for male patients and 7.1 years (SD = 6.2) for female patients ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). These scores are substantially lower than those of the Spanish PD cohort, indicating milder average disease severity in the German dataset ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)).

The German dataset is particularly important in the transfer learning experiments because the highest accuracy for German was obtained when the CNN was fine-tuned from a Spanish base model. The accuracy improved from 69.3% in the baseline model to 77.3% when the model was fine-tuned from Spanish ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). This improvement of over 8 percentage points is one of the central findings of the study and directly depends on the composition and quality of the German dataset ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)).

## Czech Dataset

The Czech dataset contains a total of 100 native Czech speakers, comprising 50 PD patients and 50 healthy controls ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The Czech data are associated with the work of Rusz (2018) and prior Czech data sources cited in the paper ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The speech tasks performed by Czech participants include the rapid repetition of the syllables /pa-ta-ka/, a read text with 80 words, and a monologue ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). Like the German dataset, the Czech dataset is not presented under a formal corpus name in the provided text; it is described as Czech speech recordings ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)).

The primary source reports clinical and demographic information for the Czech PD patients as well. The mean MDS-UPDRS-III score was 21.4 (SD = 11.5) for male patients and 18.1 (SD = 9.7) for female patients. The mean time after diagnosis was 6.7 years (SD = 4.5) for male patients and 6.8 years (SD = 5.2) for female patients ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). These values are similar to the German cohort and lower than the Spanish cohort, again indicating milder average disease severity in the Czech data ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)).

In the experiments, the Czech dataset benefited from transfer learning when the base model was trained on Spanish utterances. The initial CNN accuracy for Czech was 68.5%, and it improved to 72.6% when the model was fine-tuned from Spanish, an improvement of over 4.1 percentage points ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). This result reinforces the role of the Spanish dataset as a robust base model for cross-lingual transfer.

## Dataset Structure and Preprocessing

Although the query focuses on which datasets are used, it is important to describe how the datasets were structured and preprocessed, because these steps define the effective data used by the models. The speech signals from all three datasets were analyzed based on the automatic detection of onset and offset transitions, which model the difficulties of patients in starting and stopping the movement of the vocal folds ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The detection of transitions is based on the presence of the fundamental frequency of speech in short-time frames ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The border between voiced and unvoiced frames is detected, and 80 ms of the signal are taken to the left and to the right, forming segments with 160 ms length ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)).

Two modeling approaches were applied to these transition segments. The baseline model used hand-crafted features: 12 Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs) with their first and second derivatives, and the log energy of the signal distributed into 22 Bark bands, yielding 58 descriptors ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). Four statistical functionals—mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis—were computed for each descriptor, producing a 232-dimensional feature vector per utterance ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The CNN model, by contrast, used time-frequency representations. A short-time Fourier transform (STFT) with 256 frequency bins was computed for each segmented transition, using a window length of 16 ms and a step size of 4 ms, forming 41 time frames per transition ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The resulting spectrogram was transformed into the Mel scale using 80 filters, producing an 80×41 spectrogram that was used to train the CNNs ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)).

For the final decision per speaker, a majority voting strategy was applied across the different speech exercises performed by each participant ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). This means that the datasets were not used as isolated utterance-level samples alone; instead, the speaker-level classification was derived from multiple tasks per speaker, which is an important characteristic of the dataset design.

## Speech Tasks Across Datasets

The three datasets differ in the specific speech tasks performed by participants. The following table summarizes these tasks.

| Language | Speech Tasks |
|---|---|
| Spanish | 10 sentences; rapid repetition of /pa-ta-ka/, /pe-ta-ka/, /pa-ka-ta/, /pa/, /ta/, /ka/; one text with 36 words; monologue |
| German | Rapid repetition of /pa-ta-ka/; 5 sentences; one text with 81 words; monologue |
| Czech | Rapid repetition of /pa-ta-ka/; read text with 80 words; monologue |

*Source: ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)).*

The tasks are similar in that they include rapid syllable repetition, read speech, and monologue. However, the exact number of sentences and text lengths differs, which may introduce task-related variability across languages. The study does not appear to harmonize the tasks perfectly across languages, and this is a potential source of dataset heterogeneity ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The use of multiple tasks per speaker is nevertheless a strength, because it allows the majority voting strategy to combine evidence across different speech conditions.

## Clinical and Demographic Differences Between Datasets

The primary source provides a table with age ranges, mean ages, time after diagnosis, and MDS-UPDRS-III scores for the three datasets ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). Although the table is partially malformed in the provided text, the key clinical differences are clear: the Spanish PD cohort had higher MDS-UPDRS-III scores and longer disease duration than the German and Czech cohorts ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The following table summarizes the reported MDS-UPDRS-III and time-after-diagnosis values for PD patients.

| Dataset | MDS-UPDRS-III (Male) | MDS-UPDRS-III (Female) | Time After Diagnosis, years (Male) | Time After Diagnosis, years (Female) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spanish PD | 37.8 (22.1) | 37.6 (14.1) | 12.6 (11.6) | 8.7 (5.9) |
| German PD | 22.1 (9.9) | 23.3 (12.0) | 7.0 (5.5) | 7.1 (6.2) |
| Czech PD | 21.4 (11.5) | 18.1 (9.7) | 6.7 (4.5) | 6.8 (5.2) |

*Source: ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)).*

These differences are not merely descriptive; they help explain the experimental results. The paper notes that the difference in results among the three languages can be explained by the higher average MDS-UPDRS-II score for Spanish patients compared with German and Czech patients, meaning there were patients with higher disease severity in the Spanish data ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). Although the paper uses the label “MDS-UPDRS-II” in that sentence, the third section of the scale is the MDS-UPDRS-III, and the research note confirms that the patients were evaluated according to MDS-UPDRS-III ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). This discrepancy is a minor reporting inconsistency in the primary source, but it does not change the substantive point that the Spanish cohort was more severely affected on average.

## Use of the Datasets in Experiments

The datasets are used in two main experimental stages. First, baseline and CNN models are trained considering each language individually ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). Second, the trained CNNs for each language are used as base models in a transfer learning strategy to improve accuracy in the other two languages ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). In other words, the Spanish, German, and Czech datasets are not only separate evaluation sets; they are also sources of pre-trained weights for cross-lingual adaptation ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)).

The results show that the Spanish dataset produced the most robust base model. When the Spanish model was used to initialize the German model, accuracy improved from 69.3% in the baseline to 77.3% ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). When the Spanish model was used to initialize the Czech model, accuracy improved from 68.5% to 72.6% ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The paper concludes that the transfer learning among languages improved accuracy in up to 8% when the base model used to initialize the weights of the classifier was robust enough ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The Spanish dataset, with its higher disease severity and better initial separability, fulfilled this condition ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)).

The datasets also differ in how balanced the classification results are in terms of sensitivity and specificity. The paper notes that for the three languages, the results without transfer learning were unbalanced toward one of the two classes according to specificity and sensitivity values ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). After transfer learning, the results were in most cases more balanced, and the standard deviation of the transferred CNNs was lower, which the authors interpret as an improvement in generalization ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). This suggests that the dataset composition and cross-lingual transfer interact in ways that affect not only overall accuracy but also the balance between true positive and true negative rates.

## Data Quality, Limitations, and Reliability

The three datasets have several strengths. They are multi-language, include balanced numbers of PD and HC speakers, and involve clinical evaluation by a neurologist expert using MDS-UPDRS-III ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The recordings were captured in noise-controlled conditions and down-sampled to 16 kHz, which supports reproducibility and comparability ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The use of multiple speech tasks per speaker and majority voting at the speaker level further strengthens the dataset design ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)).

However, there are also limitations and data quality issues. First, the German and Czech datasets are not given formal corpus names in the provided text, unlike the Spanish PC-GITA corpus ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). This makes it harder to trace the exact provenance of those data from the provided information alone. Second, the primary source’s Table 1 has formatting problems in the provided text, including ambiguous counts for the German speakers ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The third-party research note helps resolve this by explicitly stating that the German data contain 44 PD patients and 44 HC speakers ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Third, the paper contains a minor inconsistency in labeling the clinical scale as MDS-UPDRS-II in one passage, while the third section of the scale is MDS-UPDRS-III and the research note uses MDS-UPDRS-III ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Fourth, the datasets differ in disease severity and task protocols, which may confound cross-lingual comparisons ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). Finally, the transfer learning approach only improved accuracy when the base model was robust enough, which means that not all datasets function equally well as source domains ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)).

In terms of reliability, the primary source is an arXiv preprint by Vasquez-Correa et al. (2020), and the third-party note is a research summary that aligns with the primary source’s main dataset counts ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The PC-GITA corpus is a known Spanish speech corpus for Parkinson’s disease research, and the Czech data are linked to established work by Rusz and colleagues ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The German data are described in sufficient detail to understand their composition, even without a formal corpus name ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Overall, the datasets are appropriate for the study’s aims, but readers should be aware of the reporting inconsistencies and the lack of formal names for two of the three datasets.

## Conclusion

The study by Vasquez-Correa et al. (2020) uses three language-specific speech datasets: the Spanish PC-GITA corpus, German speech recordings, and Czech speech recordings ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The Spanish dataset comprises 50 PD patients and 50 HC Colombian Spanish native speakers; the German dataset comprises 44 PD patients and 44 HC speakers; and the Czech dataset comprises 50 PD patients and 50 HC native Czech speakers ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Across all three datasets, the study includes 144 PD patients and 144 healthy controls, totaling 288 speakers. The datasets were evaluated clinically with MDS-UPDRS-III, recorded in noise-controlled conditions, down-sampled to 16 kHz, and processed into voiced/unvoiced transition segments for CNN and baseline modeling ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The Spanish PC-GITA corpus is the most clearly named and documented dataset, and it also served as the most effective base model for cross-lingual transfer learning, improving German accuracy to 77.3% and Czech accuracy to 72.6% ([Vasquez-Correa et al., 2020](document_1.txt)). The German and Czech datasets are described by speaker counts and task protocols rather than formal corpus names in the provided text, and the primary source contains some table formatting inconsistencies that the third-party note helps clarify ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). These three datasets form the empirical foundation for the paper’s conclusions about CNN-based Parkinson’s disease classification from speech and cross-lingual transfer learning.
