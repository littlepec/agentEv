# Size and Composition of the AntiScam Dataset: A Detailed Report

## Introduction

The AntiScam dataset is a non-collaborative dialog corpus introduced by Li, Qian, Shi, and Yu (2019) to support research on dialog systems that operate in adversarial settings, specifically anti-scam scenarios ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). The dataset was collected via Amazon Mechanical Turk using a role-playing Amazon customer service scam scenario, where one participant acts as an attacker attempting to elicit personal information and the other acts as a user defending their information ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). The question “How big is the ANTISCAM dataset?” can be answered along multiple dimensions: number of dialogs, turns, utterances, annotated sentences, and annotation granularity. This report synthesizes information from the primary paper and a third-party research note to provide a comprehensive answer. The primary source is the original research paper, which is the most reliable for factual claims about the dataset’s construction and statistics ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). The third-party note corroborates most figures but contains at least one characterization discrepancy that should be noted ([Document 2, n.d.]).

## Core Size Metrics

### Number of Dialogs

The AntiScam dataset contains 220 dialogs ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). The paper states: “We collected 220 human-human dialogs” ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). A third-party research note describes the corpus as consisting of “220 human-machine anti-scam dialogs” ([Document 2, n.d.]). However, the primary source specifies human-human interactions, which is the more reliable characterization because it is the original research publication describing the data collection procedure. The dataset was collected through a role-playing task on Amazon Mechanical Turk where two workers were paired: one as an attacker and one as a user ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). Thus, the base unit of size is 220 two-party conversations. This is the fundamental size figure for the AntiScam dataset, and it is consistently reported across both the primary paper and the third-party note ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742); [Document 2, n.d.]).

### Conversation and Utterance Length

The average conversation length is 12.45 turns ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). A turn is defined as one speaker’s contribution in the dialog. The average utterance length is 11.13 words ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). These statistics provide a sense of the scale per dialog. Multiplying the number of dialogs by average turns yields an approximate total of 2,739 turns (220 × 12.45) ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). However, the paper does not report exact total turn counts, so this is an estimate derived from the reported averages. The average utterance length suggests that dialogs are composed of relatively short sentences, typical of typed conversations on crowdsourcing platforms. If one assumes that each turn contains roughly one utterance, the total word count of the full corpus would be approximately 30,485 words (220 × 12.45 × 11.13), though this is a rough approximation because utterances may contain multiple sentences and the paper does not provide a precise total word count ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)).

### Annotated Subset

Not all 220 dialogs were manually annotated with the full hierarchical scheme. The paper reports that two expert annotators with linguistic training annotated 3,044 sentences in 100 dialogs ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). This subset represents approximately 45.5% of the total dialogs (100 out of 220). The annotation achieved an averaged weighted kappa value of 0.874, indicating high inter-annotator agreement ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). The annotation covered both intents and semantic slots at the sentence level, following a segmentation of each conversation turn into single sentences ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). Therefore, the fully annotated portion of the dataset consists of 100 dialogs and 3,044 sentences. This means that the annotated subset has an average of 30.44 sentences per dialog (3,044 ÷ 100) and, given the average of 12.45 turns per dialog, approximately 2.44 sentences per turn ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). This level of granularity is important for training models that predict intents and semantic slots at the sentence level.

## Annotation Scheme and Data Composition

The size of the AntiScam dataset is not solely measured by dialog count; its annotation richness adds significant depth. The hierarchical intent annotation scheme separates on-task and off-task intents ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). For AntiScam, on-task intents include elicitation, providing_information, and refusal ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). Off-task intents are universal across non-collaborative tasks and include six general intents (open_question, yes_no_question, positive_answer, negative_answer, responsive_statement, nonresponsive_statement) and six social intents (greeting, closing, apology, thanking, respond_to_thank, hold) ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). This yields a total of 3 on-task intents plus 12 off-task intents, for 15 intent categories specific to AntiScam. The semantic slot annotation scheme identifies 13 main slots, such as order_detail, payment, name, address, phone_num, card_info, card_num, card_cvs, card_date, account_detail, and others ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). This annotation granularity allows the dataset to support multi-task learning for intent prediction and slot filling, even though the number of dialogs is relatively modest.

The paper also reports intent distribution differences between attackers and users. For example, users produced more refusal (74 vs. 19) than attackers, and users asked more open_questions (173 vs. 54) and yes_no_questions (165 vs. 117) ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). Both roles exhibited substantial social content: 292 social intent sentences for attackers and 252 for users ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). These numbers indicate that the 3,044 annotated sentences are not evenly distributed across intents; the dataset is rich in off-task and social content, which is central to non-collaborative dialogs. The presence of such content confirms the necessity of a hierarchical on-task/off-task annotation scheme and suggests that the dataset’s effective size for studying social dynamics is larger than the raw dialog count might imply ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)).

## Comparative Context

To appreciate the size of AntiScam, it is useful to compare it with the PersuasionForGood dataset, another non-collaborative dialog corpus used in the same paper ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). PersuasionForGood contains 1,017 dialogs, of which 300 are annotated with dialog acts ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). Its average conversation length is 10.43 turns, and its vocabulary size is 8,141 ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). In contrast, AntiScam has 220 dialogs, 100 annotated dialogs, and an average conversation length of 12.45 turns ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). Thus, while AntiScam is smaller in total dialog count, it has a longer average conversation length and a higher proportion of manually annotated dialogs relative to its total size (45.5% vs. 29.5% for PersuasionForGood) ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). This makes AntiScam a densely annotated resource for its scale. The paper does not report a vocabulary size for AntiScam, which is a minor gap in the size profile.

## Data Collection and Quality Indicators

The AntiScam dataset was collected on Amazon Mechanical Turk with a role-playing setup. Two workers were randomly paired; one played an attacker aiming to elicit personal information, and the other played a user who was instructed to protect their information and potentially elicit the attacker’s information ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). The users were asked to determine whether their partner was an attacker. Only 172 out of 220 users successfully identified their partner as an attacker ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). This 78.18% identification rate suggests that the attackers were well-trained and not too easily identifiable, adding realism to the dataset ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). The dataset thus captures authentic adversarial dynamics, which is valuable for training non-collaborative dialog systems. The 48 users who did not identify the attacker contribute to the dataset’s diversity, as their dialogs reflect interactions where the user remained unaware of the adversarial nature of the conversation ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)).

## Source Reliability and Discrepancies

The primary source for this report is the original research paper by Li et al. (2019), which provides detailed statistics on the AntiScam dataset ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). A third-party research note also describes the dataset and corroborates the core size figures: 220 dialogs, 100 annotated dialogs, 3,044 sentences, 12.45 turns, 11.13 words, and 172 out of 220 users identifying the attacker ([Document 2, n.d.]). However, the third-party note characterizes the dataset as “human-machine anti-scam dialogs” ([Document 2, n.d.]), whereas the primary paper explicitly states that the dataset consists of human-human dialogs ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). This discrepancy likely arises from a misinterpretation of the data collection setup, where a human user interacted with a human attacker, not a machine. For the purpose of answering the size question, the primary source is more reliable and should be preferred. The third-party note is useful as a secondary confirmation but should not override the original paper on factual details.

## Implications of Dataset Size for Research

The size of AntiScam—220 dialogs with 3,044 annotated sentences—positions it as a small but richly annotated benchmark. The paper used an 80/10/10 split for training, validation, and testing ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). With 220 dialogs, the training set would contain approximately 176 dialogs, validation 22, and test 22 ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). This is modest compared to large-scale task-oriented dialog datasets, but the annotation depth and the inclusion of off-task content make it suitable for studying hierarchical intent modeling and response generation with constraints. To mitigate the small dataset size, the paper pre-trains the model on the PERSONA-CHAT dataset before fine-tuning on AntiScam ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). This transfer learning approach allows the model to leverage a larger corpus of open-domain dialogs, partially compensating for the limited number of anti-scam dialogs. The paper’s experiments show that models trained on this dataset can outperform baselines on both automatic and human evaluations ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). However, the authors acknowledge limitations: the model still produces responses that are inconsistent with distant conversation history because GPT can only track limited context ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). This limitation is partly a function of dataset size and model architecture, and it suggests that larger or longer-context datasets may be needed for further improvements.

## Conclusion

In answer to the query “How big is the ANTISCAM dataset?”, the AntiScam dataset comprises 220 human-human dialogs collected via Amazon Mechanical Turk in a role-playing anti-scam scenario ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). The average conversation length is 12.45 turns, and the average utterance length is 11.13 words ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). A subset of 100 dialogs containing 3,044 sentences was manually annotated by two expert annotators, achieving a weighted kappa of 0.874 ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). The dataset includes hierarchical intent annotations (on-task and off-task) and 13 semantic slot categories ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). While smaller than some comparable non-collaborative datasets such as PersuasionForGood (1,017 dialogs), AntiScam is more densely annotated and features longer average conversations ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)). Its size and annotation quality make it a valuable benchmark for non-collaborative dialog research, particularly for tasks involving persuasion, negotiation, and anti-scam strategies. Researchers should be aware that the fully annotated portion is only 100 dialogs, and that transfer learning from larger corpora is likely necessary for effective model training. The dataset’s release, along with code, supports reproducibility and further research ([Li et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10742)).
