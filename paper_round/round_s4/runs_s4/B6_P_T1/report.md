# Datasets Used in the Cross-Lingual Parkinson's Disease Speech Classification Study

## Introduction

The study under review investigates a transfer learning scheme to improve the accuracy of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) when classifying Parkinson's disease (PD) from speech across different languages. The core research question addressed is whether initializing neural network weights with utterances from a different language than the test set can enhance model performance. To answer this, the authors employ speech recordings from three distinct language datasets: Spanish, German, and Czech. This report identifies and describes each dataset, including their composition, recording conditions, speech tasks, clinical evaluations, and how they are used in the transfer learning experiments. All factual details are drawn from the primary source document and a third-party research note that summarizes the study ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt); [Document 2, n.d.](document_2.txt)).

## Overview of the Three Language Datasets

The study uses speech recordings of patients in three different languages: Spanish, German, and Czech ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). All recordings were captured in noise-controlled conditions, and the speech signals were down-sampled to 16 kHz ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). The patients in all three datasets were evaluated by a neurologist expert according to the third section of the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS-III) ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). These datasets form the basis for training and evaluating both baseline models and CNN models, as well as for the transfer learning strategy across languages ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt); [Document 2, n.d.](document_2.txt)).

### Spanish Dataset: PC-GITA Corpus

The Spanish data come from the PC-GITA corpus ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). This corpus contains utterances from 50 PD patients and 50 healthy control (HC) speakers, all Colombian Spanish native speakers ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt); [Document 2, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The speaker composition is therefore 50 PD patients and 50 HC speakers ([Document 2, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Participants were asked to perform a variety of speech tasks: pronouncing a total of 10 sentences; rapid repetition of /pa-ta-ka/, /pe-ta-ka/, /pa-ka-ta/, /pa/, /ta/, and /ka/; one text with 36 words; and a monologue ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). All patients were in the ON state at the time of recording, meaning they were under the effect of their daily medication ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)).

### German Dataset

The German data consist of speech recordings from Germany ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). According to the primary source, speech recordings of 88 PD patients and 88 HC speakers from Germany are considered ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). However, a third-party research note states that the German data consist of 44 PD patients and 44 HC speakers, and later reiterates that the speaker counts for the German data are 44 PD patients with 44 HC speakers ([Document 2, n.d.](document_2.txt)). This discrepancy is notable and will be discussed later. The speech tasks performed by German participants include the rapid repetition of /pa-ta-ka/, 5 sentences, one text with 81 words, and a monologue ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). Table 1 in the primary source provides detailed demographic and clinical information for German participants, which supports the count of 88 PD and 88 HC ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)).

### Czech Dataset

The Czech dataset contains a total of 100 native Czech speakers, comprising 50 PD patients and 50 healthy controls ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt); [Document 2, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The speech tasks performed by the participants include the rapid repetition of the syllables /pa-ta-ka/, a read text with 80 words, and a monologue ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). Table 1 in the primary source confirms this composition, showing 30 male and 20 female PD patients, and 30 male and 20 female HC speakers ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). The Czech recordings supply the third language-specific portion of the cross-lingual speech data ([Document 2, n.d.](document_2.txt)).

## Recording Conditions and Preprocessing

All speech recordings across the three datasets were captured in noise-controlled conditions ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). The signals were down-sampled to 16 kHz ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). These standardized conditions help ensure that differences in model performance across languages are less likely to be caused by environmental noise or sampling rate variations. The patients in all datasets were evaluated by a neurologist expert using the MDS-UPDRS-III ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). This clinical evaluation provides a common metric for disease severity across the language groups, which is essential for interpreting cross-lingual transfer learning results.

## Speech Tasks Across Datasets

The speech tasks differ somewhat across the three datasets, which is an important characteristic of the data. In the Spanish PC-GITA corpus, participants performed 10 sentences, rapid syllable repetitions (/pa-ta-ka/, /pe-ta-ka/, /pa-ka-ta/, /pa/, /ta/, /ka/), a 36-word text, and a monologue ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). The Czech dataset included rapid repetition of /pa-ta-ka/, an 80-word read text, and a monologue ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). The German dataset included rapid repetition of /pa-ta-ka/, 5 sentences, an 81-word text, and a monologue ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). These tasks are designed to elicit different aspects of speech production that may be affected by PD, such as articulation, prosody, and voice quality. The study considered all speech exercises performed by participants for the classification strategy, and the final decision for each speaker was obtained by a majority voting strategy among the different speech exercises ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)).

## Segmentation and Feature Representation

Although the query focuses on datasets, it is relevant to note how the speech signals from these datasets were processed for modeling. The speech signals were analyzed based on automatic detection of onset and offset transitions, which model the difficulties of patients to start and stop the movement of the vocal folds ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). This detection is based on the presence of the fundamental frequency of speech in short-time frames ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). The border between voiced and unvoiced frames is detected, and 80 ms of the signal is taken to the left and right, forming segments with 160 ms length ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). Two modeling approaches were used: a baseline model based on hand-crafted features classified with an SVM, and a CNN model based on time-frequency representations ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). For the CNN, time-frequency representations based on the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) were used as input ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). The STFT with 256 frequency bins was computed for each segmented transition, with a window length of 16 ms and a step-size of 4 ms, forming 41 time frames per transition ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). The spectrogram was then transformed into the Mel-scale using 80 filters, forming a spectrogram of size 80 × 41, which was used to train the CNNs ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). These processing steps were applied to the speech data from all three language datasets.

## Use of Datasets in Transfer Learning Experiments

The three datasets are used in two stages. First, baseline and CNN models are trained considering each language individually ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). Then, the trained CNNs for each language are used as base models in the transfer learning strategy to improve accuracy in the other two languages ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). A CNN trained with utterances from one language (the base language) is fine-tuned with utterances from the target language ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). The results show that transfer learning improved accuracy considerably when the target languages were German and Czech ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). Specifically, accuracy improved over 8% for German (from 69.3% in the baseline to 77.3% when the model is fine-tuned from Spanish), and over 4.1% for Czech (from 68.5% with the initial CNN to 72.6% when the model is fine-tuned from Spanish) ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). The highest accuracy for German and Czech was obtained when the base language was Spanish, which is explained by the Spanish speakers having the best initial separability, meaning the other two languages benefit from the best initial model ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). This finding underscores the central role of the Spanish PC-GITA dataset in the study's transfer learning framework.

## Discussion of Data Discrepancies and Reliability

A critical examination of the provided sources reveals a discrepancy in the reported size of the German dataset. The primary source, Document 1, states that speech recordings of 88 PD patients and 88 HC speakers from Germany are considered ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). Table 1 in the same document supports this count, listing 47 male and 41 female PD patients (total 88) and 44 male and 44 female HC speakers (total 88) ([Document 1, n.d.](document_1.txt)). However, the third-party research note, Document 2, states that the German data consist of 44 PD patients and 44 HC speakers, and later repeats this figure ([Document 2, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Given that Document 1 is the primary study description with a detailed demographic table, it is more reliable for dataset composition. Document 2 appears to contain an error, possibly confusing the number of male and female HC speakers (44 each) with the total PD and HC counts. Therefore, based on the most reliable available information, the German dataset should be understood as comprising 88 PD patients and 88 HC speakers. This discrepancy highlights the importance of consulting primary sources when extracting dataset specifications.

## Conclusion and Opinion

The datasets used in this study are three language-specific speech corpora: the Spanish PC-GITA corpus, a German speech dataset, and a Czech speech dataset. The Spanish PC-GITA contains 50 PD patients and 50 HC speakers, all Colombian Spanish native speakers, with a comprehensive set of speech tasks and the highest recorded disease severity according to MDS-UPDRS-III. The German dataset, based on the primary source, contains 88 PD patients and 88 HC speakers, though a secondary source reports 44 PD and 44 HC. The Czech dataset contains 50 PD patients and 50 HC speakers. All recordings were made in noise-controlled conditions, down-sampled to 16 kHz, and clinically evaluated with MDS-UPDRS-III. These datasets are used to train baseline and CNN models and to implement a cross-lingual transfer learning strategy. In my assessment, the Spanish PC-GITA dataset is the most robust and influential of the three because its higher disease severity and better initial separability make it the optimal base model for transfer learning to German and Czech. The reported improvements of up to 8% in accuracy demonstrate the value of this dataset combination. However, the German sample size discrepancy in the secondary source should be noted as a limitation in any replication or meta-analysis. Future work could benefit from resolving this discrepancy and from using the described datasets for additional experiments, such as hyper-parameter optimization, multi-language base models, and transfer learning among neurological diseases.
