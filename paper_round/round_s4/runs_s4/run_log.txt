[32m# How Many Hand-Crafted Templates Were Required? A Detailed Analysis of Question Generation from a Knowledge Base

[0m
[32m## Introduction
[0m
[32mQuestion generation from a knowledge base (KB) is the task of generating questions related to the domain of the input KB ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). The challenge is that function words and morphological forms for entities are abstracted away when a KB is created ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). Previous work relies on massive human-labeled data to address this challenge ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). The proposed system reduces human effort by leveraging massive web resources ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). The only human labor in this work is the question template construction ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). Therefore, a central question is: how many hand-crafted templates did the authors have to make? Based on the provided sources, the answer is not a single number because the paper reports two separate evaluations: 106 hand-crafted templates for the Freebase evaluation and 163 hand-crafted templates for the in-house power tool domain knowledge base ([Document 1](document_1.txt); [Document 2](document_2.txt)). If one combines both reported efforts, the total is 269 templates, but the authors present them as distinct experimental setups. This report analyzes those counts, their derivation, and their significance.

[32m## The Role of Templates in the Question Generation System
[0m
[32mThe system first constructs a small set of question templates ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). Each template is associated with a predicate in the KB ([Document 1](document_1.txt); [Document 2](document_2.txt)). Templates consist of a transcription of the predicate (e.g., performsActivity ⇒ how to) and placeholders for the subject (#X#) and the object (#Y#) ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). A seed question set is generated by applying the templates on the KB ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). For example, template “how to use #X#” is constructed for predicate “performsActivity” ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). Applying it to triple ⟨jigsaw, performsActivity, CurveCut⟩ yields seed question “how to use jigsaw” ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). Then more questions are retrieved from a search engine by iteratively forming already obtained questions as search queries ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). Finally, questions are selected by estimating fluency and domain relevance ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). Because template construction is the only manual step, the number of templates directly reflects the human effort required ([Document 1](document_1.txt); [Document 2](document_2.txt)).

[32m## The Function of Templates in the Proposed System

[0m
[32mThe proposed system generates fluent and natural questions from a knowledge base by leveraging massive web resources ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The overall pipeline includes question template construction, seed question generation, question expansion, and question selection ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Templates are associated with predicates in the knowledge base. For example, the predicate “performsActivity” can be transcribed into the question form “how to use #X#,” where #X# is a placeholder for the subject and #Y# is a placeholder for the object ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). A seed question set is then generated by applying these templates to the triples in the knowledge base ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). After that, each generated question is iteratively used as a search query in a standard search engine such as Google or Bing to retrieve more related question candidates ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Finally, a selection step estimates the fluency and domain relevance of each candidate ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)).

[0m
[32mThe paper emphasizes that this design significantly reduces human effort because the iterative question expansion can produce a large number of questions even with a relatively small number of seed questions ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). In addition, multiple entities in the knowledge base share the same predicates, so the template set does not need to scale linearly with the number of triples ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Another advantage is that the system can easily generate updated questions as the web is self-updating ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Nevertheless, template construction remains the only manual component, making the count of hand-crafted templates a critical measure of the method’s human cost.

[32m## Why the Template Count Matters

[0m
[32mThe number of hand-crafted templates is not merely a technical detail. It directly measures the amount of human effort required by the system. Because the system relies on web exploration to expand the seed question set, the authors do not need a large number of templates. The iterative question expansion can produce a large number of questions even with a relatively small number of seed questions ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The template count therefore scales with the number of distinct predicates rather than the number of triples, which is a significant efficiency gain.

[32m## Signal Processing and Segmentation Derived from the Datasets
[0m
[32mThe datasets are not used in raw waveform form alone. Speech signals are analyzed based on the automatic detection of onset and offset transitions, which model the difficulties of patients to start or stop the movement of the vocal folds ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The detection of transitions is based on the presence of the fundamental frequency of speech in short-time frames ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The border between voiced and unvoiced frames is detected, and 80 ms of the signal are taken to the left and to the right, forming segments with 160 ms length ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). These transition segments are modeled with two approaches: a baseline model based on hand-crafted features classified using an SVM, and a model based on time-frequency representations used as input to train a CNN ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Time-frequency representations based on the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) are used as input to a CNN. The STFT with 256 frequency bins is computed for each segmented transition, for a window length of 16 ms and a step-size of 4 ms, forming 41 time frames per transition. The obtained spectrogram is transformed into the Mel-scale using 80 filters, forming a spectrogram with a size of 80 × 41, which is used to train the CNNs ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). These derived representations are the actual inputs to the models, but the underlying datasets remain the three language-specific speech corpora.

[32m## Role of the Datasets in Transfer Learning
[0m
[32mThe three datasets are used in a transfer learning strategy that aims to improve the accuracy of models when the weights of the neural network are initialized with utterances from a different language than the one used for the test set ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). CNNs trained with utterances from one language are used to initialize a model to classify speech utterances from PD patients in a different language ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The models are evaluated with speech utterances in Spanish, German, and Czech ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The experiments first train the baseline and CNN models considering each language individually. Then, the trained CNNs for each language are used as a base model in the transfer learning strategy to improve accuracy in the other two languages ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The final decision for each speaker is obtained by a majority voting strategy among the different speech exercises ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)).

[0m
[32mThe results indicate that the transfer learning among languages improved the accuracy of the models in up to 8% when a base model trained with Spanish utterances is used to fine-tune a model to classify PD German utterances ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). For example, the Spanish base model transferred to German achieved 77.3% accuracy (SD = 11.3), with sensitivity 86.2% (SD = 13.8), specificity 68.3% (SD = 14.3), and MCC 0.57 ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The Spanish base model transferred to Czech achieved 76.7% accuracy (SD = 7.9), sensitivity 87.5% (SD = 11.0), specificity 66.0% (SD = 15.6), and MCC 0.55 ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The German base model transferred to Spanish achieved 70.0% accuracy (SD = 12.5), and to Czech achieved 72.0% accuracy (SD = 13.1) ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The Czech base model transferred to Spanish achieved 72.6% accuracy (SD = 13.9), and to German achieved 70.7% accuracy (SD = 14.5) ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The results obtained after transfer learning were also more balanced in terms of specificity and sensitivity and had a lower variance than those trained without the transfer learning strategy ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The transfer learning scheme was accurate to improve accuracy in the target language only when the base model was robust enough, which was observed when the model trained with Spanish utterances was used to initialize the models for German and Czech languages ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)).

[32m## Strengths and Limitations of the Datasets
[0m
[32mThe three datasets offer several strengths for cross-lingual PD speech classification. First, each dataset includes both PD patients and HC subjects, allowing binary classification ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt); [document_2.txt](document_2.txt)). The German dataset has more male than female PD patients (47 vs. 41), and the Czech dataset has a notable sex imbalance among PD patients (30 male vs. 20 female) ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The Spanish PD group had higher average MDS-UPDRS-III scores than the German and Czech groups, indicating higher disease severity, which may affect cross-lingual generalization ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The specific speech tasks for the German dataset are not enumerated in the provided excerpts, although the study states that all speech exercises were used ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Additionally, the datasets differ in size: German has 176 speakers, while Spanish and Czech each have 100 speakers ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt); [document_2.txt](document_2.txt)). These differences should be considered when interpreting the transfer learning results.

[0m
[32m### Spanish: The PC-GITA Corpus

[0m
[32mThe Spanish portion of the data comes from the PC-GITA corpus ([document_2.txt](#ref-doc2)). PC-GITA contains utterances from 50 PD patients and 50 healthy control (HC) speakers, all Colombian Spanish native speakers, giving a total of 100 speakers and a perfectly balanced 1:1 case–control design ([document_2.txt](#ref-doc2)). The corpus is attributed to the Spanish speech database described by Orozco-Arroyave and colleagues for the analysis of people suffering from Parkinson's disease ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)).

[0m
[32mPC-GITA is not exclusive to this study. It had previously been used in phonation and tremor analysis work based on several time–frequency representations, in which energy and entropy features were classified with Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) and support vector machines (SVMs), reporting accuracies of up to 77% ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)). This prior use matters for interpreting the present results, because the Spanish data are therefore the best-characterised and arguably the most methodologically mature of the three language subsets.

[0m
[32m### German Speech Recordings

[0m
[32mThe German data consist of speech recordings from 44 PD patients and 44 HC speakers ([document_2.txt](#ref-doc2)). As with the Spanish set, the case–control distribution is balanced, yielding 88 speakers in total. The German data are associated with the vowel-articulation work of Skodda, Visser and Schlegel published in the *Journal of Voice* ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)). The supplied extracts do not itemise the specific speech tasks performed by the German participants in the way that they do for the Czech participants; the report therefore treats the German set as a balanced 44/44 PD–HC corpus without a documented task list in the available evidence.

[0m
[32m### Czech Speech Recordings

[0m
[32mThe Czech dataset comprises a total of 100 native Czech speakers, of whom 50 are PD patients and 50 are healthy controls ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1); [document_2.txt](#ref-doc2)). Unlike the other two sets, the specific elicitation tasks are documented: participants performed the rapid repetition of the syllables /pa-ta-ka/, read a text of 80 words, and produced a monologue ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)). The Czech data derive from the work of Rusz and colleagues on the automatic evaluation of articulatory disorders in Parkinson's disease ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)).

[0m
[32mIt is notable that the Czech protocol combines a diadochokinetic task (rapid syllable repetition), a controlled reading task, and a spontaneous-speech task. This heterogeneity is methodologically valuable: it allows the classification pipeline to draw on complementary articulatory, prosodic and fluency information, and it explains why the study aggregates decisions across exercises.

[32mTwo observations follow directly from this table. First, the three corpora are of comparable size (88–100 speakers), which is important because transfer learning performance depends heavily on the representativeness of the base model. Second, all three are balanced between classes, so accuracy is an interpretable metric in the baseline condition and any drift toward sensitivity or specificity reflects genuine model behaviour rather than class priors.

[0m
[32m## Clinical Annotation and Severity Distribution

[0m
[32mAll three datasets share a common clinical anchor: each participant was rated by a neurologist according to the MDS-UPDRS-III ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)). This shared scale is what makes cross-lingual pooling analytically defensible, since it provides a language-independent measure of disease severity that can be used to explain differences in classification performance between languages.

[0m
[32mThat explanatory role is explicit in the study's own interpretation. The authors attribute the differing results across the three languages to the information summarised in their Table 1: the average MDS-UPDRS-III score of the Spanish patients is higher than that of the German and Czech patients, meaning the Spanish data contain patients with greater disease severity ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)). Since more severe disease generally produces more marked dysarthric symptoms, the Spanish subset is the "easiest" of the three languages to classify — a pattern confirmed by the baseline results described later in this report.

[0m
[32mThe study also documents that the speech effects of dysarthria in PD include increased acoustic noise, reduced intensity, harsh and breathy voice quality, increased voice nasality, monopitch, monoloudness, speech rate disturbances, imprecise articulation of consonants, and the involuntary introduction of pauses ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)). These are the clinical phenomena that the automated pipeline is designed to capture, and the MDS-UPDRS-III scores provide the corresponding clinical ground truth.

[0m
[32m## Recording Conditions and Preprocessing Pipeline

[0m
[32mThe three datasets were harmonised before modelling. All recordings were captured under noise-controlled conditions and down-sampled to 16 kHz ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)). Segmentation was based on the automatic detection of onset and offset transitions, which model the patients' difficulties in starting and stopping the movement of the vocal folds; the detection of these transitions relies on the presence of the fundamental frequency of speech in short-time frames ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)). The border between voiced and unvoiced frames is identified, and 80 ms of signal is taken to the left and to the right of that border, producing segments of 160 ms ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)).

[0m
[32mFor the CNN branch, short-time Fourier transform (STFT) representations are computed for each segmented transition with 256 frequency bins, using a window length of 16 ms and a step size of 4 ms, forming 41 time frames per transition ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)). Each spectrogram is then transformed to the Mel scale using 80 filters, giving a final input of 80 × 41 ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)). The summarised pipeline parameters are:

[0m
[32m|---|---|
[0m

[0m
[32m| Segment definition | 80 ms either side of the voiced/unvoiced border |
[0m
[32m| Segment length | 160 ms |
[0m
[32m| STFT frequency bins | 256 |
[0m
[32m| STFT window length | 16 ms |
[0m
[32m| STFT step size | 4 ms |
[0m
[32m| Time frames per transition | 41 |
[0m
[32m| Mel filters | 80 |
[0m
[32m| Final spectrogram size | 80 × 41 |

[32mRead against the monolingual baselines, the transfer results show that the best German model improves from 69.3% (baseline) and 63.1% (monolingual CNN) to 77.3% when the CNN is initialised with Spanish utterances — an improvement of roughly 8 percentage points over the strongest German-only result, and more than 14 points over the German CNN ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)). [32m## Datasets Referenced in the Related Work but Not Used

[0m
[32mThe reference list of the source document also includes the Parkinson speech dataset with multiple types of sound recordings collected by Sakar and colleagues and published in the *IEEE Journal of Biomedical and Health Informatics* ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)). This corpus is cited in the related-work context, but the extracts do not indicate that it contributes speakers to the present experiments; the analysed data are limited to the Spanish (PC-GITA), German and Czech sets. Separately, the study's own discussion references prior work in which the same three-language data were modelled with a deep-learning articulation approach, reporting accuracies between 70% and 89% depending on language, but below 60% in a language-independent scenario where the CNN was trained on one language and tested on the other two ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)). The present study is therefore best understood as an attempt to solve the generalisation failure observed on exactly these datasets.

[0m
[32m## Limitations and Analytical Commentary

[0m
[32mThree limitations concerning the datasets are worth stating plainly. First, the datasets are not perfectly matched on clinical severity: the Spanish patients have higher average MDS-UPDRS-III scores than the German and Czech patients, which confounds language with disease severity ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)). This is almost certainly why the Spanish models — and the Spanish-initialised transfer models — perform best. The reported 8% gain may therefore reflect, in part, the transfer of a model trained on a more severely affected population, rather than the transfer of purely linguistic or acoustic invariances. Second, sample sizes are modest (88–100 speakers per language), and the reported standard deviations are substantial in several conditions (for example, a specificity of 42.0 with a standard deviation of 33.2 for the Czech CNN) ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)). Third, the German task inventory is not documented in the available evidence, so the degree of task-level comparability across the three corpora cannot be verified from these sources alone.

[0m
[32mThese caveats are relevant to how the datasets should be reused. The study itself proposes future work that would address them: more robust base models via hyper-parameter optimisation such as Bayesian optimisation, base models trained on two languages rather than one, evaluation of PD staging based on MDS-UPDRS-III or dysarthria severity, and transfer learning across diseases — for instance training a base model on PD and using it to initialise a classifier for Huntington's disease ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)).

[0m
[32m## Conclusion

[0m
[32mThe study draws on three language-specific speech datasets of Parkinson's disease patients and healthy controls: the Spanish PC-GITA corpus (50 PD, 50 HC, Colombian Spanish speakers), German speech recordings (44 PD, 44 HC), and Czech speech recordings (50 PD, 50 HC native Czech speakers performing /pa-ta-ka/ repetition, an 80-word reading task and a monologue) ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1); [document_2.txt](#ref-doc2)). All three were recorded under noise-controlled conditions, down-sampled to 16 kHz, clinically annotated with the MDS-UPDRS-III, and processed identically into 160 ms voiced/unvoiced transition segments and 80 × 41 Mel spectrograms. The datasets are balanced in class composition but not in disease severity, and this imbalance is the most plausible explanation for both the higher baseline accuracy observed for Spanish and the disproportionate success of the Spanish-initialised transfer models, which raised target-language accuracy by up to roughly 8 percentage points over the strongest monolingual German result ([document_1.txt](#ref-doc1)).

[32m# Hand-Crafted Template Counts in Question Generation from a Knowledge Base with Web Exploration

[0m
[32m## Direct Answer to the Query

[0m
[32mThe authors reported two separate template-construction efforts. For the Freebase evaluation, they hand-crafted **106 templates** ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). For the in-house power tool domain knowledge base, they hand-crafted **163 templates** ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Across both evaluations, this amounts to **269 hand-crafted templates**. These templates were the only human labor in the system, as the paper explicitly states that the only human labor in this work is question template construction ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt); [document_2.txt](document_2.txt)). The template counts are tied to the predicates present in each knowledge base: 53 distinct predicates in the Freebase sample and 67 distinct predicates in the power tool KB ([document_2.txt](document_2.txt)).

[0m
[32m## Introduction and Context

[0m
[32mQuestion generation from a knowledge base (KB) is the task of generating questions related to the domain of the input KB ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). This task is challenging because function words and morphological forms for entities are abstracted away when a KB is created ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Previous work, such as Seyler et al. (2015) and Serban et al. (2016), relies on massive human-labeled data ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). For example, one approach treats question generation as a machine translation problem and trains a neural machine translation (NMT) system with 10,000 ⟨triple, question⟩ pairs ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The question part of those pairs is human generated, which requires a large amount of human effort, and the grammaticality and naturalness of generated questions cannot be guaranteed ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). In contrast, the proposed system significantly reduces human effort by leveraging massive web resources ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)).

[0m
[32mThe proposed system consists of four sub-modules: question template construction, seed question generation, question expansion, and selection ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Given an input KB, a small set of question templates is first constructed such that each template is associated with a predicate ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). A seed question set is then generated by applying the templates on the KB ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). More questions are retrieved from a search engine by iteratively forming already-obtained questions as search queries ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Finally, questions are selected by estimating their fluency and domain relevance ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The only human labor in this work is the question template construction ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Therefore, the number of hand-crafted templates is a central metric of the system's human-effort requirements.

[0m
[32m## Template Construction in the Freebase Evaluation

[0m
[32m### Predicate Distribution and Template Design

[0m
[32mFor the Freebase evaluation, the authors used 500 randomly selected triples from Freebase ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt); [document_2.txt](document_2.txt)). These 500 triples share only 53 distinct predicates ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt); [document_2.txt](document_2.txt)). To cover these predicates, the authors hand-crafted 106 templates ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt); [document_2.txt](document_2.txt)). On average, this is two templates for each predicate ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt); [document_2.txt](document_2.txt)). This design ties template creation to predicates, keeping the template set compact relative to the triple set ([document_2.txt](document_2.txt)).

[0m
[32m### Seed Question Generation and Expansion

[0m
[32mApplying the 106 templates to the Freebase triples generated 991 seed questions ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt); [document_2.txt](document_2.txt)). The system then retrieved 1529 more questions from Google ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt); [document_2.txt](document_2.txt)). This expansion step uses an iterative algorithm. As shown in Algorithm 1, the expanded question set E is initialized as the seed question set (Line 1) ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). In each iteration, an already-obtained question is expanded from the web, and retrieved questions are added to E if E does not contain them (Lines 6-10) ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The maximum number of iterations is limited by I_max (Line 4) ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). This approach allows a relatively small number of seed questions to produce a large number of candidate questions.

[0m
[32mFor fluency evaluation, the authors trained a 4-gram language model (LM) on Gigaword (LDC2011T07) with Kneser-Ney smoothing ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Using the averaged language model score as an index, the top 500 questions were selected to compare with the baseline results ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Three native English speakers evaluated the fluency and naturalness of both results on a 4-point scheme where 4 is the best ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The system's generated questions received a grammaticality score of 3.53 and a naturalness score of 3.31, compared with the baseline's 3.36 and 3.14, respectively ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Table 1 provides examples comparing generated questions, and Table 2 presents the human ratings ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). These results indicate that the 106 hand-crafted templates, combined with web expansion and selection, produced questions judged more fluent than the baseline ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)).

[32m## Template Construction in the Power Tool Domain Evaluation

[0m
[32m### Domain-Specific KB Characteristics

[0m
[32mThe third experiment was conducted on an in-house KB in the power tool domain ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). This KB contains 67 distinct predicates, 293 distinct subjects, and 279 distinct objects ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). For the 67 predicates, the authors hand-crafted 163 templates ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt); [document_2.txt](document_2.txt)). [32m### Seed Question Generation and Expansion

[0m
[32mUsing the 163 templates, the system generated 12,228 seed questions ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). From these, 20,000 more questions were expanded with Google ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The authors used the same language model as in the first experiment and learned a skip-gram model on Wikipedia for evaluating domain relevance ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Table 4 shows example expanded questions, including "how to change circular saw blade," "how to measure lawn mower cutting height," "how to sharpen drill bits on bench grinder," "how does an oscillating multi tool work," "how to cut a groove in wood without a router," "what type of sander to use on deck," "do I need a hammer drill," "can I use acrylic paint on wood," and "how to use a sharpening stone with oil" ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The authors note that most expanded questions are grammatical and relevant to the power tool domain ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Most are informative and correspond to a specific answer, except "do I need a hammer drill," which lacks context information ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). In addition to simple factoid questions, the system generates many complex questions such as "how to cut a groove in wood without a router" ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). This demonstrates that 163 hand-crafted templates can yield a large and diverse set of questions when combined with web expansion.

[32m## Why the Template Counts Matter

[0m
[32mThe number of hand-crafted templates is the primary measure of human effort in this system. The authors state that the only human labor in this work is question template construction ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). By keeping the template set small, the system avoids the massive human-labeled data required by previous NMT-based approaches, which used 10,000 ⟨triple, question⟩ pairs ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). For Freebase, 106 templates covered 53 predicates across 500 triples ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). For the power tool domain, 163 templates covered 67 predicates and enabled the generation of over 32,000 questions (12,228 seed plus 20,000 expanded) ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The difference between 106 and 163 templates reflects the different predicate sets and the domain-specific nature of the power tool KB ([document_2.txt](document_2.txt)). The Freebase triples shared only 53 distinct predicates, while the power tool KB had 67 distinct predicates ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The authors made two templates per predicate on average for Freebase and a somewhat higher average for the power tool domain ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt); [document_2.txt](document_2.txt)).

[0m
[32mThe evaluation results also support the effectiveness of this approach. Human graders judged the system's questions to be more fluent than the baseline's on 500 randomly selected Freebase triples ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The grammaticality score was 3.53 versus 3.36, and the naturalness score was 3.31 versus 3.14 ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). These ratings were collected from three native English speakers using a 4-point scheme ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The authors note that word embeddings capture the similarity between distinct words, which helps the system outperform previous methods ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). This suggests that even with a limited number of hand-crafted templates, the combination of web expansion and embedding-based selection can yield high-quality questions.

[32m## Limitations and Open Questions

[0m
[32mSeveral limitations are evident from the provided information. First, the template counts are specific to the evaluations reported. The Freebase evaluation used 500 randomly selected triples, while the power tool evaluation used an in-house KB with 67 predicates ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Scaling to a new domain would require new templates, as template creation follows the predicates present in the KB ([document_2.txt](document_2.txt)). Second, the exact number of templates needed depends on how many distinct predicates are present and how many templates the authors choose to create per predicate. The paper reports averages but not the full distribution of templates per predicate ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Third, the baseline system is anonymized in the provided text as "? )" ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Although the references list Seyler et al. (2015) and Serban et al. (2016), the provided information does not explicitly identify which baseline was used for the human evaluation ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). Finally, the paper does not report the time or cost of constructing the 106 or 163 templates, only the counts ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). These open questions do not diminish the clear answer to the query: the authors hand-crafted 106 templates for Freebase and 163 templates for the power tool domain.

[32mIn answer to the query "How many hand-crafted templates did they have to make?", the provided sources report two figures. [32m# Hand-Crafted Template Counts in *Question Generation from a Knowledge Base with Web Exploration*

[0m
[32m## Introduction

[0m
[32mQuestion generation from a knowledge base (KB) is the task of generating questions related to the domain of the input KB ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). The problem is challenging because function words and morphological forms for entities are abstracted away when a KB is created ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). Previous work has addressed this challenge by relying on massive human-labeled data; for example, one approach trains a neural machine translation (NMT) system with 10,000 triple–question pairs, where the question part is human generated ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). That requirement imposes a large amount of human effort and does not guarantee grammaticality or naturalness ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). The paper *Question Generation from a Knowledge Base with Web Exploration* proposes a system that significantly reduces human effort by leveraging massive web resources ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). In this system, the only human labor is question template construction ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt); [Research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The central question for this report is: how many hand-crafted templates did the authors have to make? The provided sources report two evaluation settings and, in one case, conflicting numbers. This report analyzes those counts, explains the template design, and provides a concrete assessment.

[0m
[32m## The Role of Hand-Crafted Templates

[0m
[32mThe proposed system uses a small set of question templates. Each template is associated with a predicate in the KB ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). A template consists of a transcription of the predicate in the KB—for example, `performsActivity` might become “how to”—and placeholders for the subject (`#X#`) and the object (`#Y#`) ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). A seed question set is generated by applying these templates to the KB ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). The seed questions are then expanded iteratively by using already obtained questions as search queries in a standard search engine, such as Google or Bing, to retrieve more related question candidates ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). Finally, a selection step estimates fluency and domain relevance to choose the best questions ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). Because templates are tied to predicates rather than triples, and because multiple entities in the KB share the same predicates, the authors can cover a large number of triples with a relatively small template set ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). The paper explicitly states that the only human labor in this work is question template construction ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). A third-party research note confirms that template construction is the manual component reported ([Research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)).

[0m
[32m## Freebase Evaluation: 106 Hand-Crafted Templates

[32mIn the first evaluation, the authors randomly selected 500 triples from Freebase ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt); [Research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). For these predicates, the authors hand-crafted 106 templates, which corresponds to an average of two templates per predicate ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt); [Research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Applying the templates to the triples generated 991 seed questions ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt); [Research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The experiment also retrieved 1,529 additional questions from Google ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt); [Research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). To evaluate fluency, the authors trained a 4-gram language model on Gigaword with Kneser–Ney smoothing and selected the top 500 questions by averaged language model score ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). Three native English speakers then rated grammaticality and naturalness on a four-point scheme ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). The system’s questions were rated 3.53 for grammaticality and 3.31 for naturalness, compared with 3.36 and 3.14 for the baseline ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). [32m## Why the Manual Effort Remains Low

[0m
[32mThe template counts are modest relative to the size of the KBs and the number of generated questions. The paper explains that a large number of templates is not required because iterative question expansion can produce many questions from a small seed set, and because multiple entities in the KB share the same predicates ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). For example, in the Freebase evaluation, 106 templates applied to 500 triples yielded 991 seed questions, which were then expanded to 1,529 more questions via Google ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). In the power tool evaluation, 163 templates (per the primary source) generated 12,228 seed questions, which were expanded by 20,000 additional questions ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). This design contrasts sharply with the NMT approach described in the paper, which used 10,000 human-generated question pairs ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). The paper also notes that the system can easily generate updated questions because the web is self-updating ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt)). Thus, the human effort is concentrated on a small, predicate-level template set, while the web provides scale and freshness.

[32m## Implications and Limitations

[0m
[32mThe central implication of these counts is that the proposed system reduces manual annotation to template construction only ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt); [Research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The template set is associated with predicates, so the manual cost scales with predicate coverage rather than with the number of triples or entities ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt); [Research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). In the Freebase evaluation, 53 predicates required 106 templates, exactly two per predicate on average ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt); [Research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). In the power tool evaluation, 67 predicates required either 163 templates (primary) or 103 templates (secondary), which would be approximately 2.43 or 1.54 templates per predicate, respectively ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt); [Research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The discrepancy matters for any precise accounting of human effort, but it does not change the overall conclusion that the template set is small relative to the generated question volume. The third-party note also emphasizes that the Freebase and power tool counts show how manual template-building effort scales with predicate coverage in each evaluation ([Research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). A limitation of this report is that the provided corpus contains conflicting figures for the power tool domain and no independent verification of the total. Future work or access to the original paper’s template-construction section would be needed to resolve the 163 versus 103 discrepancy.

[32m## Conclusion

[0m
[32mThe answer to “How many hand-crafted templates did they have to make?” depends on which evaluation and which source is used. In the Freebase evaluation, the authors made 106 hand-crafted templates for 500 randomly selected triples sharing 53 distinct predicates ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt); [Research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). In the power tool domain evaluation, the primary paper excerpt reports 163 hand-crafted templates for 67 predicates, while a third-party research note reports 103 templates and a total of 209 across both evaluations ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt); [Research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Based on the primary source, the most reliable total is 106 + 163 = 269 hand-crafted templates. Based on the secondary note, the total is 106 + 103 = 209. Because the primary source is the paper itself and directly states 163 for the power tool domain, this report treats 269 as the most defensible answer, while flagging the 209 figure as a reported discrepancy. In either case, the manual template construction is the only human labor in the system, and the predicate-level design keeps the template count low relative to the thousands of questions generated ([Song & Zhao, 2016](document_1.txt); [Research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)).

[32m# Datasets Used in the Cross-Lingual Parkinson's Disease Speech Classification Study

[32mThe study uses three language-specific datasets: a Spanish dataset based on the PC-GITA corpus, a German dataset, and a Czech dataset ([Document 2](document_2.txt)). [0m
[32m### Spanish Dataset: PC-GITA Corpus

[0m
[32mThe Spanish data come from the PC-GITA corpus ([Document 2](document_2.txt)). PC-GITA contains utterances from 50 PD patients and 50 HC speakers who are Colombian Spanish native speakers ([Document 2](document_2.txt)). Document 1 specifies the same composition and cites the corpus as reference [6] ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). The participants were asked to perform a relatively broad set of speech tasks: they pronounced a total of 10 sentences, rapidly repeated the syllables /pa-ta-ka/, /pe-ta-ka/, /pa-ka-ta/, /pa/, /ta/, and /ka/, read one text with 36 words, and produced a monologue ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). All patients were in the ON state at the time of recording, meaning they were under the effect of their daily medication ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). This is a critical clinical detail because medication status can influence speech motor performance and therefore the acoustic characteristics of the recordings.

[0m
[32mThe PC-GITA corpus also appears in the study's literature review as a previously used resource. For example, earlier work proposed a phonation analysis based on time-frequency representations to assess tremor in PD speech, using classifiers such as Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) and support vector machines (SVMs), with accuracies up to 77% on utterances from PC-GITA ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). That earlier study also described PC-GITA as formed with utterances from 50 PD patients and 50 HC subjects who were Colombian Spanish native speakers ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). The present study therefore builds on a corpus that has been used in prior PD speech research, which supports its relevance and comparability.

[0m
[32m### German Dataset

[0m
[32mThe German dataset contains speech recordings of 88 PD patients and 88 HC speakers from Germany ([Document 2](document_2.txt)). This makes the German dataset the largest of the three in terms of speaker count, with 176 participants in total ([Document 2](document_2.txt)). The provided information does not name the German corpus explicitly, nor does it list the specific speech tasks performed by the German participants ([Document 2](document_2.txt)). It does, however, state that these recordings form part of the cross-lingual dataset used for the convolutional neural network (CNN) and transfer learning experiments ([Document 2](document_2.txt)). The German data are clinically evaluated in the same way as the Spanish and Czech data: patients were assessed by a neurologist expert according to the third section of the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS-III) ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). The German dataset is also important in the transfer learning results because a model trained on Spanish utterances was used to fine-tune a model for classifying German PD utterances, yielding an accuracy improvement of up to 8% ([Document 1](document_1.txt)).

[0m
[32m### Czech Dataset

[32m## Recording Conditions and Preprocessing

[0m
[32mAll recordings in the three datasets were captured under noise-controlled conditions ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). The speech signals were down-sampled to 16 kHz ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). For modeling, the speech signals were analyzed based on automatic detection of onset and offset transitions, which are intended to model patients' difficulties in starting and stopping vocal fold movement ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). The detection of transitions is based on the presence of the fundamental frequency of speech in short-time frames ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). The border between voiced and unvoiced frames is detected, and 80 ms of the signal are taken to the left and right, forming segments with 160 ms length ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). These transition segments are the units used by both the baseline and CNN models ([Document 1](document_1.txt)).

[32m## Feature Extraction and Model Inputs Derived from the Datasets

[0m
[32mTwo modeling approaches were applied to the segmented speech data. The baseline model uses hand-crafted features: 12 Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs) with their first and second derivatives, plus the log energy of the signal distributed into 22 Bark bands ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). This yields a total of 58 descriptors, and four statistical functionals (mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis) are computed for each descriptor, producing a 232-dimensional feature vector per utterance ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). Classification is performed with a radial basis SVM with margin parameter C = 10 and a Gaussian kernel with parameter γ = 0.0001, tested with a speaker-independent 10-fold cross-validation strategy ([Document 1](document_1.txt)).

[0m
[32mThe CNN model uses time-frequency representations based on the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). The STFT is computed for each segmented transition with 256 frequency bins, a window length of 16 ms, and a step size of 4 ms, forming 41 time frames per transition ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). The spectrogram is transformed into the Mel scale using 80 filters, forming an 80 × 41 spectrogram that is used to train the CNNs ([Document 1](document_1.txt)). These processing steps are applied to the speech data from all three language datasets, making the datasets comparable in terms of input representation even though the languages and speaker populations differ ([Document 1](document_1.txt)).

[32m## Introduction

[0m
[32mThe study under review investigates a transfer learning scheme to improve the accuracy of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) when classifying Parkinson's disease (PD) from speech across different languages. To answer this, the authors employ speech recordings from three distinct language datasets: Spanish, German, and Czech. This report identifies and describes each dataset, including their composition, recording conditions, speech tasks, clinical evaluations, and how they are used in the transfer learning experiments. [32m# Hand-Crafted Templates in Knowledge Base Question Generation: A Detailed Report

[0m
[32m## Introduction

[0m
[32mQuestion generation from a knowledge base (KB) is the task of generating questions related to the domain of the input KB ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)). The challenge is that function words and morphological forms for entities are abstracted away when a KB is created ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)). Previous work relies on massive human-labeled data, such as 10,000 ⟨triple, question⟩ pairs ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)). The proposed system reduces human effort by leveraging massive web resources ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)). The only human labor in this work is the question template construction ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)). Therefore, the number of hand-crafted templates is a central figure for understanding the system's manual cost.

[0m
[32mThe query asks: "How many hand-crafted templates did they have to make?" Based on the provided information, the paper reports two distinct template counts for two separate evaluations. For the Freebase evaluation on 500 randomly selected triples, the authors hand-crafted 106 templates ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). For the in-house power tool domain KB, the authors hand-crafted 163 templates ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). There is no single combined total reported across both experiments. Thus, the answer is 106 templates for the Freebase experiment and 163 templates for the domain-specific power tool experiment. This report details these figures, the context in which they were produced, and their implications.

[0m
[32m## Background on the System and Template Construction

[0m
[32mThe proposed system contains sub-modules of question template construction, seed question generation, question expansion, and selection ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)). Given an input KB, a small set of question templates is first constructed such that each template is associated with a predicate ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)). These templates consist of a transcription of the predicate in the KB (e.g., performsActivity ⇒ how to) and placeholders for the subject (#X#) and the object (#Y#) ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)). For example, template "how to use #X#" is constructed for predicate "performsActivity" ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)). A seed question set is then generated by applying the templates on the KB ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)). For instance, seed question "how to use jigsaw" is generated by applying the template on triple ⟨jigsaw, performsActivity, CurveCut⟩ ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)).

[0m
[32mThe seed question set is further expanded through a search engine (e.g., Google, Bing), by iteratively forming each generated question as a search query to retrieve more related question candidates ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)). Algorithm 1 describes the question expansion method: the expanded question set E is initialized as the seed question set, and in each iteration, an already-obtained question is expanded from the web and retrieved questions are added to E if E does not contain them ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)). The maximum number of iterations is limited by I_max ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)). Finally, a selection step is applied by estimating the fluency and domain relevance of each question candidate ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)).

[0m
[32mThe only human labor in this work is the question template construction ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The system does not require a large number of templates because: (1) the iterative question expansion can produce a large number of questions even with a relatively small number of seed questions, and (2) multiple entities in the KB share the same predicates ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)). Another advantage is that the system can easily generate updated questions as the web is self-updating consistently ([Song & Zhao, n.d.](document_1.txt)). Thus, template construction is the manual bottleneck, and its size is directly tied to the predicates present in the KB.

[32m### Segmentation

[0m
[32mSpeech signals were analyzed on the basis of automatic detection of onset and offset transitions, which model the difficulties patients experience in starting and stopping the movement of the vocal folds ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). [0m
[32mTime–frequency representations based on the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) served as CNN input ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)). The resulting spectrogram was transformed to the Mel scale with 80 filters, forming an 80 × 41 spectrogram used to train the CNNs ([document_1.txt](document_1.txt)).

The study's core research question concerns whether a CNN pre-trained on utterances from one language can be fine-tuned to improve classification accuracy in another language, and the choice of datasets is therefore not incidental but constitutive of the experimental design: without at least two parallel, clinically annotated, language-specific corpora, the transfer learning question could not be posed at all ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)).

[32mTwo source documents inform this report. The first is the study text itself, including its data description, tables of speaker demographics, baseline classification results, and cross-lingual transfer results ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). The second is a third-party research note that summarises the study's dataset composition and identifies the named corpora involved ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Where the two sources diverge, this report flags the discrepancy rather than resolving it silently.

[0m
[32m## 2. The Three Primary Speech Corpora

[0m
[32m### 2.1 Spanish Data: The PC-GITA Corpus

[0m
[32mThe Spanish portion of the data is drawn from the **PC-GITA corpus** ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). PC-GITA contains utterances from 50 PD patients and 50 healthy control (HC) speakers, all native speakers of Colombian Spanish ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). The third-party note confirms this composition and identifies PC-GITA as the source that "provides the Spanish portion of the cross-lingual speech data" ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The corpus is attributed to Orozco-Arroyave et al. (2014), cited as reference [6] in the study's reference list, and is also referenced in earlier related work where a phonation analysis based on time-frequency representations and classifiers including Gaussian mixture models and support vector machines reported accuracies of up to 77% on PC-GITA utterances ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)).

[0m
[32mA notable protocol detail is that all Spanish PD patients were recorded **in the ON state**, that is, under the effect of their daily medication ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). This is a substantive data characteristic that affects the interpretability of the speech signal as a marker of disease.

[0m
[32m### 2.2 German Data: German-Language Speech Recordings

[0m
[32mThe German data are described only as speech recordings of German PD patients and healthy controls; the provided material does not name the corpus ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). The third-party note states that the German data consist of speech recordings of 44 PD patients and 44 HC speakers ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). However, the primary source's demographic table indicates a larger German cohort: 47 male PD and 41 female PD speakers (88 PD total) alongside 44 male HC and 44 female HC speakers (88 HC total) ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). A German-language corpus of 88 PD and 88 HC speakers is consistent with the earlier study by Grósz, Busa-Fekete, Gosztolya, and Tóth, cited as reference [11] in the reviewed work ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). The most defensible reading is that the German cohort comprises roughly 88 PD and 88 HC speakers, with the "44/44" figure in the third-party note plausibly describing the male speakers only; the discrepancy is nevertheless recorded here as a data-provenance uncertainty.

[0m
[32m### 2.3 Czech Data: Czech-Language Speech Recordings

[0m
[32mThe Czech data comprise a total of 100 native Czech speakers, of whom 50 have PD and 50 are healthy controls ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The demographic table disaggregates this as 30 male PD, 20 female PD, 30 male HC, and 20 female HC speakers ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). The Czech recordings are cited to reference [19] in the study's bibliography, though the corresponding citation details are not reproduced in the provided material ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). The third-party note describes these as "Czech speech recordings" supplying the third language of the cross-lingual dataset base ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)).

[32m## 3. Recording Conditions, Clinical Assessment, and Speech Tasks

[0m
[32m### 3.1 Acquisition Conditions

[0m
[32mAll recordings across the three datasets were captured under **noise-controlled conditions**, and the speech signals were **down-sampled to 16 kHz** ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). These two constraints establish the acoustic comparability of the corpora at the signal level and are prerequisites for the cross-corpus transfer learning design.

[0m
[32m### 3.2 Clinical Annotation

[0m
[32mIn all three datasets, patients were evaluated by a **neurologist expert** according to the third section of the Movement Disorder Society–sponsored revision of the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS-III) ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). This provides a common, clinician-administered severity metric across languages and is the basis for the severity-based explanation of cross-lingual performance differences.

[0m
[32m### 3.3 Speech Tasks

[0m
[32mThe speech tasks differ across corpora, which is an important consideration for anyone reusing these datasets:

[0m
[32m| Task type | Spanish (PC-GITA) | German | Czech |
[0m
[32m|---|---|---|---|
[0m
[32m| Sentences | 10 sentences | Not reported | Not reported |
[0m
[32m| Diadochokinetic rapid repetition | /pa-ta-ka/, /pe-ta-ka/, /pa-ka-ta/ | Not reported | /pa-ta-ka/ |
[0m
[32m| Isolated syllables | /pa/, /ta/, /ka/ | Not reported | Not reported |
[0m
[32m| Read text | 36-word text | Not reported | 80-word text |
[0m
[32m| Monologue | Yes | Not reported | Yes |

[0m
[32mSource: ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). "Not reported" indicates that the provided material does not specify the task set for that corpus. All speech exercises performed by participants were considered in the classification strategy, and a final decision per speaker was obtained through **majority voting across the different speech exercises** ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)).

[0m
[32m## 4. Derived Data Structures Generated from the Corpora

[0m
[32mThe raw recordings were not fed directly to the classifiers. Instead, the study generated derived representations:

[0m
[32m- **Transition segmentation.** Onset and offset transitions were detected automatically based on the presence of the fundamental frequency of speech in short-time frames. The border between voiced and unvoiced frames was identified and 80 ms of signal was taken to the left and right, producing segments of **160 ms** in length ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)).
[0m
[32m- **Time-frequency representations.** Short-time Fourier transform (STFT) spectrograms with **256 frequency bins** were computed for each segmented transition, using a window length of **16 ms** and a step size of **4 ms**, yielding **41 time frames** per transition. The spectrogram was then Mel-scaled using **80 filters**, producing an input tensor of size **80 × 41** for CNN training ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)).
[0m
[32m- **Hand-crafted feature baseline.** A parallel baseline model used hand-crafted features classified with a support vector machine, providing a non-deep-learning comparison point against the CNN ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)).

[0m
[32mThus, the effective unit of analysis is not the speaker or the recording, but the **segmented transition**, with speaker-level decisions aggregated by majority voting ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)).

[0m
[32m## 5. How the Datasets Were Partitioned and Used Experimentally

[0m
[32mThe experiments proceeded in two stages ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). First, baseline and CNN models were trained **within each language individually**. Second, each trained CNN was used as a **base model** to initialize models for the other two languages, implementing transfer learning among languages ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)).

[32m## 6. Assessment of Dataset Strengths, Gaps, and Limitations

[0m
[32m### 6.1 Strengths

[0m
[32mThe dataset design has three clear strengths. First, **parallelism**: the same clinical endpoint (MDS-UPDRS-III) and the same acquisition standard (noise-controlled, 16 kHz) were applied across all three languages, enabling legitimate cross-lingual comparison ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). Second, **balanced case-control sampling** within each language, with equal or near-equal PD and HC counts in Spanish (50/50) and Czech (50/50) ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). Third, **multi-task speech protocols**, including diadochokinetic syllable repetition, read text, and monologue, which permit analysis of different articulatory and prosodic phenomena ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)).

[0m
[32m### 6.2 Limitations and Open Questions

[0m
[32m- **Composition discrepancy for the German data.** The third-party note reports 44 PD and 44 HC speakers, while the primary demographic table implies 88 PD and 88 HC speakers ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt); [Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). This must be resolved against the original corpus documentation before the German cohort size is cited.
[0m
[32m- **Medication state confounding.** Spanish patients were recorded in the ON state, and the provided material does not state the medication state for German or Czech participants ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). Cross-corpus comparisons of speech markers are therefore confounded by an uncontrolled variable.
[0m
[32m- **Severity imbalance across corpora.** Mean MDS-UPDRS-III scores diverge widely (roughly 38 for Spanish versus 18–23 for German and Czech), so cross-lingual transfer results confound language with disease severity ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)).
[0m
[32m- **Incomplete task harmonisation.** Only the Czech and Spanish corpora are documented as sharing the /pa-ta-ka/ diadochokinetic task; the German task set is not described in the provided material ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). Text-reading materials also differ in length (36 words in Spanish versus 80 words in Czech).
[0m
[32m- **Small samples.** Cohorts of 50–100 speakers per language, with speaker-level decisions aggregated from a modest number of exercises, constrain statistical power and increase variance, as reflected in the large standard deviations reported for some conditions (e.g., CNN sensitivity of 94.0% with 13.5 SD in Czech) ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)).
[0m
[32m- **Undisclosed access conditions.** The provided material contains no information on data licencing, availability, or anonymisation procedures for any of the three corpora.
[0m
[32m- **Corpora cited but not used.** The reference list includes other Parkinsonian speech corpora, such as the Turkish dataset of Sakar et al. and Czech articulation studies by Rusz and colleagues ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). These are cited as related literature; the provided material does not indicate that they contributed data to the present experiments, and they should not be conflated with the three corpora described above.

[0m
[32m## 7. Conclusion

[0m
[32mThe study rests on **three language-specific speech corpora**: the Spanish **PC-GITA** corpus (50 PD, 50 HC Colombian Spanish speakers), a German-language corpus of PD patients and healthy controls, and a Czech-language corpus of 100 speakers (50 PD, 50 HC) ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). All recordings were noise-controlled and down-sampled to 16 kHz, and all patients were clinically rated with MDS-UPDRS-III ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). The datasets were transformed into 160 ms voiced–unvoiced transition segments and represented as 80 × 41 Mel spectrograms for CNN training, with a hand-crafted feature/SVM pipeline serving as a baseline ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). Transfer learning between these corpora improved accuracy by up to 8% and produced more balanced, lower-variance models, but only when the base model was sufficiently robust—an outcome observed for Spanish-initialised models ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt)). The principal constraints on reuse are the unresolved German cohort size, the ON-state recording of Spanish patients, the severity imbalance across languages, and the uneven speech-task protocols across corpora ([Arias-Vergara et al., 2019](document_1.txt); [Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)).
