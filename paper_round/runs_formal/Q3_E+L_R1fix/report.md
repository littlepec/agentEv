# Quantifying the Improvement of an Embedding-Based Semantic Similarity Measure over BM25 for PubMed Query-Document Mapping

## Introduction

The task of mapping PubMed queries to relevant documents is a central challenge in biomedical information retrieval, where lexical matching methods such as BM25 have long served as strong baselines. A recent study titled *Bridging the Gap: Incorporating a Semantic Similarity Measure for Effectively Mapping PubMed Queries to Documents* (arXiv:1608.01972) investigates whether a semantic similarity measure based on embeddings can improve retrieval effectiveness beyond traditional lexical approaches ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). The specific query addressed in this report is: **By how much does their similarity measure (the embedding approach) outperform BM25?** According to the experimental results reported in the TREC Experiments section of the source paper, the embedding approach boosts the average precision of BM25 by **19% on TREC 2006** and **6% on TREC 2007** ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). A third-party research note independently corroborates these exact figures, stating that the proposed semantic-similarity approach raises average precision by 19% on TREC 2006 and by 6% on TREC 2007 relative to BM25 ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). This report provides a detailed, objective analysis of that finding, including the comparative performance of BM25 against TFIDF and CENTROID, the significance of the improvement magnitudes, and the limitations of the available evidence.

## The Core Quantitative Finding

The direct answer to the query is that the embedding approach outperforms BM25 by **19% on TREC 2006** and by **6% on TREC 2007**, measured in terms of average precision ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). The third-party note repeats these figures verbatim, noting that the semantic-similarity approach “raises average precision by 19% on TREC 2006 and by 6% on TREC 2007” ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). The phrasing “boosts the average precision of BM25 by 19% and 6%” implies a relative improvement over the BM25 baseline rather than an absolute percentage-point gain ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). In other words, if BM25 achieved a certain average precision score, the embedding approach increased that score by 19% of the BM25 value in 2006 and by 6% of the BM25 value in 2007. The exact baseline scores are not provided in the excerpt, so the absolute point improvement cannot be computed from the available information ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). Nevertheless, the relative improvement is the standard way to report such gains in information retrieval experiments, and the source explicitly frames the result as an enhancement of BM25 performance ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)).

## Interpretation of the Improvement Magnitudes

A 19% relative improvement in average precision is generally considered substantial in information retrieval, where even small gains can be meaningful ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). The 6% improvement in 2007 is more modest but still positive and practically relevant, especially if the baseline BM25 score is already high ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). The fact that the embedding approach outperforms BM25 in both TREC years suggests that the semantic similarity measure is robust across different evaluation sets, even though the effect size varies ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). The third-party note does not question these magnitudes and presents them as the key finding of the study ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). However, without access to the full table, the absolute average precision values, or statistical significance tests, it is not possible to determine whether the 6% gain in 2007 is statistically significant or whether it might be within the range of random variation ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). The excerpt does not report confidence intervals or p-values, so the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)).

## Why the Embedding Approach Might Help

The title of the source paper, *Bridging the Gap: Incorporating a Semantic Similarity Measure for Effectively Mapping PubMed Queries to Documents*, suggests that the motivation for the embedding approach is to address the gap between the literal terms used in queries and the conceptual content of relevant documents ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). BM25 is a lexical matching function that relies on term overlap and weighting, so it may fail to retrieve documents that are semantically relevant but use different terminology ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). A semantic similarity measure based on embeddings can capture latent semantic relationships, potentially improving retrieval for biomedical queries where synonymy and paraphrasing are common ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). The source does not provide a detailed mechanism in the excerpt, but the reported improvements are consistent with the hypothesis that semantic matching complements lexical matching ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). The third-party note echoes this framing by describing the method as a “semantic-similarity (embedding) approach” that “improves retrieval over BM25” ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). This interpretation is plausible, but it remains an inference from the title and the reported numbers rather than a claim explicitly tested in the excerpt.

## Limitations and Caveats

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the answer to the query. First, the evidence is limited to two TREC years, 2006 and 2007, so the findings may not generalize to other collections, query sets, or domains ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). Second, the excerpt does not provide the absolute average precision scores for BM25 or the embedding approach, which means the practical significance of a 19% or 6% relative gain cannot be fully assessed ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). Third, no statistical significance testing is reported in the available text, so it is unclear whether the improvements are statistically reliable ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). Fourth, the third-party note, while corroborating, is a secondary source and does not offer independent experimental evidence ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Fifth, the excerpt does not describe the embedding method in detail, so the reproducibility of the result cannot be evaluated from the provided information ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). These caveats do not contradict the core answer—the embedding approach outperforms BM25 by 19% in 2006 and 6% in 2007—but they temper the strength of any broader conclusion that might be drawn about semantic similarity measures in general ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)).

## Conclusion

The embedding-based semantic similarity measure outperforms BM25 by **19% on TREC 2006** and **6% on TREC 2007** in terms of average precision ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). This finding is corroborated by an independent third-party research note, which reports the same percentages ([Third-party research note, n.d.](document_2.txt)). Additionally, BM25 itself outperforms TFIDF and CENTROID, while CENTROID scores lower than both BM25 and the embedding approach ([Bridging the Gap, 2016](document_1.txt)). Therefore, among the compared methods, the embedding approach is the most effective for mapping PubMed queries to documents in the reported TREC experiments. The improvement is larger in 2006 than in 2007, indicating that the benefit of semantic similarity may vary by evaluation set. While the results are promising, the limited scope of the available evidence—two years, no absolute scores, and no statistical tests—means that further research is needed to confirm the generalizability of these gains. Nevertheless, the direct answer to the query is clear and well-supported by both sources: the embedding approach improves over BM25 by 19% on TREC 2006 and 6% on TREC 2007.
