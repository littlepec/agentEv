# Quantifying the BLEU Score Difference Between the Insertion-Deletion Transformer and the Insertion-Only Baseline

## Introduction

The Insertion-Deletion Transformer is a transformer-based neural architecture and training method for sequence generation proposed by Ruis, Stern, Proskurnia, and Chan ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). The central empirical claim of the paper is that augmenting an insertion-only generation framework with an explicit deletion phase yields measurable gains in translation quality as measured by BLEU. The model consists of two phases executed iteratively: an insertion phase that parameterizes a distribution of insertions over the current output hypothesis, and a deletion phase that parameterizes a distribution of deletions over that same hypothesis ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). The baseline against which the proposed approach is compared is the Insertion Model (KERMIT), an insertion-only model that extends the Insertion Transformer framework to handle insertions but not deletions ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)).

The question of how large the BLEU difference actually is has a precise but non-singular answer: the paper reports **two separate per-task differences rather than a single aggregate figure**. Specifically, the reported gaps are **21.34 BLEU points** on the shifted alphabetic sequence task and **2.02 BLEU points** on the Caesar's cipher task, the latter described by the authors as "around 2 BLEU points" ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). This report details those numbers, the conditions under which they were obtained, the significance of the differences, and an important discrepancy between the primary source and a secondary summary of it.

## The Proposed Approach and the Baseline

Understanding the magnitude of the BLEU difference requires understanding what is being compared. The Insertion-Deletion Transformer stacks two transformer decoders: a lower insertion model parameterized by θ_i and an upper deletion model parameterized by θ_d ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). The deletion model receives its training signal directly on-policy from the output of the insertion model, meaning it is trained to correct the specific errors the insertion model actually makes rather than errors from an external expert policy ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). The authors emphasize that gradients do not flow backward from the deletion model through the insertion model, and that both models are trained simultaneously by maximizing the log-probability of their respective distributions ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)).

The insertion-only baseline, Insertion Model (KERMIT), follows the insertion-based framework of Stern et al. (2019) and Chan et al. (2019), in which the target sequence is modeled purely with insertion edits generated in an arbitrary order, with left-to-right generation as a special case ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). Because this baseline cannot undo generation errors, the proposed deletion phase is positioned as the mechanism that enables iterative refinement of the output sequence ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). The comparison therefore isolates the contribution of the deletion phase.

## Headline Answer: Reported BLEU Differences

The following table consolidates the reported BLEU scores and the resulting differences, as presented in the primary source ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)).

| Task | Insertion Model (KERMIT) | Insertion-Deletion Model | Difference (BLEU points) | Source Table |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shifted alphabetic sequence | 70.15 | 91.49 | +21.34 | Table 2 |
| Caesar's cipher | 35.55 | 37.57 | +2.02 | Table 3 |

Two derived quantities help contextualize these absolute differences. On the shifted alphabetic sequence task, the 21.34-point gain corresponds to a relative improvement of approximately 30.4% over the baseline, computed as (91.49 − 70.15) / 70.15. On the Caesar's cipher task, the 2.02-point gain corresponds to a relative improvement of approximately 5.7%, computed as (37.57 − 35.55) / 35.55. Both derived figures are arithmetic computations from the reported numbers rather than values stated in the paper.

### The Shifted Alphabetic Sequence Task

The first task is the shifting of alphabetic sequences. Training and evaluation data were generated by sampling a sequence length n from a uniform distribution bounded by min_n = 3 and max_n = 10, then uniformly sampling a starting token and completing the alphabetic sequence until it reached length n; each character was shifted by max_n to ensure no overlap between source and target ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). The authors generated 1,000 training examples and evaluated on 100 held-out examples, training for 200k steps with a batch size of 32 and performing no model selection ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)).

On this task the Insertion-Deletion Transformer reached 91.49 BLEU against 70.15 BLEU for the insertion-only KERMIT baseline, a difference of 21.34 BLEU points ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). The authors characterize the outcome as the Insertion-Deletion Transformer "outperform[ing] the Insertion Transformer significantly on this task" and present Table 2 as the evidence ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). Notably, this is the task on which the insertion-only baseline performs comparatively well in absolute terms (70.15 BLEU), yet the absolute gap is also the largest of the two reported, suggesting that the deletion phase contributes substantial correction capacity even when the insertion model is already reasonably strong.

### The Caesar's Cipher Task

The second task is Caesar's cipher, an encryption method in which each letter in the source sequence is replaced by a letter a fixed number of positions down the alphabet ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). Because the sequences need not be in alphabetic order, the diversity of input sequences is much larger than in the first task. Sequence lengths were sampled with min_n = 3 and max_n = 25, and each letter was shifted by max_n = 25 ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). The dataset was substantially larger: 100k training examples and 1,000 held-out evaluation examples, again with 200k training steps, batch size 32, and no model selection ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)).

On this harder task, the Insertion-Deletion Model scored 37.57 BLEU against 35.55 BLEU for the insertion-only baseline, a difference of 2.02 BLEU points ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). The authors describe this as the deletion model increasing the BLEU score "by around 2 BLEU points" ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). The contrast between the two tasks is instructive: the absolute gain is an order of magnitude smaller on Caesar's cipher (2.02 points) than on the alphabetic shifting task (21.34 points), even though absolute BLEU levels on Caesar's cipher are far lower (mid-30s versus low-90s). This pattern indicates that the benefit of the deletion phase is not simply a function of how much headroom remains, but depends on the structure of the task and the nature of the insertion model's errors.

## Overall Claim Versus Per-Task Reporting

The paper's abstract states that the approach obtains "significant BLEU score improvement over an insertion-only model" on synthetic translation tasks ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)), and the conclusion states that the deletion model "can significantly increase the BLEU score on simple tasks by iteratively refining the output sequence via sequences of insertion-deletions" ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). However, no single pooled or averaged BLEU difference across tasks is reported. The secondary research note confirms this explicitly, stating that "the paper reports separate BLEU results for two synthetic translation tasks rather than one overall BLEU difference," with comparisons being per task at 21.34 BLEU points and 2.02 BLEU points respectively (Third-party research note, n.d.). Any answer to the question of "the" BLEU difference must therefore be given as a pair of task-specific figures rather than one number.

## An Important Discrepancy Between Sources

A material inconsistency exists between the primary paper and the secondary note regarding which task corresponds to which BLEU pair. The primary source places the 70.15 → 91.49 comparison in Table 2, labeled "Alphabetic Sequence Shifting BLEU," and the 35.55 → 37.57 comparison in Table 3, labeled "Caesar's Cipher BLEU" ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). The secondary note reverses the task labels, attributing the 91.49 versus 70.15 pair to Caesar's cipher and "Table 2," and the 37.57 versus 35.55 pair to the shifted alphabetic sequence task and "Table 3" (Third-party research note, n.d.).

Two observations follow. First, the discrepancy concerns task attribution only; both sources agree on the numeric values themselves and on the resulting differences of 21.34 and 2.02 BLEU points (Third-party research note, n.d.; [Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). Second, because the primary source is the peer-visible arXiv paper containing the original tables, it should be treated as the authoritative attribution, and the secondary note's task labels should be regarded as erroneous. This matters for substantive interpretation: the paper's own narrative implies the large 21.34-point gain belongs to the simpler alphabetic shifting task, while the more diverse Caesar's cipher task yields the modest 2.02-point gain ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). Reading the secondary note in isolation would invert that interpretation.

## Why the Deletion Phase Produces These Differences

The authors' explanation for the gain is mechanistic. In a conventional insertion-based model, an error made during generation cannot be undone; introducing the deletion phase makes it possible to undo insertion mistakes because the deletion model is trained on the on-policy errors of the insertion phase ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). The training loop samples a generation step uniformly, samples a partial permutation for the preceding insertions, passes the sequence through the insertion model, inserts the predicted tokens, and then passes the result to the deletion model, whose output is a per-location deletion probability distribution ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)).

Because the deletion model's signal depends on the insertion model's state, it is possible for the deletion model to receive no learning signal — either because the insertion model is too accurate to insert a wrong token, or because it inserts nothing at all ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). To address this, the authors propose an adversarial sampling method in which, with some probability p_adv, ground-truth tokens in the target are masked for the insertion model during training, causing it to select the incorrect token about which it is most confident, thereby guaranteeing the deletion model a signal ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). Critically for the reported numbers, the authors state that "adversarial deletion training did not improve BLEU scores on these synthetic tasks," while noting that the scheme can still be helpful when the deletion model would otherwise receive no signal ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). The reported 21.34-point and 2.02-point gains therefore do not depend on the adversarial component; they arise from the basic insertion-deletion training loop.

## Significance, Interpretation, and Limitations

The comparative significance of the two differences is best assessed separately. A 21.34-point BLEU improvement is large by conventional standards in machine translation evaluation and, in the authors' framing, constitutes significant improvement on the alphabetic shifting task ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). A 2.02-point improvement is modest in absolute terms but is consistent with the authors' own characterization of "around 2 BLEU points" and is reported as a genuine improvement on Caesar's cipher ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)).

Several limitations constrain how far these differences can be generalized. First, both evaluations are on **synthetic, character-based translation tasks**, and the authors present the work explicitly as a proof of concept, stating that future work should verify the model on non-synthetic data for tasks such as machine translation, paraphrasing, and style transfer ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). Second, **no model selection was performed** and both models were trained for 200k steps at batch size 32, meaning the reported figures reflect single fixed training configurations rather than tuned results ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). Third, the evaluation sets are small — 100 held-out examples for the alphabetic task and 1,000 for Caesar's cipher — which limits the precision of the reported BLEU values ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). Fourth, the paper positions its approach as a simpler alternative to the concurrent Levenshtein Transformer, arguing that the LevT's expert policy requires dynamic programming to minimize Levenshtein distance and that its extra classifier for predicting insertion counts adds an additional transformer pass per generation step, while the proposed framework has only one insertion phase and one deletion phase ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). That architectural simplicity claim is independent of the BLEU figures and would need separate benchmarking against LevT to substantiate.

## Conclusion

Based on the available sources, the BLEU score difference between the proposed Insertion-Deletion Transformer and the insertion-only Insertion Model (KERMIT) is **not a single number**. It is reported as two task-specific differences: **21.34 BLEU points** in favor of the Insertion-Deletion Model on the shifted alphabetic sequence task (91.49 versus 70.15, Table 2) and **2.02 BLEU points** in favor of the Insertion-Deletion Model on the Caesar's cipher task (37.57 versus 35.55, Table 3), the latter described by the authors as "around 2 BLEU points" ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). A secondary research note reports the same two numeric differences but swaps the task labels, an attribution error that should be resolved in favor of the primary arXiv source (Third-party research note, n.d.; [Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). Both gains stem from the on-policy deletion phase, not from the adversarial sampling variant, which the authors found did not improve BLEU on these synthetic tasks ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)). The magnitudes are substantial on the simpler task and modest on the harder one, and both remain bounded by the synthetic, proof-of-concept nature of the experiments and the absence of model selection ([Ruis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.05540)).
